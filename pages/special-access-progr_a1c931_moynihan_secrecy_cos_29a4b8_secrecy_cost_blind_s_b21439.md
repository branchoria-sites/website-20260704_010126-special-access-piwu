## Introduction

Official estimates of the cost of government secrecy are real, useful, and increasingly detailed—but they do not capture the full economic or institutional price of secrecy. Annual cost reports measure tangible items such as secure facilities, personnel security investigations, classified information technology, training, and compliance. What they struggle to measure are the indirect effects: slower decision-making, duplicated research, fragmented oversight, reduced technical scrutiny, and poorer policy judgement. Those hidden costs become especially significant in special access programmes (SAPs), including waived and [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}), where additional [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) restrict the flow of information beyond ordinary classification.

The distinction matters because the most expensive consequences of secrecy may not appear as line items in any budget. Daniel Patrick Moynihan's Commission argued that secrecy should be evaluated not only by what it protects but also by what it prevents government from doing efficiently and intelligently. The result is a persistent [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) blind spot: official figures describe the cost of operating the secrecy system, while many of secrecy's broader consequences remain largely unpriced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">personnel security practices...</span></span></span>

## What official secrecy cost surveys count

The Information Security Oversight Office (ISOO) publishes annual estimates of the federal government's security classification costs under the framework established by executive orders governing classified information. These reports include expenditures that agencies can reasonably estimate, such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Personnel security investigations and clearance processing.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">personnel security practices...</span></span></span>
* Physical security, including secure facilities and access controls.
* Classified information systems and communications.
* Security management, oversight and planning.
* Classification training and awareness programmes.
* Industrial security costs reported by contractors.

</div>

The reports provide valuable trend data and demonstrate that maintaining a classification system requires substantial continuing investment. They also acknowledge an important limitation: agencies provide estimates rather than precise accounting, and many reported expenditures would continue because they also support broader security requirements rather than classification alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/2010-cost-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives2010 COST REPORTMay 3, 2011 — 29 Apr 2011 — This report provides information on the cost estimates of the security class...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 2011</span></span></span>

This means official cost surveys are designed primarily to answer a narrow administrative question: how much does it cost to operate the machinery of classified information? They are not designed to estimate whether secrecy causes programmes to take longer, cost more, or produce poorer decisions.

## Why intangible costs are harder to price

The hidden costs identified by the Moynihan Commission are difficult to convert into conventional budget figures because they emerge through organisational behaviour rather than direct expenditure.

One example is delay. Every additional access restriction narrows the number of people able to review technical work, approve procurement decisions or identify mistakes. The resulting schedule extensions may appear in programme [budgets]({{ 'budgets/' | relative_url }}), but they are rarely recorded as costs caused by compartmentation itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">personnel security practices...</span></span></span>

Duplication presents a similar problem. Separate compartments may fund parallel analytical efforts because participants are unaware that comparable work already exists elsewhere. The duplicated salaries, contracts or research projects are recorded in individual programme budgets, not in secrecy accounts, making the inefficiency effectively invisible as a classification expense. The Moynihan Commission highlighted contractor concerns that differing security practices across special access programmes created unnecessary redundancy and compliance burdens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ures to standardize security practices in such programs...</span></span></span>

Judgement is harder still to value. Restricting access inevitably reduces opportunities for independent technical review, challenge and error correction. The financial consequences may emerge years later through design flaws, procurement mistakes or policy failures, but there is rarely a credible method for assigning a monetary value specifically to excessive secrecy.

These effects resemble what economists describe as opportunity costs: resources are consumed not only by what government spends directly, but by what it cannot accomplish because information moves more slowly or less effectively.

## How SAP compartments magnify accounting blind spots

Special access programmes exist because some information requires safeguards beyond ordinary classification. Executive Order 13526 states that SAPs should be established only where exceptional vulnerability or threat makes normal classification insufficient, and that they should be kept to an absolute minimum.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security InformationDecember 29, 2009 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

Those additional controls, however, create accounting challenges that extend beyond ordinary classified programmes.

First, each compartment introduces its own administrative structure. Separate access approvals, secure workspaces, information systems, record-keeping requirements and compliance procedures increase operating complexity. Many of these costs appear in ordinary administrative budgets rather than as identifiable "SAP costs".

Secondly, compartmentation can reduce economies of scale. Security procedures, software, infrastructure or contractor compliance mechanisms that might otherwise be shared across programmes may instead be replicated to preserve compartment boundaries.

Thirdly, oversight itself becomes more resource-intensive. Officials responsible for reviewing [multiple SAPs]({{ 'multiple-saps/' | relative_url }}) often require multiple read-ins, specialised staff and separate reporting channels. For waived SAPs, statutory oversight continues, but reporting pathways are narrower than for ordinary programmes, increasing the difficulty of evaluating programme-wide efficiency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In 1994, the DoD created the Special Access Program Oversight Committee.Read more...</span></span></span>

These are not arguments against SAPs as a category. Rather, they illustrate why the administrative cost of stronger protection is easier to measure than the wider organisational effects of increasingly segmented information.

## Oversight bodies have recognised the measurement gap

The Moynihan Commission did not argue that secrecy costs were unknowable. Instead, it argued that existing measurement focused too narrowly on direct expenditures.

The Commission noted improvements in security cost reporting but also observed that reviews of special access programmes frequently concentrated on programme cost, schedule and performance without giving equivalent attention to whether the additional security measures themselves remained justified or economical. It recommended greater standardisation of SAP security practices specifically to reduce unnecessary redundancy and associated costs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In 1994, the DoD created the Special Access Program Oversight Committee.Read more...</span></span></span>

Modern ISOO reports continue to provide increasingly comprehensive estimates of classification-related spending, yet they remain explicit that the methodology is intended to indicate trends rather than produce exact accounting. By design, these reports measure the operation of the classification system, not its broader effects on governmental effectiveness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/2010-cost-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives2010 COST REPORTMay 3, 2011 — 29 Apr 2011 — This report provides information on the cost estimates of the security class...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 3, 2011</span></span></span>

Independent analysts have argued that overclassification imposes additional costs beyond those reflected in official figures, including increased administrative workload, reduced transparency and diminished accountability. ## The hidden bill for secrecy

The central lesson of the Moynihan critique is that secrecy has two balance sheets.

The visible balance sheet contains measurable expenditures: guards, secure buildings, classified networks, clearance investigations and compliance systems. These costs are substantial and are reported with increasing sophistication.

The hidden balance sheet contains costs that spread across organisations rather than appearing in security budgets. They include delayed collaboration, duplicated effort, weaker external review, fragmented institutional memory, reduced congressional visibility and lower public confidence when excessive secrecy later becomes apparent. These consequences rarely have identifiable budget codes, yet they can shape programme performance as much as direct security spending.

For special access programmes, including waived and unacknowledged SAPs, this distinction is especially important. The more restrictive the compartmentation, the greater the need to demonstrate that the additional protection produces security benefits that justify not only the measurable administrative expense but also the less visible costs that conventional secrecy accounting leaves largely unmeasured.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">personnel security practices...</span></span></span>
