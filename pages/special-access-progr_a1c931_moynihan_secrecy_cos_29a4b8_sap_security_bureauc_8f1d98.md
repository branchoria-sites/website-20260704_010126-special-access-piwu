## Introduction

Special Access Programmes (SAPs) exist because some national security information is considered too sensitive for the normal classification system alone. They add additional controls beyond ordinary security clearances, such as programme-specific approvals, compartmented access, secure facilities and stricter handling rules. Executive Order 13526 requires that such programmes be kept to an “absolute minimum” and used only when ordinary classification is insufficient to protect exceptionally vulnerable information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

The Moynihan Commission's critique was not that these additional protections are inherently unjustified. Rather, it argued that every additional security layer creates an administrative system with its own paperwork, personnel, infrastructure and oversight demands. Over time, those supporting mechanisms can become a "second bureaucracy": an organisation devoted not to producing military capability or [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}), but to administering access to it. That administrative burden carries financial costs, slows collaboration and can make the true price of secrecy difficult to measure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moynihan_Commission_on_Government_Secrecy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## When protection becomes its own bureaucracy

Ordinary classified information already requires background investigations, secure storage, training and controlled dissemination. SAPs introduce another level of administration on top of those requirements.

Instead of simply determining whether someone holds an appropriate clearance, programme managers must determine whether that individual has been formally "read into" a particular programme. Every addition or removal requires documentation, approvals and records that are separate from ordinary personnel security processes. These controls are designed to reduce unnecessary exposure, but they also multiply administrative work.

This is the mechanism behind the Moynihan critique. Secrecy does not merely restrict information; it regulates organisational behaviour. Each additional compartment requires people whose primary task is administering the compartment itself rather than performing the programme's technical mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moynihan_Commission_on_Government_Secrecy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## Access rosters, forms and duplicate vetting

One of the most visible manifestations of this second bureaucracy is the maintenance of programme-specific access systems.

Although an individual may already possess a [Top Secret clearance]({{ 'clearance-gap/' | relative_url }}), SAP participation often requires additional steps such as:

* programme nomination and approval;
* documented need-to-know determinations;
* programme indoctrination and acknowledgement forms;
* separate access rosters maintained by programme security officers;
* periodic reviews confirming continued eligibility;
* formal [debriefing]({{ 'debriefing/' | relative_url }}) when access ends.

Each SAP therefore develops administrative records that exist alongside—not instead of—the government's ordinary clearance databases.

Because individuals may participate in multiple [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) simultaneously, separate programme offices frequently maintain overlapping records concerning the same employee. Even where modern information systems reduce [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}), programme-specific access decisions remain decentralised because the central purpose of SAPs is to limit knowledge of programme membership.

The result is that security management becomes a substantial operational function in its own right rather than simply an attribute of personnel management. Executive Order 13526 also requires annual review of each SAP by the responsible agency head, adding another recurring administrative process intended to ensure programmes continue to satisfy the high threshold for special access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Separate facilities and contractor compliance burdens

The administrative burden extends beyond paperwork into physical infrastructure.

Many SAP activities occur within specially controlled facilities whose construction, accreditation and maintenance require dedicated security staff, inspection processes and compliance documentation. Contractors working on SAPs must often satisfy additional facility-security requirements beyond those applying to conventional classified contracts.

These requirements may include:

* accredited secure work areas;
* specialised information systems approved for compartmented work;
* controlled visitor procedures;
* programme-specific document [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }});
* additional security training;
* recurring compliance inspections.

For large defence contractors participating in multiple SAPs, these requirements can produce parallel security organisations dedicated to separate programmes. Engineering teams may share a corporate employer yet work under entirely different administrative regimes because each programme maintains distinct access controls and reporting procedures.

None of these measures is inherently unnecessary. They are deliberate safeguards against compromise. The bureaucratic cost arises because every compartment requires continuing administration regardless of whether programme activity is expanding or declining.

## How inconsistent rules hide marginal costs

A further consequence is that many costs become difficult to identify.

The expense of a secure building may appear under facilities management. Personnel devoted to maintaining access rosters may belong to programme security. Clearance processing may fall under another office, while contractor compliance costs are absorbed by private firms and ultimately reflected in contract prices.

Viewed individually, each requirement appears modest.

Collectively, however, they create an administrative ecosystem whose total cost is rarely visible in a single budget line. This fragmentation was one of the concerns highlighted by the Moynihan Commission, which argued that secrecy-related costs are often dispersed across agencies and organisations, making meaningful evaluation difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moynihan_Commission_on_Government_Secrecy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

The problem is not merely [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}). When costs are distributed across numerous offices, individual programme managers experience only a fraction of the administrative burden generated by additional compartmentation. Decisions that appear inexpensive locally may impose substantial cumulative costs across the wider national security system.

## Why duplication persists

Many forms of duplication survive because they serve genuine security objectives.

Separate programme rosters reduce the number of people who know who participates in particularly sensitive activities. Independent approval processes ensure that access decisions remain under programme control rather than becoming automatic consequences of holding a high-level clearance.

From a security perspective, these are intentional features rather than administrative mistakes.

The tension identified by critics is that every additional control also requires another supporting process. Once established, these supporting organisations tend to persist because eliminating them may itself appear risky. Security offices therefore develop institutional continuity independent of the operational programme they support.

This dynamic helps explain why Executive Order 13526 directs agencies to review SAPs annually and states that such programmes should exist only where ordinary classification cannot adequately protect the information. The review requirement is intended to counter organisational inertia by forcing agencies to reconsider whether extraordinary controls remain justified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## The mechanism behind the Moynihan critique

The idea of SAP security rules becoming a second bureaucracy does not imply that special access programmes lack value. Rather, it illustrates how protective measures generate administrative structures with lives of their own.

Access rosters require administrators. Separate facilities require inspectors and accreditation. Contractor compliance requires dedicated security organisations. Duplicate vetting requires additional reviews and record keeping. Individually these mechanisms strengthen protection; collectively they create a parallel administrative system that exists alongside ordinary government management.

This is precisely the type of hidden regulatory effect emphasised by the Moynihan Commission. Secrecy is not simply the absence of public information. It is an organisational system that consumes resources, shapes behaviour and imposes continuing administrative obligations. The central policy question is therefore not whether SAP security is necessary, but whether each additional layer produces security benefits that justify the bureaucratic system required to sustain it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moynihan_Commission_on_Government_Secrecy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moynihan Commission on Government Secrecy</span></span></span>
