## Introduction

A Department of Defense special access programme (SAP) does not become publicly acknowledged simply because officials decide to reveal it. Under 10 U.S.C. §119, planned changes to a SAP's classification—or any planned public release of previously classified SAP information—trigger a distinct congressional notification requirement before disclosure occurs. This requirement recognises that changing a programme's classification can itself be a major national-security decision, even if the underlying programme continues unchanged. Rather than allowing public announcements to come without warning, the [statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}) requires [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) to receive advance notice describing what is changing, why it is changing, and whether a public announcement is planned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

Within the broader framework of annual SAP reporting, these classification-change notices serve a different purpose. Annual reports tell Congress what protected programmes exist and how they are managed. Classification-change notices alert Congress that the government's public posture toward a programme is about to change, allowing oversight before information enters the public domain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

## Before a Secret Programme Becomes Public

Section 119(c) covers two closely related situations:

* a planned change in the classification of a Department of Defense SAP; or
* a decision to declassify classified SAP information and release it publicly.

In either case, the Secretary of Defense must submit a report to the congressional defence committees before the change takes effect. The statute requires the report to contain:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a description of the proposed classification change;
* the reasons for making that change; and
* notice of any planned public announcement accompanying it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

</div>

Importantly, the law does not require Congress merely to be informed that disclosure has already happened. Instead, it establishes advance notification as part of the oversight process, recognising that decisions to acknowledge or partially declassify highly protected programmes often involve strategic, operational and diplomatic consequences.

## The 14-day notice rule

The default rule is straightforward: the required report must reach the congressional defence committees at least 14 days before the proposed classification change or public announcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

That waiting period serves several oversight purposes.

First, it prevents Congress from learning about a significant SAP disclosure from the news media or an official press conference. The committees responsible for defence oversight receive advance warning and an explanation before the Executive Branch changes the programme's public status.

Secondly, it allows members and cleared staff to evaluate whether the proposed disclosure appears consistent with national-security interests. The statute does not give Congress a formal veto over declassification decisions, but advance notice creates an opportunity for oversight discussions before irreversible public disclosure occurs.

Finally, the requirement recognises that disclosure decisions may involve more than simply removing a security marking from documents. Public acknowledgement can alter foreign [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) collection priorities, reveal previously hidden budget lines, affect industrial partners, or confirm capabilities that competitors had only suspected.

## Exceptional circumstances and late notice

Congress recognised that not every disclosure decision can accommodate a full two-week notification period.

Accordingly, Section 119 permits an exception where the Secretary of Defense determines that "exceptional circumstances" make the normal [14-day notice]({{ '14-day-notice/' | relative_url }}) impossible. In those cases, the report may be submitted at any time before the classification change or public announcement occurs. However, the report must also explain why the ordinary notification period could not be met.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

The statute deliberately sets a high threshold. It does not create a routine administrative shortcut or a blanket emergency exemption. Instead, it requires an affirmative determination by the Secretary and an accompanying explanation to Congress.

The law does not define "exceptional circumstances," leaving room for judgement in genuinely unusual situations. Plausible examples might include rapidly developing military [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}), urgent diplomatic agreements, or circumstances where delaying disclosure would itself create significant national-security risks. Because these reports remain classified, public information about how frequently this exception has been used is extremely limited.

## Why acknowledgement can be a major classification event

Outside government, declassification is often imagined as a simple decision to make information public. Within the SAP system, acknowledgement may itself represent one of the programme's most consequential [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }}).

A programme may move through several different states over its lifetime:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* remaining entirely unacknowledged;
* becoming publicly acknowledged while retaining classified technical details;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_access_program" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special access program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programThe existence of an acknowledged SAP may be publicly disclosed, but the details of the program remain classified...</span></span></span>
* selectively declassifying specific capabilities or historical information; or
* eventually losing SAP status altogether.

</div>

Each transition changes what may legally be discussed, what contractors may acknowledge, what appears in public budget documents, and what adversaries can confirm from official statements. The programme itself may continue with identical missions and technologies even though its public classification posture has changed.

For that reason, Congress requires advance notification not only when an entire programme is declassified, but whenever a planned classification change could alter the government's official treatment of the programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

Current Department of Defense SAP security guidance reflects this sensitivity. Public release of SAP information requires written approval by the Secretary or Deputy Secretary of Defense together with congressional notification under 10 U.S.C. §119 before authorised release, underscoring that public acknowledgement is treated as a controlled governance decision rather than a routine communications matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS ESDDoDM 5205.07, &quot;Special Access Program Security Manual...17 Jan 2025 — Public release of SAP information is not authorized withou...</span></span></span>

## What these notices reveal—and what they do not

The notification requirement creates transparency for Congress without requiring broad public disclosure.

The report must explain the proposed change and the reasons for it, but Section 119 does not require that these reports themselves be made public. As a result, congressional committees may receive significantly more context than appears in any subsequent public announcement.

Equally important, the statute allows the Secretary of Defense to waive inclusion of particular information in reports under Section 119 if providing that information would adversely affect national security. Such waivers must be made individually rather than categorically, and the omitted information must instead be provided to the chair and ranking member of each congressional defence committee together with the justification for the waiver.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">to the defense committees a report on special access programs...</span></span></span>

This structure reflects the central balance running throughout Section 119: preserving legislative oversight even when the public remains unaware of the full scope, rationale or continuing classified elements of a special access programme.
