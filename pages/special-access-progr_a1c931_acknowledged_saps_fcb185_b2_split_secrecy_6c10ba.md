## Introduction

The B-2 Spirit is one of the clearest examples of how an acknowledged Special Access Program (SAP) can become publicly visible without becoming fully transparent. Once the United States government acknowledged the aircraft and its development programme, [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}), auditors and the public gained access to extensive information about its history, procurement costs, production schedule and programme management. Yet the aircraft's most sensitive capabilities remained compartmented. This deliberate division between what could be discussed openly and what continued to require special protection illustrates a form of "split secrecy": the programme itself becomes part of the public record, while the information that gives it military value remains classified.

The B-2 therefore demonstrates that acknowledgement is not an all-or-nothing event. Instead, governments can selectively disclose information that supports democratic oversight while continuing to protect technical and operational secrets.

## What Became Public in the B-2 Record

One of the most significant changes occurred in 1990 when the U.S. Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office (GAO) published its first unclassified report on the B-2 programme. The report explained that recent changes in the programme's security classification made it possible to release information that had previously appeared only in classified reviews. The resulting publication discussed the programme's history, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) strategy, costs, schedule, industrial base and testing progress in considerable detail. At the same time, the GAO explicitly stated that performance information would remain in a separate classified report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current IssuesFebruary 22, 1990 — Recent changes in the security classification of the program permit t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 1990</span></span></span>

This created an unusually rich public record. Information that became openly available included:

* programme origins and acquisition history;
* estimated procurement and development costs;
* production quantities and planned force structure;
* schedule [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }}) and testing timelines;
* contractor responsibilities and programme management;
* congressional funding debates and budget oversight.

Subsequent GAO reports continued this pattern. Public reports evaluated cost growth, testing progress, production risks and programme execution, allowing Congress and taxpayers to scrutinise whether the programme was being managed effectively even though they could not see every classified detail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-95-164/html/GAOREPORTS-NSIAD-95-164.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GAOREPORTS NSIAD</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">B-2 Bomber: Status of Cost, Development, and ProductionB-2 Bomber: Status of Cost, Development, and Production (Letter Report, 08/...</span></span></span>

The result was an aircraft that was no longer a secret in any ordinary sense. Its existence, purpose and procurement history were extensively documented in public hearings, budget documents and audit reports.

## What GAO Still Treated as Classified

Despite this greater openness, the same reports drew a firm boundary around information that could reveal the aircraft's military advantage.

The GAO's 1990 report makes the distinction unusually explicit. After noting that changes in classification allowed publication of programme history and cost information, it immediately adds that "information on performance remains classified" and would instead appear in a separate report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-90-120.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BOMBERS B-2 Program Status and Current IssuesFebruary 22, 1990 — Recent changes in the security classification of the program permit t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 1990</span></span></span>

The protected information broadly covered matters such as:

* detailed low-observable ([stealth]({{ 'stealth/' | relative_url }})) performance;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* radar signature characteristics;
* survivability against advanced air defences;
* operational effectiveness;
* mission capability assessments;
* vulnerabilities and limitations discovered during testing.

</div>

These are precisely the categories that could enable an adversary to understand how to detect, track or defeat the aircraft.

The distinction continued well after the B-2 entered operational service. For example, unclassified Selected Acquisition Reports for later B-2 modernisation programmes openly discussed funding, procurement quantities and programme status while stating that performance information was provided only in a classified annex. The reporting format itself demonstrates that acquisition transparency and capability secrecy were intentionally separated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Selected_Acquisition_Reports/FY_2010_SARS/B-2_RMP-SAR-25_DEC_2010.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ESDSelected Acquisition Report (SAR) B-2 RMPB-2 RMP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 31, 2010 SAR. ## Why Split Secrecy Matters for Oversight

The B-2 illustrates why governments sometimes separate financial transparency from operational secrecy instead of choosing complete openness or complete concealment.

Congress cannot meaningfully oversee an expensive defence programme without understanding its costs, schedules and management performance. Public audit reports also allow outside analysts, journalists and taxpayers to evaluate whether procurement decisions are reasonable and whether programmes remain on budget.

At the same time, publishing the aircraft's actual stealth effectiveness, sensor performance or penetration capability would reduce the military advantage that justified the programme's enormous investment in the first place.

Split secrecy therefore serves two objectives simultaneously:

* **Democratic accountability.** Legislators can debate procurement, funding and management using publicly available information.
* **Military protection.** The technological and operational characteristics that provide battlefield advantage remain compartmented.

The B-2 demonstrates that these objectives are not necessarily incompatible. Transparency can exist at the level of programme governance while secrecy remains concentrated around capability.

## The Limits of Public Knowledge

The B-2's public history also illustrates an important limitation of open-source analysis. Because large portions of programme administration became public, it is tempting to assume that analysts can accurately judge the aircraft's real military performance.

In practice, that assumption is unsafe.

Official fact sheets acknowledge broad capabilities such as long-range penetration and low observability, but they do not disclose the measurable characteristics that determine how the aircraft performs against specific threats. Likewise, public GAO reports discuss testing milestones without revealing the classified test results themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104482/b-2-spirit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[U.S. Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Air ForceB-2 Spirit &gt; Air Force &gt; Fact Sheet DisplayThe B-2 Spirit is a multi-role bomber capable of delivering both conventional an...</span></span></span>

This creates an information environment in which observers know a great deal about how the programme was funded and managed, yet comparatively little about the exact technical performance that determines its operational value.

For [acknowledged SAPs]({{ 'acknowledged/' | relative_url }}), that distinction is intentional rather than accidental.

## What the B-2 Case Demonstrates About Acknowledged SAPs

Within the broader framework of acknowledged Special Access Programs, the B-2 is a particularly useful case because it shows that acknowledgement is not equivalent to declassification.

Instead, the programme demonstrates a layered disclosure model:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the existence of the programme became public;
* its strategic purpose became public;
* substantial cost and schedule information became public;
* congressional oversight became increasingly visible;
* the performance characteristics that justified the aircraft's strategic value remained classified.

</div>

The enduring separation between administrative transparency and technical secrecy explains why the B-2 is frequently cited as a model of split secrecy. It shows that governments can expose enough information for meaningful oversight without disclosing the engineering, testing and operational data that would compromise a uniquely sensitive military capability.
