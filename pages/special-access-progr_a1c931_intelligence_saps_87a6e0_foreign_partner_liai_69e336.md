## Introduction

Within [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) special access programmes (SAPs) and other highly compartmented systems, one of the most sensitive secrets is often not the intelligence itself but the identity of the foreign service that helped obtain it. Intelligence liaison relationships depend on trust that cooperation, access arrangements and operational roles will remain confidential. If a disclosure reveals that a particular ally supplied information, granted access, hosted collection equipment or participated in a covert activity, the resulting damage may extend well beyond the immediate operation. It can expose the partner to political, legal or security risks, weaken future intelligence sharing and make other governments more reluctant to cooperate in similarly sensitive programmes. These risks explain why liaison information is frequently protected within the most restrictive intelligence [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) and why even seemingly minor disclosures can have strategic consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190515_R45720_a76e5d9b7d41c6aeff4889236aacc9df0f6de4ef.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportUnited States Foreign Intelligence Relationships15 May 2019 — From its inception, the United States Intelligence Communit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

## Why partner identity can be sensitive

Foreign intelligence cooperation is built on reciprocal confidence rather than simply on formal treaties. Intelligence services exchange information because each side believes that the other can protect both the information and the relationship itself. The identity of the provider may therefore be as sensitive as the intelligence provided.

This sensitivity arises for several reasons:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A government may publicly deny cooperation with another state while quietly maintaining an intelligence relationship.
* Disclosure can expose officials or intelligence officers inside the partner country to political criticism or legal investigation.
* Adversaries may infer where intelligence collection occurred, narrowing their search for compromised systems or personnel.
* Other potential partners may conclude that confidential cooperation cannot be kept secret and become more cautious about future exchanges.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190515_R45720_a76e5d9b7d41c6aeff4889236aacc9df0f6de4ef.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportUnited States Foreign Intelligence Relationships15 May 2019 — From its inception, the United States Intelligence Communit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

</div>

Congressional Research Service analysis of US foreign intelligence relationships notes that liaison partnerships often involve far more than exchanging finished reports. They may include access to otherwise inaccessible locations, basing rights for collection systems, joint [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}), technical cooperation, training and burden-sharing arrangements. Revealing one element can therefore expose a much broader intelligence architecture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190515_R45720_a76e5d9b7d41c6aeff4889236aacc9df0f6de4ef.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportUnited States Foreign Intelligence Relationships15 May 2019 — From its inception, the United States Intelligence Communit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

## How disclosures strain alliances

### Trust is harder to rebuild than technical capability

Technical systems can sometimes be replaced. Trust between intelligence organisations is considerably harder to restore once damaged.

Many intelligence-sharing relationships operate under long-established understandings that information supplied by one service will not be redistributed without permission. This principle—often called the "third-party rule" in intelligence liaison practice—allows partners to exchange highly sensitive reporting while retaining control over further dissemination. If that understanding is breached, the supplying service may reduce future cooperation, insist on tighter handling restrictions or withhold particularly sensitive intelligence altogether.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.yale.edu/mfia/case-disclosed/newly-disclosed-documents-five-eyes-alliance-and-what-they-tell-us-about-intelligence-sharing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.yale.edu">[Yale Law School]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yale Law SchoolNewly Disclosed Documents on the Five Eyes Alliance and...25 Apr 2018 — The Five Eyes alliance facilitates the sharing of...</span></span></span>

The UK Parliament's Intelligence and Security Committee has described Five Eyes cooperation as resting on unusually high levels of trust, including a strong presumption that signals intelligence will normally be shared among partners. Such integration depends on confidence that shared material will continue to receive equivalent protection across all participating governments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://isc.independent.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ISC-International-Partnerships.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: isc.independent.gov.uk">[isc.independent.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">isc.independent.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Partnerships5 Dec 2023 — Engagement with Five Eyes partners includes formal intelligence sharing, joint operational working...</span></span></span>

### Political consequences for partner governments

A leak may also create domestic political difficulties for the partner itself.

Some governments cooperate extensively with allies against terrorism, espionage or [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) threats while simultaneously managing public opinion, parliamentary oversight or regional diplomatic relationships. If classified cooperation becomes public unexpectedly, leaders may face accusations of surrendering sovereignty, conducting secret surveillance or assisting controversial military operations.

In some cases, the political cost of exposure exceeds the operational value of continuing the relationship, encouraging governments to narrow future cooperation even where shared security interests remain.

## Concrete examples of liaison damage

Historical examples illustrate that liaison damage is not merely theoretical.

The intelligence disclosures made by Edward Snowden in 2013 revealed extensive details about international intelligence-sharing arrangements, including aspects of Five Eyes cooperation. Although the alliance remained intact, the disclosures prompted political controversy, increased scrutiny by oversight bodies and debates over intelligence-sharing rules in several member countries. The episode demonstrated that exposing partnership mechanisms can generate diplomatic friction even among exceptionally close allies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://law.yale.edu/mfia/case-disclosed/newly-disclosed-documents-five-eyes-alliance-and-what-they-tell-us-about-intelligence-sharing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.yale.edu">[yale.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.yale.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yale Law SchoolNewly Disclosed Documents on the Five Eyes Alliance and...25 Apr 2018 — The Five Eyes alliance facilitates the sharing of...</span></span></span>

Another widely reported example occurred in 2017, when US media disclosures concerning the investigation of the Manchester Arena bombing were criticised by UK authorities because sensitive British investigative information had entered the public domain before UK investigators had completed parts of their work. The incident prompted temporary restrictions on intelligence sharing while assurances regarding handling procedures were sought. Although cooperation quickly resumed, the episode illustrated how confidence can be affected even between longstanding partners.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com/s3fs-public/2025-01/250128_Byman_Intelligence_Sharing.pdf?VersionId=dTE404K_zq877C6RONG14Y5b_Ge3JHBf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com">[csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csis-website-prod.s3.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Improving U.S</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Sharing with Allies and PartnersJanuary 28, 2025 — 2 Jan 2025 — • The Five Eyes share exchange liaison officers, do joint op...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 28, 2025</span></span></span>

## How sharing rules limit wider access

Because liaison relationships depend upon continued confidence, intelligence systems generally distinguish between possessing information and being authorised to redistribute it.

Typical restrictions include:

* **Originator control.** The originating service determines whether intelligence may be passed beyond the initial recipients.
* **Need-to-know access.** Personnel may require both the appropriate clearance and explicit operational need before receiving partner-originated reporting.
* **Separate dissemination controls.** Reports containing foreign-partner information may carry additional handling caveats beyond normal classification markings.
* **Compartmentation.** Particularly sensitive liaison arrangements may be protected inside controlled-access programmes or equivalent compartments so that even cleared personnel cannot infer the identity of the contributing partner without authorisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190515_R45720_a76e5d9b7d41c6aeff4889236aacc9df0f6de4ef.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportUnited States Foreign Intelligence Relationships15 May 2019 — From its inception, the United States Intelligence Communit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

These controls exist because the practical question is often not whether a document is classified, but whether broader circulation would inadvertently reveal the existence, identity or role of the foreign partner.

## Why liaison protection supports intelligence SAPs

Within intelligence SAPs and other highly restricted compartments, protecting foreign liaison relationships serves several overlapping objectives.

First, it preserves access to intelligence that cannot be collected unilaterally. Partners may possess geographic access, language expertise, technical capabilities or human sources unavailable to another service. Second, it protects ongoing joint operations whose success depends upon neither side being publicly associated with the activity. Third, it reassures current and future partners that exceptionally sensitive cooperation will remain confidential even inside large intelligence bureaucracies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190515_R45720_a76e5d9b7d41c6aeff4889236aacc9df0f6de4ef.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[everycrsreport.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportUnited States Foreign Intelligence Relationships15 May 2019 — From its inception, the United States Intelligence Communit...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2019</span></span></span>

For these reasons, intelligence professionals often treat the identity of a cooperating foreign service as a protected source in its own right. Revealing which ally provided access or intelligence can enable adversaries to reconstruct collection methods, pressure the partner politically, identify vulnerable operational networks and reduce the willingness of other governments to participate in future high-risk cooperation. In this way, liaison protection is not simply diplomatic courtesy; it is a core mechanism for sustaining the international relationships on which many of the most sensitive intelligence programmes depend.
