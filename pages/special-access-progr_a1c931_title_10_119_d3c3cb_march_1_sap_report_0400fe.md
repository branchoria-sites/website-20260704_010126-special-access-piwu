## Introduction

The annual report on Department of Defense special access programmes (SAPs), due to [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) by 1 March under 10 U.S.C. §119, is designed around money rather than technical disclosure. That is a deliberate feature of the oversight system. Congress does not need the engineering details of a classified capability to judge whether a programme is growing, slipping, changing direction, or consuming resources as planned. Instead, the statute requires budget figures, cost history, projected spending and major [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }}), giving congressional defence committees practical tools to oversee even highly classified, unacknowledged or waived SAPs while protecting operational secrets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense committees...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2025</span></span></span>

This financial focus reflects a basic constitutional principle. Congress authorises and appropriates defence spending, so effective oversight depends on understanding how taxpayer funds are committed and whether programmes remain justified, not on exposing sensitive technologies. The March 1 report therefore follows the money because [budgets]({{ 'budgets/' | relative_url }}), schedules and programme maturity are the most useful oversight indicators that can usually be shared within classified congressional channels without revealing the capabilities the SAP exists to protect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. ## What the March 1 report must include

The annual report is not a narrative history of every SAP. Instead, 10 U.S.C. §119 specifies a consistent management dataset for each programme appearing in the President's defence budget. The Secretary of Defense must report:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A brief description of the programme.
* Its major milestones.
* Actual costs for each completed fiscal year.
* The estimated total programme cost.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[uscode.house.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A) the current estimate of the total program cost for the program; and. (B) an...Read more...</span></span></span>
* Funding estimates for the current fiscal year, the upcoming budget year and the following four fiscal years.
* The aggregate amount requested for all Department of Defense SAPs in the President's budget.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. This structure mirrors the information Congress routinely uses to oversee conventional [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) programmes. Rather than creating a separate oversight system for secret programmes, the law adapts familiar financial controls to a classified environment.

The inclusion of multiple years of historical and projected costs is particularly important. A single annual appropriation reveals little, but a multi-year funding profile allows congressional staff to identify accelerating costs, changing priorities, delayed procurement or unexpected programme expansion. Those trends often reveal management issues long before technical details become relevant to oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. ## How costs and milestones create oversight leverage

Financial reporting gives Congress several practical ways to evaluate a SAP without requiring access to every operational detail.

First, actual spending can be compared with earlier projections. If a programme repeatedly exceeds previous estimates or requests significantly more funding than anticipated, committees can require explanations during classified hearings or [budget reviews]({{ 'budget-review/' | relative_url }}).

Second, milestones provide context for the numbers. Large increases in expenditure may be expected if a programme is moving from research into production, entering testing or beginning deployment. Conversely, high spending accompanied by repeated milestone delays may indicate technical problems or management weaknesses rather than planned growth. The [statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}) intentionally requires both schedule and cost information because neither is especially informative on its own.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense committees...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2025</span></span></span>

Third, projected funding over the following four fiscal years exposes the Department's long-term intentions. Congress can see whether a SAP is expected to expand, stabilise or wind down, helping appropriators judge whether future commitments remain consistent with defence priorities.

These reporting elements also support comparisons across programmes. Defence committees oversee portfolios rather than isolated projects, so understanding how classified programmes compete for resources is essential even when individual technologies remain compartmented.

## Why budget oversight matters more than technical disclosure

The March 1 report reflects a distinction between programme [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) and operational secrecy.

Technical information such as platform performance, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) sources, software architecture or collection methods may lose much of its value if disclosed. Budget information, however, can often be reviewed in classified congressional settings without creating the same operational risks. The statute therefore concentrates on the information most relevant to Congress's constitutional responsibilities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether funding requests are justified.
* Whether programmes remain on schedule.
* Whether estimated total costs remain credible.
* Whether long-term financial commitments are changing.

</div>

In other words, the report allows legislators to ask, "Is this programme being managed responsibly?" rather than, "How exactly does this capability work?" That separation enables meaningful oversight while preserving the compartmentation that SAPs are intended to provide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. ## What the report deliberately leaves classified

Although the statute requires a programme description, it specifies only a **brief** description rather than a comprehensive technical account. The law similarly requires discussion of major milestones without demanding disclosure of classified test results, operational concepts or engineering specifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the. Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense committees...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2025</span></span></span>

Many details typically remain outside the annual report, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Sensitive technical performance data.
* Intelligence sources and methods.
* Operational tactics or deployment plans.
* Design features that would compromise the capability.
* Classified vulnerabilities or countermeasure assessments.

</div>

The Secretary of Defense also has statutory authority to waive inclusion of particular information in a report if disclosure would adversely affect national security. That authority is not unlimited: it must be exercised case by case, and the withheld information, together with the justification for withholding it, must still be provided to the chair and ranking member of each congressional defence committee. ## Following the money even when programmes remain secret

The March 1 reporting requirement demonstrates that secrecy and accountability are not mutually exclusive. An unacknowledged or waived SAP may remain invisible to the public and inaccessible to most government personnel, yet it still generates budget obligations, programme milestones and management decisions that Congress is expected to review.

The report therefore operates as a financial oversight mechanism rather than a vehicle for public transparency. It gives Congress structured evidence about programme health, resource consumption and future commitments without requiring disclosure of the classified capabilities themselves. In practice, the law recognises that even the most closely protected defence programmes ultimately depend on appropriated funds, making financial reporting the most durable and practical form of democratic oversight available for deeply classified activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[govinfo.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">119August 22, 2025 — (a)(1) Not later than March 1 of each year, the.
