## Introduction

Annual reviews are one of the few recurring [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) mechanisms that apply even to the most sensitive Special Access Programs (SAPs), including unacknowledged and, in some cases, waived programmes. Their purpose is straightforward: to ensure that extraordinary secrecy remains justified, that enhanced protections are still necessary, and that programmes continue to comply with classification and oversight requirements rather than simply persisting through institutional inertia. Under Executive Order 13526, agencies must maintain an [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) system for SAPs and conduct annual reviews of each programme, even though the underlying details may remain highly classified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

The existence of an annual review, however, does not by itself guarantee effective accountability. The quality of any review depends on whether reviewers can obtain complete records, whether they have authority to challenge programme managers, and whether recommendations lead to real changes. As a result, annual reviews are best understood as a governance safeguard whose effectiveness depends far more on implementation than on the existence of the requirement itself.

## What annual review requirements are meant to test

Annual SAP reviews are intended to answer a limited but important set of governance questions rather than re-justify every classified mission from first principles.

At a minimum, reviewers are expected to determine whether:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* the programme still requires SAP-level protection because ordinary classification controls would remain insufficient;
* the scope of restricted access remains proportionate to the security risk;
* administrative controls, security procedures and access records remain compliant with governing policy;
* the programme continues to satisfy legal and policy requirements for management and oversight; and
* opportunities exist to reduce, restructure or eventually remove SAP protections when exceptional controls are no longer necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

</div>

Executive Order 13526 establishes the broader principle that SAPs should be exceptional rather than permanent. It requires agencies to maintain an accounting system for SAPs and to review them annually, reflecting the assumption that extraordinary secrecy should be periodically re-examined rather than automatically renewed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

Recent Department of Defense policy reinforces this lifecycle approach by requiring planning for eventual disestablishment of SAP [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) or transfer of information to ordinary classification protections when enhanced controls are no longer justified. That emphasis recognises that exceptional secrecy should not survive simply because it already exists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

## Why review quality depends on records and access

An annual review can only evaluate what reviewers are able to see. For highly compartmented programmes, that creates a central accountability challenge.

Because SAPs deliberately restrict access to a very small number of cleared individuals, reviewers frequently rely on programme documentation, security records, financial reporting, audit trails and management briefings that are themselves classified. If documentation is incomplete, inconsistently maintained or difficult to reconcile across multiple compartments, reviewers may struggle to identify emerging problems even while technically completing the required review.

The accounting requirement in Executive Order 13526 therefore serves an accountability purpose as well as an administrative one. Maintaining a formal accounting system means there should be an authoritative record of the programme's existence, responsible officials and security status inside government even when no comparable public record exists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

Access is equally important. Reviewers who possess the appropriate clearances but lack practical access to all relevant information cannot fully evaluate necessity, duplication, compliance or efficiency. This is particularly significant for programmes that span multiple organisations or combine [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}), [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) and operational components, where information may be compartmented across several separate security channels.

For that reason, oversight relies not only on annual reviews but also on institutional actors—including agency inspectors general, designated security authorities, specialised SAP management offices and the Information Security Oversight Office (ISOO)—whose responsibilities include examining how classified information systems are administered. Executive Order 13526 specifically grants ISOO oversight responsibilities for the classification system, including access related to SAP oversight subject to the order's provisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

## Why weak follow-through can leave risks hidden

Completing an annual review is not the same as resolving identified problems.

Like many governance processes, annual reviews create recommendations rather than automatically producing corrective action. If programme managers delay implementation, if senior leadership does not prioritise recommendations, or if oversight bodies cannot verify completion, weaknesses identified during review may persist into subsequent years.

This implementation gap matters particularly in highly classified environments because relatively few outsiders are positioned to detect whether recommendations were ignored. Unlike public programmes, there is generally no routine external scrutiny from journalists, civil society organisations or independent researchers capable of comparing promised reforms against observable outcomes.

Historical experience across the wider classification system illustrates this broader governance problem. Reviews by inspectors general, ISOO and the Government Accountability Office have repeatedly emphasised that oversight depends on agencies conducting meaningful self-[inspections]({{ 'inspections/' | relative_url }}), maintaining accurate reporting and supporting an organisational culture in which classification decisions and security practices can be questioned by authorised personnel. Formal review mechanisms lose value when they become routine paperwork rather than critical evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/2013/Sep/30/2001713314/-1/-1/1/DODIG-2013-142.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[defense.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DODIG</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of WarDoD Evaluation Over-classification of National Security...November 12, 2014 — 30 Sept 2013 — Self-Inspection Reporting...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 12, 2014</span></span></span>

The same principle applies to SAP reviews. Their effectiveness depends less on the annual calendar requirement than on whether reviewers possess sufficient independence, technical expertise and institutional backing to recommend changes that programme leadership is expected to implement.

## Why annual reviews matter even for waived SAPs

Waived SAPs present the greatest accountability challenge because congressional notification may be limited to a very small group of legislators when broader reporting would endanger national security.

That narrower reporting does not eliminate oversight obligations. Current Department of Defense policy explicitly states that waived SAPs remain subject to congressional oversight, even though reporting procedures differ from those used for ordinary SAPs. Public discussion of the programmes may be impossible, but internal accountability requirements—including management responsibilities and oversight mechanisms—continue to apply.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

In practice, this makes annual reviews proportionally more important. As the number of people with knowledge of a programme decreases, periodic internal evaluation becomes one of the few structured opportunities to question whether exceptional protections remain justified, whether management practices remain sound and whether continued compartmentation serves a legitimate security purpose rather than organisational convenience.

## Do annual reviews really catch SAP problems?

Annual reviews provide an important accountability mechanism, but they are not a comprehensive safeguard.

They are strongest at ensuring that secret programmes remain within a formal governance framework rather than disappearing from institutional oversight. The requirement for annual review, programme accounting and continuing executive oversight creates documented opportunities to reassess necessity and compliance even when programmes cannot be discussed publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — Each agency head shall establish and maintain a system of accounting fo...</span></span></span>

Their limitations are equally clear. Reviews cannot compensate for incomplete records, restricted access, insufficient reviewer independence or weak implementation of recommendations. In highly compartmented environments, those practical factors determine whether an annual review functions as a rigorous accountability exercise or becomes a procedural confirmation that existing arrangements continue unchanged.

For that reason, annual reviews should be viewed as one element of a broader accountability system. They help ensure that extraordinary secrecy remains subject to recurring institutional scrutiny, but their effectiveness ultimately depends on the quality of the evidence available, the authority of the reviewers and the willingness of leadership to act on what the reviews uncover.
