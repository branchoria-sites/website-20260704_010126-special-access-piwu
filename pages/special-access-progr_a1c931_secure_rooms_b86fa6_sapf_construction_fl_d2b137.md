## Introduction

Special Access Program Facilities (SAPFs) are inspected for construction problems before anyone occupies them because many security weaknesses become expensive, disruptive or impossible to correct once the building is operational. Unlike ordinary offices, a SAPF must be formally accredited before classified Special Access Program information can be generated, discussed, processed or stored. That means the building itself must demonstrate that its physical design supports security requirements rather than relying on operational procedures after the fact. Modern Department of Defense guidance therefore integrates security review into planning, design, construction and commissioning, with [accreditation]({{ 'accreditation/' | relative_url }}) following successful inspection rather than simple completion of the building project.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/FFC/DOD/UFC/ufc_4_010_05_2023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com">[amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFC 4-010-05 SCIF/SAPF Planning, Design, and...May 20, 2025 — 26 May 2023 — The Unified Facilities Criteria (UFC) system is prescribed b...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why inspections begin before the walls are closed

A SAPF is not inspected only after construction is finished. Security personnel become involved while the facility is still being designed because structural decisions determine whether the finished room can ever satisfy accreditation requirements.

The planning guidance for SCIFs and SAPFs explains that security requirements must be incorporated into project planning, design, budgeting and construction from the outset. This avoids situations where contractors unknowingly install unsuitable walls, utilities or building systems that later require demolition and reconstruction. Early coordination also allows security requirements to be included in construction [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}), quality-control [inspections]({{ 'inspections/' | relative_url }}) and technical reviews instead of becoming expensive change orders near project completion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/FFC/DOD/UFC/ufc_4_010_05_2023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com">[nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFC 4-010-05 SCIF/SAPF Planning, Design, and...May 20, 2025 — 26 May 2023 — The Unified Facilities Criteria (UFC) system is prescribed b...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

Construction reviews therefore answer questions that cannot be resolved once people have moved in, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Whether perimeter walls have been built to the approved design.
* Whether service penetrations have been properly protected.
* Whether doors, frames and locking hardware satisfy required standards.
* Whether telecommunications and alarm infrastructure have been installed correctly.
* Whether acoustic and technical-security features can be verified before finishes conceal them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA501-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SA501 guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">o Analyze SAPF windows, ducts, ventilation, and access/inspection ports for compliance.Read more...</span></span></span>

## The small building flaws that can break secrecy

Many accreditation findings involve ordinary construction details rather than dramatic security failures. Individually these issues may appear insignificant, but together they define whether the room can adequately resist unauthorised entry, observation or information leakage.

Common areas examined include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Doors and frames.** Inspectors verify that doors, hinges, locks and surrounding construction match approved specifications and cannot be defeated through weak installation rather than weak hardware.
* **Walls, ceilings and floors.** Construction must provide a continuous secure perimeter. False ceilings, raised floors and adjoining spaces receive particular attention because concealed pathways can bypass otherwise secure entrances.
* **Windows and view ports.** Any opening capable of visual or acoustic exposure requires appropriate protection.
* **Ventilation ducts and utility penetrations.** Air ducts, cable routes, plumbing penetrations and inspection ports are examined because every opening through the perimeter represents a potential vulnerability unless properly protected.
* **Telecommunications infrastructure.** Cable routing, communications equipment and associated penetrations must comply with approved security requirements before systems become operational.
* **Intrusion detection systems.** Alarm devices, sensors and monitoring arrangements are verified before accreditation to ensure that physical protection is functional from the first day of operation.
* **Classified destruction areas.** Facilities intended for document destruction or handling classified waste are inspected to ensure that disposal processes do not introduce avoidable security weaknesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA501-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SA501 guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">o Analyze SAPF windows, ducts, ventilation, and access/inspection ports for compliance.Read more...</span></span></span>

</div>

These inspections deliberately focus on mundane construction details because seemingly minor deficiencies often become hidden permanently once finishes are complete.

## Why correcting problems later costs far more

Finding a problem before occupancy is usually a construction issue. Finding the same problem after accreditation often becomes both a construction project and a security event.

For example, an incorrectly installed duct may require finished ceilings to be removed. An unapproved wall assembly may require occupied offices to be emptied while reconstruction occurs. Poorly planned telecommunications pathways may require secure [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}) to stop while infrastructure is replaced.

The financial consequences extend beyond labour and materials. Delays may affect programme schedules, contractor availability, equipment installation and accreditation timelines. The planning guidance therefore treats security requirements as project-management requirements as much as physical-security requirements. Early coordination helps avoid redesign, repeated inspections and costly rework.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/FFC/DOD/UFC/ufc_4_010_05_2023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com">[nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nibs-s3-wbdg3-production.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFC 4-010-05 SCIF/SAPF Planning, Design, and...May 20, 2025 — 26 May 2023 — The Unified Facilities Criteria (UFC) system is prescribed b...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 20, 2025</span></span></span>

## How inspections support accreditation rather than simply checking workmanship

Construction inspections are not ordinary building inspections. Their purpose is to determine whether the facility can qualify for formal accreditation to handle SAP information.

The Centre for Development of Security Excellence (CDSE) training identifies the Fixed Facility Checklist (FFC) as the primary inspection tool used during initial accreditation and subsequent inspections. The checklist records the facility's construction, security layers, access controls, structural features, telecommunications, acoustical protection, destruction methods and technical-security information. Because it is treated as a living document, changes to the facility after occupancy can trigger further review if they affect accreditation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/shorts/SAS0122-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fixed Facility Checklist Short Student GuideIt&#x27;s the responsibility of the SAPF-AO to inspect any SAP area before accreditation...</span></span></span>

This means inspectors are evaluating whether the completed construction matches the approved security design—not merely whether the building complies with conventional construction codes.

## Construction decisions that influence long-term operating costs

Early construction choices continue affecting a SAPF throughout its operational life.

A well-designed facility generally experiences:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Fewer accreditation delays.
* Less disruptive renovation work.
* Lower risk of operational interruptions.
* Easier periodic re-inspections.
* Better documentation when modifications become necessary.

</div>

Conversely, poor documentation or undocumented construction changes can complicate future renovations because every alteration that affects the secure perimeter, utilities or technical protections may require additional review before the facility can continue operating under its accreditation. The Fixed Facility Checklist is intended to capture these characteristics so that later inspections have a documented baseline against which changes can be assessed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/shorts/SAS0122-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fixed Facility Checklist Short Student GuideIt&#x27;s the responsibility of the SAPF-AO to inspect any SAP area before accreditation...</span></span></span>

## Why pre-occupancy review is a security measure, not just a building milestone

The central purpose of finding SAPF construction problems before occupancy is to prevent security weaknesses from becoming permanent parts of an accredited facility. Once classified work begins, every reconstruction effort introduces operational disruption, additional expense and renewed security oversight.

By shifting inspection into the planning and construction phases, SAP security treats ordinary architectural details—doors, ducts, wall penetrations, communications pathways and destruction facilities—as security decisions rather than maintenance issues.
