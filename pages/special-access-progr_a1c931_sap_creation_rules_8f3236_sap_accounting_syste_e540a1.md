## Introduction

A common myth about United States special access programmes (SAPs), particularly unacknowledged or waived programmes, is that they can exist completely outside government records: funded through invisible [budgets]({{ 'budgets/' | relative_url }}), unknown to the agencies that sponsor them, and immune from later review. That is not how the executive-order framework is designed to operate. While the public, most officials and even many cleared personnel may be unaware of a programme's existence, the responsible agency is expected to maintain an internal accounting system for every SAP. Executive Order 13526 expressly requires each agency head to establish and maintain such a system, ensuring that even exceptionally secret programmes remain traceable inside authorised government oversight mechanisms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2010-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2010-title3-vol1-eo13526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Classified National Security InformationSeptember 3, 2014 — 29 Dec 2009 — (2) Each agency head shall establish and maintain a syst...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 3, 2014</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to understanding SAP governance. Secrecy limits who may know about a programme, but it does not eliminate institutional responsibility for recording, managing and reviewing it. The accounting requirement exists precisely because highly compartmented programmes would otherwise become difficult to supervise, evaluate or terminate.

## Why agencies must account for SAPs

Executive Order 13526 treats accounting as part of the legal architecture of special access rather than as an optional administrative task. Section 4.3 requires agency heads to establish and maintain a system of accounting for SAPs consistent with implementing directives. The same section also subjects SAPs to oversight under Section 5.4 and grants the Director of the Information Security Oversight Office ([ISOO]({{ 'isoo/' | relative_url }})) access to programmes necessary to perform statutory oversight functions, although particularly sensitive programmes may restrict that access to the Director alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2010-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2010-title3-vol1-eo13526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[govinfo.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Classified National Security InformationSeptember 3, 2014 — 29 Dec 2009 — (2) Each agency head shall establish and maintain a syst...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 3, 2014</span></span></span>

The accounting requirement serves several practical purposes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It identifies that a programme formally exists under authorised authority.
* It records the programme's sponsoring organisation and management responsibility.
* It supports periodic reviews to determine whether the extraordinary protection remains justified.
* It allows authorised oversight bodies to distinguish legitimate compartmented programmes from unauthorised or improperly managed activities.
* It provides an institutional record that survives personnel changes.

</div>

Without such records, agencies could not reliably answer basic governance questions such as how many SAPs they operate, who approved them, whether duplicate [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) exist or whether protection requirements remain necessary.

Importantly, an accounting system does not imply a publicly accessible database. The existence of the records is itself classified when appropriate, and access to those records is tightly controlled.

## What accounting makes visible inside secrecy

The accounting required by Executive Order 13526 is often misunderstood as ordinary financial bookkeeping. In practice it is broader than budget accounting.

Within classified administration, accounting makes several aspects of a programme visible to authorised officials while keeping them hidden from everyone else. Depending on the programme and agency, internal records may include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* programme establishment and approval documentation;
* responsible officials and sponsoring authority;
* classification and compartment identifiers;
* security control requirements;
* review schedules;
* authorised access structures;
* termination or continuation decisions.

</div>

This internal visibility allows agencies to manage a portfolio of compartmented programmes rather than treating each as an isolated secret known only to its immediate participants.

Modern Department of Defense SAP policy reinforces this governance model. Current policy places SAP management within an enterprise governance structure headed by the Special Access Program Oversight Committee (SAPOC), whose membership includes senior defence leadership as well as the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller) and Chief Financial Officer. The directive emphasises governance, transparency within authorised channels, accuracy and timely management rather than unrestricted secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod directive 5205.07 special access program policySeptember 11, 2024 — 12 Sept 2024 — (1) The SAPOC is the senior governing body of t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 11, 2024</span></span></span>

The presence of the Department's chief financial official on the governing body illustrates an important principle: protecting classified information does not remove financial [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}). Instead, financial management itself operates inside classified channels.

## Why "black" does not mean "unrecorded"

Popular culture often portrays a "black programme" as existing entirely outside government administration, funded by hidden money with no official trace.

The executive-order framework supports a much narrower understanding. A programme may be:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* publicly acknowledged;
* unacknowledged, meaning its existence is classified; or
* waived from broader congressional reporting under statutory procedures.

</div>

None of those categories implies that the sponsoring department lacks internal knowledge that the programme exists. Instead, secrecy changes who may see the records, not whether records exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2010-title3-vol1/pdf/CFR-2010-title3-vol1-eo13526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Classified National Security InformationSeptember 3, 2014 — 29 Dec 2009 — (2) Each agency head shall establish and maintain a syst...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 3, 2014</span></span></span>

This distinction explains an apparent paradox. A member of the public—or even a senior official without the required authorisation—may be unable to confirm whether a particular SAP exists. Yet within the authorised security, financial and oversight systems, the sponsoring agency is still expected to know that it has created, funded and manages that programme.

The myth of the "completely unrecorded" black programme therefore confuses compartmentation with administrative invisibility. The executive-order system is designed to permit the former while preventing the latter.

## Accounting supports review, oversight and termination

Internal accounting also enables one of the least discussed aspects of SAP governance: programmes are expected to be reviewed throughout their lifecycle.

A programme cannot be meaningfully evaluated if there is no authoritative record showing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* when it was established;
* why extraordinary protection was justified;
* who approved it;
* whether the original security rationale still applies.

</div>

Executive Order 13526 links SAPs to continuing oversight rather than one-time authorisation. ISOO oversight responsibilities, combined with agency accounting systems, create institutional mechanisms through which programmes can be examined, modified or terminated as circumstances change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information29 Dec 2009 — Special access programs shall be subject to the oversight program esta...</span></span></span>

Department of Defense policy similarly treats SAP governance as an ongoing management process involving designated oversight bodies rather than permanent autonomous compartments. Governance structures exist to oversee programme creation, administration and eventual closure where appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod directive 5205.07 special access program policySeptember 11, 2024 — 12 Sept 2024 — (1) The SAPOC is the senior governing body of t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 11, 2024</span></span></span>

Without accounting records, agencies would struggle to determine whether a programme should continue, merge with another compartment, transition to ordinary classified handling or be closed entirely.

## Historical experience shows that records matter

Past oversight reviews demonstrate both the importance of records and the distinction between incomplete records and nonexistent programmes.

For example, a 1993 review by the U.S. Government Accountability Office found weaknesses in certain Department of Defense administrative records, including the absence of centralised records for some SAP access denials and revocations. The review criticised limitations in record management but did not conclude that SAPs themselves operated without official accounting. Instead, it highlighted shortcomings in specific administrative processes that complicated oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-162.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-93-162 DOD Special Access ProgramsMay 5, 1993 — 6 May 1993 — Because DOD does not maintain centralized records on SAP denials an...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 5, 1993</span></span></span>

This distinction is significant. Weak record management is an oversight problem. It is not evidence that programmes legally exist outside government accounting systems altogether.

## The real limit of secrecy

The executive-order framework recognises that extraordinary secrecy can create governance risks if left unchecked. Requiring agencies to maintain accounting systems addresses one of those risks by ensuring that institutional memory does not disappear behind compartmentation.

The result is a layered model of visibility. The public may know nothing about an [unacknowledged SAP]({{ 'unacknowledged-7fdfef/' | relative_url }}). Most government employees may know nothing either. Even many cleared personnel may have no access. Yet the sponsoring agency is expected to maintain classified records sufficient for authorised management, financial accountability, oversight and eventual review.

For that reason, the statement that "the most secret SAP is unrecorded" is inconsistent with the governing framework. The law anticipates that secrecy and accountability coexist: information may be concealed from almost everyone while remaining formally recorded inside the government's own classified accounting and oversight systems.
