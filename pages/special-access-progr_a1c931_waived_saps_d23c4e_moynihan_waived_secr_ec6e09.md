## Introduction

The 1997 report of the Moynihan Commission did not argue that highly sensitive programmes should be abandoned. Instead, it warned that increasingly restrictive secrecy controls, including the use of waived special access programmes (SAPs), could weaken democratic [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) even when they served legitimate security purposes. The Commission's concern was not simply that some programmes were hidden from the public, but that layers of compartmentation could also reduce oversight inside government and [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}). Waived programmes became a prominent example because they represented the most restrictive end of the Department of Defense's secrecy system: programmes whose existence was reported to only a handful of congressional leaders, often through oral briefings rather than ordinary written reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

Within the broader debate over waived SAPs and narrowed congressional reporting, the Commission's contribution was to place these programmes inside a larger critique of excessive secrecy. Its argument was that protecting genuine national-security secrets and maintaining effective oversight were complementary goals rather than competing ones.

## How the Commission described waived programmes

The Commission treated special access programmes as part of a continuum of increasingly restrictive access controls. It explained that SAPs imposed security measures beyond ordinary classified information and distinguished between acknowledged, unacknowledged, and the most sensitive category—waived programmes.

Its description of waived programmes was strikingly concise. Black, or unacknowledged, programmes concealed even their existence from the public, while waived programmes went further by being exempt from normal congressional reporting requirements. According to the report, only the chair, ranking member and, in some circumstances, a very small number of additional committee members or staff were informed, and this notification was typically oral rather than written.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

The Commission did not portray waived status as inherently illegitimate. Instead, it treated it as an exceptional mechanism whose use illustrated a broader institutional problem: every additional layer of secrecy reduced the number of people able to evaluate whether secrecy itself remained justified.

## Why oral notification raised concerns

The Commission's concern about oral notification was primarily institutional rather than procedural.

Written reporting creates records that can be reviewed over time, compared against [budgets]({{ 'budgets/' | relative_url }}), examined by cleared oversight staff and used to assess whether programmes continue to satisfy legal requirements. Oral briefings inevitably leave a thinner documentary trail. Although oral notification may reduce the risk of sensitive documents being leaked or mishandled, it also makes sustained oversight more dependent upon the memories, continuity and personal engagement of a very small number of elected officials.

Seen through the Commission's broader philosophy, this reflected a recurring pattern in the American secrecy system. The report argued that excessive compartmentation often protected information by reducing institutional knowledge rather than by improving security itself. As information became concentrated among fewer officials, opportunities for independent review, policy correction and accountability diminished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

This concern aligned with one of the Commission's central findings: secrecy is a form of governmental regulation. If exercised without sufficient accountability, secrecy can impair decision-making because policymakers, overseers and even other government officials lack access to information needed to challenge assumptions or identify errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">personnel security practices...</span></span></span>

## When secrecy controls outgrow accountability

The Commission deliberately framed waived programmes as symptoms of a wider governance issue rather than isolated anomalies.

Its broader argument rested on several related observations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Special access controls often accumulated over time without equivalent review of whether extraordinary protection remained necessary.
* Multiple layers of compartmentation could create overlapping "programmes within programmes", making it difficult even for senior officials to understand the overall structure of protected activities.
* Security measures imposed real operational and financial costs that were not always weighed against their benefits.
* Oversight mechanisms tended to focus on programme performance while paying less attention to whether exceptional secrecy requirements themselves remained justified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

</div>

In this sense, waived SAPs represented an endpoint on a continuum. The Commission accepted that exceptional cases would exist, but argued that exceptional access restrictions should themselves be subject to continual scrutiny rather than becoming permanent administrative habits.

## How SAPOC answered part of the problem

The report also acknowledged reforms that had already begun inside the Department of Defense.

A central example was the creation of the Special Access Program Oversight Committee (SAPOC) in 1994. The Commission described SAPOC as an effort to standardise and formalise approval, annual validation, restructuring and termination of Department of Defense SAPs. Annual reviews were intended to determine whether programmes still required extraordinary compartmentation, whether redundant programmes could be eliminated and whether some activities could return to ordinary classified status. The Committee was also intended to improve senior-level management and ensure required information reached Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

The Commission viewed these developments positively but stopped well short of declaring the problem solved. It noted concerns that SAPOC's reviews concentrated heavily on programme cost, schedule and performance while paying less attention to the security measures themselves and their associated costs. It also observed that inconsistent security practices continued across different SAPs despite broader efforts to standardise them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>

Accordingly, SAPOC was presented as a meaningful governance improvement rather than a complete answer to the accountability issues raised by waived secrecy.

## The Commission's lasting significance

The Moynihan Commission's discussion of waived SAPs has remained influential because it shifted the debate away from the simplistic question of whether secrets should exist. Its central concern was institutional design: how governments preserve genuinely necessary secrets without allowing secrecy mechanisms themselves to escape meaningful oversight.

That perspective remains relevant to later statutory frameworks governing waived SAPs. Modern law continues to permit the Secretary of Defense to limit standard reporting for exceptionally sensitive programmes while requiring notification through a much narrower congressional channel. The Commission's warning anticipated the continuing policy tension: reducing the number of people who know a secret may improve operational security, but every reduction in visibility also narrows the pool of officials able to evaluate legality, effectiveness and continued necessity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[publicintelligence.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">“special access program” is used throughout this report to denote any program that limits access...Read more...</span></span></span>
