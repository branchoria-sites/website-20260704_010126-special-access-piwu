## Introduction

Special-access programmes and [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) compartments use the same basic access logic: a clearance is not enough, and a person must be formally approved for a particular body of information. That shared logic is why people often confuse SAPs with Sensitive Compartmented Information, or SCI. The difference is administrative and functional. A SAP is a programme-level protection regime created for especially sensitive classified information when ordinary controls are insufficient; SCI is a category of classified national intelligence protected inside formal access-control systems established by the Director of National Intelligence. Executive Order 13526 treats SAPs as exceptional programmes that should be kept to an “absolute minimum”, while Intelligence Community Directive 703 defines SCI as intelligence concerning or derived from sources, methods or analytical processes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_saps_vs_compartments_928e4c-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Compartments" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters because “compartmented” does not always mean “SAP”, and “above Top Secret” is a misleading phrase for both. The real question is not whether one label is more secret than the other, but which authority created the control system, what type of information it protects, who manages access, how it is marked, and what oversight route applies.

## Similar Access Logic

Both SAPs and intelligence compartments begin from the same security problem: some classified information is too sensitive to distribute merely because someone holds the right clearance level. A person with a [Top Secret clearance]({{ 'clearance-gap/' | relative_url }}) may still be excluded from a Top Secret SAP, and a person eligible for SCI may still be excluded from a particular SCI control system or compartment. In both cases, the working rule is a tighter version of “need to know”: access follows a specific operational, analytical, engineering or oversight requirement, not general seniority.

Executive Order 13526 states that SAPs may be established only when the threat or vulnerability is exceptional and normal eligibility rules for the same classification level are not enough. It also says SAPs should involve a “reasonably small” access population consistent with enhanced protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span> SCI policy uses similar language through a different route: ICD 703 defines SCI as classified national intelligence that must be protected within formal access-control systems established by the DNI.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-703.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

The overlap is visible in everyday security practice. Both worlds can involve [read-ins]({{ 'read-ins/' | relative_url }}), recorded access approvals, stricter handling rules, special facilities, special markings, limited distribution, and extra security briefings. Both can also sit at the same classification levels as ordinary classified information. The label does not create a fourth classification level above Top Secret; it adds a control architecture on top of Confidential, Secret or Top Secret.

A simple way to read the difference is this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Classification level** asks how much damage unauthorised disclosure could cause.
* **SAP or SCI control** asks who may enter a special access channel for that information.
* **Need to know** asks whether this particular person needs that particular information now.
* **Markings** tell authorised holders which control system, programme or dissemination limits apply.

</div>

That is why public shorthand can mislead. “TS/SCI” is often spoken of as though it were a clearance rank, but it is more accurately a combination of Top Secret eligibility plus eligibility and approval for SCI access. “SAP” is likewise not a rank. It is a programme-specific access regime, usually with its own approval chain and security rules.

## Different Programme Structures

The sharpest distinction is that SAPs are programme structures, while SCI compartments are intelligence-control structures. A SAP normally has a defined programme, sponsor, protection plan, access roster, management office and oversight route. SCI, by contrast, is tied to classified national intelligence and the DNI’s responsibility to protect intelligence sources and methods.

Executive Order 13526 lists the senior officials who may create SAPs and says that, for SAPs concerning intelligence sources, methods and activities, the Director of National Intelligence exercises that function, except for military operational, strategic and tactical programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span> DoD’s 2024 SAP instruction reflects this programme-management tradition: it establishes management, administration and oversight procedures for DoD SAPs and assigns responsibilities across the Defence Department’s SAP governance system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

SCI follows a different centre of gravity. ICD 703 is about the protection of classified national intelligence, including SCI, and it roots that system in the DNI’s statutory responsibility to protect intelligence sources and methods and to set uniform SCI access standards across government and contractors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-703.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The NSA’s declassified classification policy gives the operational feel of that system: SCI control systems contain compartments and sub-compartments designed to segregate data and limit access to those with an “absolute need-to-know”, with examples such as Special Intelligence, the HUMINT Control System and TALENT KEYHOLE.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nsa.gov/portals/75/documents/news-features/declassified-documents/nsa-css-policies/NSACSS_PM_1-52_20210108.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsa.gov">[nsa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U) NSA/CSS Policy Manual 1-52, &quot;NSA/CSS Classification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U) NSA/CSS Policy Manual 1-52, &quot;NSA/CSS Classification</span></span></span>

In practical terms, SAPs are often used to protect especially sensitive capabilities, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) efforts, operational plans or technologies. SCI compartments are more naturally associated with intelligence collection, intelligence-derived reporting, sensitive sources, technical methods and analytical processes. That is a broad distinction rather than a clean wall: DoD can have intelligence SAPs, and SAP information can interact with SCI systems. But the administrative tradition differs. A SAP asks, “What programme or capability needs extraordinary protection?” SCI asks, “What intelligence source, method or compartmented intelligence channel requires formal control?”

## Why Markings Make the Difference Visible

The distinction is not merely conceptual; it appears in document markings. DoD marking guidance treats SCI and SAP as separate categories in the banner-line syntax for classified documents. The marking order is given as classification, then SCI, then SAP, followed by other categories such as Atomic Energy Act information, foreign government information and dissemination controls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dodig.mil/Portals/48/Documents/Policy/520001_vol2.pdf?ver=2017-04-25-160926-223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dodig.mil">[DOD Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dodig.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520001 vol2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520001 vol2</span></span></span>

That order matters because it shows that SCI and SAP are not synonyms. A document may carry an SCI control marking, a SAP marking, both, or neither. The presence of both does not mean the same rule is being repeated twice; it means two different control regimes may apply to the same document.

A simplified example would be:

**TOP SECRET//SCI-COMPARTMENT//SAR-PROGRAM//NOFORN**

That line would tell an authorised reader several things at once. The document is Top Secret. It contains SCI-controlled material. It also contains SAP material marked “Special Access Required”. It may not be released to foreign nationals under the dissemination control. Each segment answers a different handling question.

The Intelligence Community’s markings material also illustrates why the public record is necessarily partial. The 2016 IC Markings System Register released through FOIA notes that some security markings are not published because of special access requirements and are kept in an unpublished register by ODNI special programmes staff.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/jobaids/information/IC-Markings-System-Register.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">IC Markings System Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IC Markings System Register</span></span></span> This does not prove anything exotic by itself; it simply shows that the visible marking system has public and non-public layers, which is exactly what one would expect in a compartmented security regime.

## Why Readers Confuse the Terms

The confusion persists because SAPs and SCI sit next to each other in the same security culture. Both are invoked in leaks, court filings, clearance discussions, journalism, contractor job descriptions and political arguments about secret programmes. Both can require special facilities, special access approval and strict need-to-know. Both are also commonly described, inaccurately, as “above Top Secret”.

The biggest source of confusion is the word “compartment”. In ordinary language, any tightly restricted information channel is a compartment. In Intelligence Community usage, SCI compartments are a particular family of control systems for classified national intelligence. In SAP usage, a programme may itself contain compartments, sub-elements or restricted access channels, but that does not automatically make it SCI. “Compartmented” is the general mechanism; SCI is one formal intelligence version of that mechanism; SAP is a programme-based version used when special access protection is approved.

Another source of confusion is historical overlap. Older and secondary sources sometimes describe SCI control systems alongside SAPs or treat both as examples of “controlled access programmes”. Current public policy is more careful: DoD’s SAP instruction explicitly coordinates with the DNI’s Controlled Access Program Central Office on Intelligence Community compartmented capabilities and requires deconfliction between DoD SAP names and DNI controlled access programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> That coordination language is important because it recognises adjacency without collapsing the systems into one label.

A third reason is that both systems can be invisible to outsiders. An [unacknowledged SAP]({{ 'unacknowledged-7fdfef/' | relative_url }}) may protect the very fact of a programme’s existence, while many intelligence compartments and [codewords]({{ 'codewords/' | relative_url }}) are classified or omitted from public marking registers. To a reader outside government, both can look like “secret codeword worlds”. Internally, however, the labels determine who owns the access decision, which office keeps the records, which oversight channel applies, and which marking rules govern the document.

## The Oversight Split

SAPs and intelligence compartments also differ in their accountability pathways. For SAPs, Executive Order 13526 requires an accounting system, annual review, and Information Security Oversight Office access, with tighter limits possible for extraordinarily sensitive programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span> For DoD SAPs, 10 U.S.C. § 119 adds a specific congressional oversight framework, including reporting to the defence committees and notification requirements before new SAPs are initiated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

DoD’s current SAP instruction reflects that statutory route. It requires congressional notification before a new SAP umbrella is created and describes how access to non-waived SAPs may be granted to professional staff members of defence and intelligence committees within oversight jurisdiction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> Waived SAPs narrow ordinary reporting further, but they are still a statutory category rather than an absence of oversight.

SCI oversight is anchored more heavily in the DNI’s intelligence-protection authorities and Intelligence Community policy. ICD 703 applies to the Intelligence Community and to others who handle SCI, including executive departments, the Executive Office of the President, the legislative and judicial branches, and other US entities as defined by Executive Order 13526.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-703.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span> The Director of the CIA also has a role in SCI access determinations and SCIF [accreditation]({{ 'accreditation/' | relative_url }}) for the legislative and judicial branches and parts of the executive branch outside the IC, unless another authority has been designated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.odni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-703.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: odni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">odni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Microsoft Word</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Microsoft Word</span></span></span>

This split helps explain why a question such as “Who is allowed to see it?” cannot be answered from the classification level alone. For a SAP, the answer may run through a programme access request, a SAP Central Office, programme-specific access approval authority and congressional SAP reporting rules. For SCI, it may run through SCI eligibility standards, control-system access, compartment approval and SCIF accreditation. Both are restrictive; they are restrictive through different bureaucratic machines.

## A Practical Test for Telling Them Apart

The most useful way to distinguish SAPs from intelligence compartments is to ask what the control is organised around.

If the control is organised around a **programme, capability, acquisition effort, operational activity or specially protected DoD effort**, it is likely to be discussed as a SAP. If the control is organised around **classified national intelligence, sources, methods, collection channels or analytical processes**, it is more likely to be SCI or another Intelligence Community controlled-access structure. That does not settle every edge case, but it usually points in the right direction.

A second test is the marking. SAP material usually appears with “SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED” or “SAR” plus a programme nickname, codeword or authorised abbreviation. SCI material appears in the SCI marking field, with control-system and compartment indicators. DoD marking rules place SCI and SAP in separate fields, which is the clearest public clue that the two regimes can coexist without being identical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dodig.mil/Portals/48/Documents/Policy/520001_vol2.pdf?ver=2017-04-25-160926-223" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dodig.mil">[DOD Intelligence Agency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dodig.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520001 vol2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520001 vol2</span></span></span>

A third test is the approving authority. SAP creation is limited to senior officials named in Executive Order 13526, with special rules for intelligence-related SAPs and DoD statutory reporting. SCI control systems are established under the DNI’s authority over intelligence sources, methods and classified national intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>

The short version for readers is this: SAPs and intelligence compartments are cousins, not twins. Both are mechanisms for enforced need-to-know. SAPs are exceptional programme-protection regimes. SCI compartments are intelligence-control systems. The confusion is understandable because both live inside the same classified ecosystem, but the distinction shapes real decisions about access, markings, oversight, facilities and accountability.
