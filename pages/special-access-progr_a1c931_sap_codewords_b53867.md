## Introduction

Special-access programmes use nicknames, codewords and programme identifiers to make secrecy usable. A SAP name is not meant to tell outsiders what the programme does; it is a handling label that helps authorised people recognise which protected channel, marking rules and distribution limits apply. In the US system, the visible name may be a two-word nickname such as the training example “BUTTER POPCORN”, a single codeword, or a short programme identifier used in [portion markings]({{ 'portion-markings/' | relative_url }}). The point is controlled recognition, not public explanation. Official marking rules require SAP material to show the classification level, the “SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED” or “SAR” caveat, and the relevant programme nickname, codeword or identifier in a prescribed order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507 V4p</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_sap_codewords_b53867-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Codewords" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That makes SAP names easy to misunderstand. To an outsider, a phrase in a leaked banner line can look like a project [title]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}), a mission slogan or a clue. Inside the system, it is closer to an access-routing tag: it signals who may handle the information, how it must be marked, and which compartmented security office or programme channel governs it.

## Nicknames Are Not the Same as Codewords

A nickname and a codeword can both label a sensitive programme, but they do different work. US military policy distinguishes a nickname as a combination of separate unclassified words with an unclassified meaning, used for administrative, morale or public-information purposes. A codeword, by contrast, is a single word assigned a classified meaning by authority to protect classified plans, [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}) or information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/navy_nicknames_and_codeword_ma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Navy Nicknames and Codeword Management</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American Scientists Navy Nicknames and Codeword Management</span></span></span>

That difference matters because the name itself may have a different classification status from the thing it points to. A nickname can remain unclassified even when the programme it labels is highly classified. A codeword may be classified while active, and official [NICKA]({{ 'nicka/' | relative_url }}) guidance warns that codewords and their meanings should not be brought together unnecessarily in correspondence. The same Joint Chiefs manual states that nicknames and exercise terms remain unclassified, while codewords must not be discussed over unclassified communications media even when they are inactive or cancelled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Joint_Staff/16-F-1538_DOC_01_CJCSM_3150-29E.pdf?ver=2019-03-15-113927-773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span></span></span>

In SAP practice, this creates a layered vocabulary:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Nickname:** usually two unclassified words that label a programme or activity without describing it.
* **Codeword:** a single word with a classified meaning, used when the association itself needs protection.
* **Programme identifier:** a shortened two- or three-character designator, often used in portion markings or controlled syntax.
* **Banner marking:** the visible line on a classified document that combines classification, SAP caveat, programme name or identifier, and any dissemination controls.

</div>

The confusion comes from everyday language. People often call all secret labels “code names”. In SAP handling, the distinction is more precise. A harmless-looking nickname can belong to a very sensitive programme, while a codeword may be sensitive because knowing what it refers to reveals protected information.

## Names Are a Handling Mechanism, Not a Puzzle Key

The best way to understand SAP names is to imagine a document moving through a controlled environment. The marking on the page must tell the right people enough to protect it, but not so much that every handler learns the underlying subject. DoD marking rules require a SAP document banner to include the classification level, the caveat “SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED” or “SAR”, and the programme nickname or codeword; assigned short PIDs are not supposed to replace the full nickname or codeword in the banner line.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507 V4p</span></span></span>

The same rules show how names become operational instructions. If two SAPs are involved, both may appear in the banner line. If three or more SAPs are involved, DoD training says the banner may use “MULTIPLE PROGRAMS” instead of listing every programme name. If a dissemination control applies, such as “WAIVED”, it appears after the programme name or names.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/shorts/SAS0030-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Speical Access Programs: Markings Short</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Speical Access Programs: Markings Short</span></span></span>

That structure is deliberately mechanical. A line such as `TOP SECRET//SAR-BUTTER POPCORN//NOFORN` in training material is not trying to tell a story about butter, popcorn or food. It tells a trained reader that the material is Top Secret, falls under a SAP channel, uses a particular programme identifier, and is not releasable to foreign nationals. The [Intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) Community markings register describes SAP markings as denoting classified information requiring extraordinary protection, and defines a programme identifier as the assigned nickname, codeword or abbreviation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/jobaids/information/IC-Markings-System-Register.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">IC Markings System Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IC Markings System Register</span></span></span>

This is why SAP names can be both visible and opaque. The system has to mark documents, media, databases and briefings clearly enough to prevent mishandling. Yet the label is stripped of descriptive content so that accidental exposure of the label does not automatically expose the programme’s purpose.

## How Names Fit Into the Classification Line

SAP labels do not replace ordinary classification levels. They sit alongside them. A person reading a marked document should first see whether the information is Confidential, Secret or Top Secret, then whether additional [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) or special-access controls apply. SAP markings normally follow any Sensitive Compartmented Information markings and are introduced with a double slash before the “SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED” or “SAR” indicator.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/jobaids/information/IC-Markings-System-Register.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">IC Markings System Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IC Markings System Register</span></span></span>

The practical result is a compact syntax. DoD and Intelligence Community examples use forms such as:

`TOP SECRET//SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED-BUTTER POPCORN`

or the abbreviated form:

`TOP SECRET//SAR-BP`

In the first version, the programme nickname is spelled out. In the second, the short programme abbreviation appears. The IC markings register notes that programme identifiers may be spelled out or abbreviated, and that a programme identifier abbreviation is the two- or three-character designator for the programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/jobaids/information/IC-Markings-System-Register.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">IC Markings System Register</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IC Markings System Register</span></span></span>

Portion markings add another layer. A paragraph containing SAP information is marked with the classification level, “SAR”, and the assigned programme identifier. DoD’s SAP marking manual gives examples such as a banner using a full nickname while the portion mark uses a shortened PID. That lets a document show full handling information at the top and bottom of the page while keeping paragraph-level markings compact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507 V4p</span></span></span>

Waived SAPs add one more visible signal. DoD rules state that SAPs exempted from normal congressional reporting requirements by the Secretary of Defense must be marked “WAIVED” in the banner line, applicable portions and media, with “WAIVED” placed last as a dissemination control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507 V4p</span></span></span>

## Why the Words Often Sound Random

SAP nicknames are supposed to avoid obvious disclosure. The ordinary reader may expect a secret aircraft programme to have a name hinting at speed, stealth or altitude. Naming rules push in the opposite direction: the safer label is often bland, disconnected or even slightly absurd.

The broader DoD naming system is managed through NICKA, the Code Word, Nickname and Exercise Term system. A Joint Chiefs manual says NICKA maintains records of reported codewords, all exercise terms and authorised nicknames; validates usage with assigned agencies; and helps ensure that authorised nicknames and codewords are not duplicated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Joint_Staff/16-F-1538_DOC_01_CJCSM_3150-29E.pdf?ver=2019-03-15-113927-773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span></span></span>

That matters for SAPs because duplication would be dangerous. If the same name pointed to different controlled activities, security offices, contractors and programme managers could misroute material or misunderstand access. NICKA is therefore less a name generator than a registry and control mechanism. Reporting on the system, based on official material, similarly describes it as a policy-and-database structure that archives defence monikers and prevents duplication rather than as a machine that invents meaningful titles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.twz.com/29353/how-the-pentagon-comes-up-with-all-those-secret-project-nicknames-and-crazy-code-words" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: twz.com">[TWZ]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">twz.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Here Is How The Pentagon Comes Up With Code Words And Secret Project Nicknames</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Here Is How The Pentagon Comes Up With Code Words And Secret Project Nicknames</span></span></span>

There are also taste and policy constraints. Navy guidance reported by the Federation of American Scientists says nicknames should not convey aggression inconsistent with US ideals or foreign policy, nor contain terms offensive to good taste or derogatory to a group, sect or creed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/navy_nicknames_and_codeword_ma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Navy Nicknames and Codeword Management</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American Scientists Navy Nicknames and Codeword Management</span></span></span>

So the oddness is partly the point. A name that sounds too descriptive may reveal something. A name that sounds too dramatic may cause political or diplomatic problems. A name that is already in use may cause handling errors. The safest label is often one that is distinctive enough for internal routing and empty enough to tell outsiders almost nothing.

## When a Name Becomes a Compartment

Some names do more than label a single project. They can describe a control system, a family of compartments or a long-lived access regime. Historical satellite reconnaissance provides the clearest public examples because many Cold War records have been declassified.

The TALENT-KEYHOLE system was created to protect US satellite reconnaissance information and products, while the BYEMAN control system protected sensitive reconnaissance satellite design, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) and operations. A declassified NRO guide says TALENT KEYHOLE was approved in 1960, BYEMAN was established in 1961, and BYEMAN was officially retired on 20 May 2005.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Portals/65/documents/foia/declass/ForAll/040819/F-2017-00100_C05102145.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[nro.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nro.gov.</span></span></span>

Those labels were not simple project names. They structured who could know about satellite systems, who could use the intelligence product, and which security rules applied to different phases of research, development, acquisition, operations and exploitation. The same NRO document explains that BYEMAN and TALENT KEYHOLE were used to protect critical satellite reconnaissance systems and programmes across those phases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/Portals/65/documents/foia/declass/ForAll/040819/F-2017-00100_C05102145.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[nro.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nro.gov.</span></span></span>

The National Security Archive’s satellite-reconnaissance collection shows the same point from a different angle. It describes TALENT-KEYHOLE as part of the effort to protect satellite reconnaissance programmes and their products, and notes that a 1964 memo defined TALENT and KEYHOLE, specified codewords for types of TK material, and gave classification guidance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive2.gwu.edu/NSAEBB/NSAEBB225/index.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive2.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive2.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gwu.edu.</span></span></span>

This is a useful corrective to the popular idea that a codeword is merely a secret project nickname. In practice, names such as BYEMAN or TALENT-KEYHOLE could act as access architecture. They mapped communities, documents, briefings and systems into controlled channels.

## Why Names Can Mislead Outsiders

SAP names mislead because they are designed to be non-explanatory. An outsider who sees a nickname may make three common mistakes.

First, they may treat the name as a literal clue. A two-word nickname could sound technological, geographical or symbolic, but naming rules often favour unrelated words precisely to avoid meaning. That means inference from the words alone is weak evidence.

Second, they may confuse a marking with a programme description. A line containing “SAR”, a nickname and “NOFORN” or “WAIVED” is primarily a handling instruction. It may reveal that material belongs to a special-access channel, but it does not by itself reveal the programme’s mission, sponsor, budget, contractor, location or legality.

Third, they may assume that a named compartment is still current. Legacy markings persist in archives, declassified documents and older reporting. The ODNI markings register, for example, records BYEMAN as a legacy SCI control system and states that the BYEMAN control system was retired in 2005, with most previous BYE data moved into TALENT-KEYHOLE and special-handling material moved into RESERVE compartments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/FOIA/Authorized%20Classification%20and%20Control%20Markings%20Register%20V1.2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National Intelligence</span></span></span>

The public history of CORONA shows how names can become clearer only decades later. The NRO says the CORONA satellite programme and most of its imagery were declassified in 1995, and that its imagery helped dispel early-1960s fears of a Soviet “missile gap”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nro.gov/About-NRO/history/history-corona/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nro.gov">[nro.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nro.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">history corona</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NRO &gt; About NRO &gt; history &gt; history-corona...</span></span></span> Before declassification, labels and compartments helped separate people who needed to build, launch, recover, analyse or use the intelligence from those who merely held a clearance.

That is the central lesson: a SAP name is evidence of a controlled channel, not a reliable explanation of what is inside it.

## What the Name Tells You—and What It Does Not

A SAP nickname, codeword or identifier can tell a trained reader that special access controls apply. It can also reveal that a document belongs to a particular handling channel, that multiple SAPs are involved, that a dissemination restriction applies, or that the programme has waived-reporting handling. Official marking rules are explicit about those functions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507 V4p</span></span></span>

But the name usually does not tell the public what the programme is. It does not prove whether a programme is acknowledged, unacknowledged or waived unless the marking or surrounding authorised context says so. It does not show who has been [read in]({{ 'read-in/' | relative_url }}). It does not show the oversight record. It does not tell whether the programme is active, cancelled, renamed, merged, declassified or moved into another control system.

That limitation is not accidental. SAP names exist because secrecy has to be administered without becoming self-defeating. People must be able to label, file, brief, audit and move protected information, but the label cannot become a public summary of the secret. In that narrow space, nicknames and codewords do their real work: they make compartmented access legible to insiders while keeping outsiders from learning more than the label itself permits.
