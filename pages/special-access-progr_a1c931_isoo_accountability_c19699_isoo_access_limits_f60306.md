## Introduction

The most sensitive Special Access Programs (SAPs) create a difficult governance problem: if knowledge of a programme is deliberately restricted to an exceptionally small number of people, can independent oversight still function? Under the United States classification system, the answer is intended to be yes—but only through a carefully qualified exception. Executive Order 13526 requires that the Director of the Information Security Oversight Office ([ISOO]({{ 'isoo/' | relative_url }})) be given access to SAPs so that government-wide oversight remains possible. At the same time, the order recognises that extraordinarily sensitive programmes may require even tighter restrictions, allowing access to be limited to the Director alone or, if the President approves, to the Director and no more than one employee. This is not a removal of oversight but a narrowing of who may exercise it, reflecting the tension between protecting exceptionally vulnerable information and preserving meaningful [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Can one person oversee a secret programme?

The governing rule appears in Section 4.3 of Executive Order 13526. As a general principle, SAPs remain subject to ISOO oversight, and the ISOO Director must be afforded access to perform the office's statutory responsibilities. However, the same section permits an exception for programmes of extraordinary sensitivity. In those cases, access may be restricted to the ISOO Director alone. If the President specifically approves, access may instead be limited to the Director and one designated ISOO employee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

This provision is striking because it acknowledges that some [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) are considered so sensitive that expanding access even within the government's own oversight office is viewed as a security risk. Rather than exempting such programmes from oversight altogether, the executive order compresses the oversight channel to its smallest possible size.

The result is a model in which institutional oversight survives through a single cleared office-holder rather than through a larger inspection team. That differs from most regulatory systems, where resilience depends on multiple reviewers, peer review, and organisational continuity.

## How the executive order narrows access

The narrowing mechanism is intentionally limited. The executive order does not state that agencies may simply deny [ISOO access]({{ 'isoo-access/' | relative_url }}) because a programme is highly classified. Instead, it establishes a default rule of ISOO access while creating a narrowly defined exception for programmes whose sensitivity is judged exceptional.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Several features distinguish this exception:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **The exception is tied to extraordinary sensitivity**, not to SAPs generally.
* **The Director retains access**, preserving at least one independent oversight channel.
* **Adding a second ISOO employee requires presidential approval**, indicating that even minimal expansion beyond the Director is treated as an exceptional decision.
* **The provision concerns who may inspect**, not whether the programme remains part of the executive branch classification system.

</div>

This design reflects an attempt to balance two competing objectives: preventing unnecessary dissemination of exceptionally sensitive information while avoiding the creation of programmes entirely insulated from government-wide review.

## Why extreme sensitivity changes oversight

The rationale behind the exception follows the broader philosophy of SAPs. Executive Order 13526 states that SAPs themselves should exist only when ordinary classified access controls are insufficient and should be kept to an absolute minimum. Some programmes may involve exceptionally vulnerable [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) sources, highly sensitive military capabilities, advanced technologies, or information whose compromise could have exceptionally grave consequences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

In such circumstances, every additional person with knowledge represents another potential security risk. Restricting oversight personnel therefore becomes an extension of the same compartmentation logic that justified creating the SAP in the first place.

Supporters of this approach argue that reducing the number of individuals exposed to exceptionally sensitive information lowers the likelihood of accidental disclosure, insider compromise, or operational exposure. From this perspective, allowing the ISOO Director to review the programme without involving a broader inspection staff preserves accountability while minimising additional risk.

## What accountability remains when access shrinks?

Although oversight becomes highly concentrated, it does not disappear.

The ISOO Director continues to exercise responsibilities assigned under Executive Order 13526, including issuing implementing directives, overseeing the executive branch classification system, reviewing agency implementation, and receiving required reporting from agencies. Those government-wide responsibilities remain in place even where direct inspection access has been narrowed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 13526August 15, 2016 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifyin...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Equally important, ISOO is only one accountability mechanism. Exceptionally sensitive SAPs may also remain subject to other oversight structures operating under different legal authorities, such as departmental leadership, inspectors general, or congressionally mandated reporting requirements applicable to certain categories of SAPs. Those mechanisms address different questions from ISOO's classification oversight and are not displaced simply because ISOO access is restricted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41528.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportClassified Information Policy and Executive Order 1352610 Dec 2010 — This report provides information on classified infor...</span></span></span>

However, shrinking oversight to one individual inevitably changes its character. Practical challenges include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Reduced institutional redundancy.** Independent verification by multiple reviewers becomes difficult.
* **Continuity risk.** Oversight knowledge may become concentrated in a single office-holder.
* **Limited peer challenge.** Internal debate within the oversight body is naturally constrained.
* **Public transparency remains impossible.** Any assessment occurs entirely inside classified channels.

</div>

These are governance trade-offs rather than legal anomalies. The executive order accepts them because it treats protection of extraordinarily sensitive information as outweighing the benefits of broader internal review in a very small category of cases.

## The central criticism: does oversight remain meaningful?

The principal criticism is not that ISOO lacks legal authority, but that oversight concentrated in one person may become difficult to test independently.

Most accountability systems rely on multiple reviewers, documentation, institutional memory, and opportunities for [internal challenge]({{ 'internal-challenge/' | relative_url }}). A one-person oversight model inevitably provides fewer opportunities for corroboration or independent reassessment. Critics therefore question whether oversight can remain fully effective when almost no one is permitted to know enough about a programme to evaluate it.

Defenders respond that the alternative would be to remove oversight entirely from the most sensitive programmes. The executive order instead preserves an authorised oversight path, even if that path is intentionally narrow.
