## Introduction

[Small access lists]({{ 'access-lists/' | relative_url }}) are intended to protect exceptionally sensitive programmes, not to isolate them from lawful oversight. Yet one of the recurring organisational risks in special-access programmes (SAPs), including unacknowledged and waived programmes, is that highly capable technical teams can become less effective at recognising budgetary and legal problems than engineering problems. This is not usually because engineers disregard compliance. Rather, the compartment itself narrows who participates in routine decision-making, making it easier for shared technical assumptions to crowd out financial, contracting and legal perspectives. Executive Order 13526 explicitly anticipates this tension by requiring SAPs to remain subject to [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) systems and oversight even while limiting access to a relatively small number of people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifying, safeguarding, a...</span></span></span>

Within the broader issue of insider [blind spots]({{ 'blind-spots/' | relative_url }}), the important question is therefore not whether technical specialists are competent, but whether the structure surrounding them allows risks visible only from other professional disciplines to reach the people making technical decisions.

## How shared assumptions form

Engineering organisations naturally optimise for technical performance. In highly compartmented programmes, this tendency becomes stronger because most daily interactions occur among people with similar training, security access and programme objectives.

Over time, several reinforcing mechanisms appear:

* **Common definitions of success.** Schedule [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }}), prototype performance and technical breakthroughs become the dominant measures of progress, while legal review or budget compliance may be viewed primarily as administrative hurdles rather than independent indicators of programme health.
* **Reduced external challenge.** Personnel outside the compartment often cannot ask detailed questions because they lack sufficient programme knowledge. Meanwhile, personnel inside the compartment may assume that if no informed objections are being raised, the programme is proceeding appropriately.
* **Normalisation of exceptional procedures.** Because SAPs legitimately require unusual security practices, participants may gradually treat other unusual practices—such as highly restricted financial discussions or limited legal consultation—as equally routine, even when those issues require broader institutional scrutiny.

These are well-known organisational dynamics rather than evidence of misconduct. Research on high-reliability organisations and accident investigations consistently shows that homogeneous expert groups become vulnerable to "groupthink" and confirmation bias when independent perspectives are systematically reduced.

## Why budget warnings are easily overlooked

Budget professionals view programmes differently from engineers.

A technical team generally asks whether a capability can be built. Budget officials ask whether resources are being used consistently with authorisations, appropriations and long-term affordability. Those questions often depend on information extending beyond the technical compartment.

In tightly compartmented programmes, engineers may not fully appreciate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* how funding decisions affect other classified programmes competing for the same resources;
* whether cost growth is becoming strategically significant rather than merely technically understandable;
* whether repeated schedule slips create reporting or reprogramming obligations;
* whether procurement strategies introduce oversight concerns beyond engineering performance.

</div>

Executive Order 13526 requires agencies establishing SAPs to maintain accounting systems and subjects SAPs to formal oversight, reflecting recognition that extraordinary secrecy cannot replace financial governance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/es/sla/ddi/docs/EUA4%20Executive%20Order%2013526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">anization of American StatesExecutive Order 13526- Classified National Security...29 Dec 2009 — (1) Special access programs shall be...</span></span></span>

## Why legal risks emerge later than technical risks

Legal questions frequently develop more slowly than engineering questions.

An engineer may immediately recognise a design flaw because it prevents the system from functioning. A legal issue may remain invisible until contract modifications, reporting obligations, statutory authorities or oversight reviews require someone to examine the broader administrative framework.

Inside highly restricted programmes, legal advisers may encounter several structural difficulties:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* They may only receive access after major technical decisions have already been made.
* Programme staff may assume existing authorities cover new activities without revisiting the legal basis.
* Lawyers with appropriate clearances may still lack enough operational context to identify emerging issues unless technical staff actively involve them.

</div>

This does not imply that SAPs routinely bypass legal review. Rather, it illustrates how sequencing matters. Legal advice is most valuable when integrated throughout development instead of appearing only during formal approval stages.

## Risks that only other functions can see

Many programme risks become visible only when different professional communities compare their perspectives.

**Engineers** detect design limitations, integration failures and performance problems.

**Budget specialists** notice unsustainable cost growth, funding mismatches and reporting obligations.

**Contracting officials** recognise procurement irregularities, competition issues and contract structure risks.

**Security officers** identify unnecessary exposure of sensitive information or weaknesses in access controls.

**Government lawyers** evaluate statutory authority, executive requirements, fiscal law and oversight obligations.

Each discipline sees only part of the picture. Small [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) deliberately restrict information sharing, but the same restriction can prevent these perspectives from intersecting at the point where important warnings would otherwise emerge.

This is one reason why Executive Order 13526 requires SAPs to remain subject to oversight despite their exceptional access restrictions. The governing framework assumes secrecy and accountability must operate together rather than as alternatives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/es/sla/ddi/docs/EUA4%20Executive%20Order%2013526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">anization of American StatesExecutive Order 13526- Classified National Security...29 Dec 2009 — (1) Special access programs shall be...</span></span></span>

## The difference between secrecy and organisational isolation

A common misunderstanding is that reducing access automatically improves security.

Operationally, limiting access reduces opportunities for unauthorised disclosure. Organisationally, however, excessive isolation can reduce opportunities for informed challenge.

The distinction matters because effective oversight does not require unrestricted disclosure. Instead, it requires carefully selected individuals from different functions—legal, financial, acquisition, inspection and security—to possess enough authorised knowledge to identify risks invisible from within the engineering compartment alone.

Historical reforms to classification policy and congressional oversight have generally sought to preserve this balance by limiting SAP access while simultaneously preserving institutional mechanisms for accounting, inspection and legally mandated reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R41528.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[EveryCRSReport]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Classified Information Policy and Executive Order 1352610 Dec 2010 — This report provides information on classified informa...</span></span></span>

## Adding challenge without broadening exposure

The practical objective is not to widen access indiscriminately but to introduce independent scrutiny while preserving the programme's security.

Approaches commonly reflected in government oversight frameworks include:

* **Early cross-functional participation.** [Read in]({{ 'read-in/' | relative_url }}) essential legal, contracting and financial personnel before major programme commitments become difficult to reverse.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Independent review authorities.** Use inspectors general, designated oversight officials and authorised auditors who possess appropriate clearances but remain organisationally separate from programme management.
* **Structured decision reviews.** Require major technical milestones to include explicit consideration of fiscal, statutory and acquisition implications rather than engineering readiness alone.
* **Need-to-know by function rather than hierarchy.** Access decisions should reflect the necessity of performing oversight responsibilities, not merely operational involvement.

</div>

Inspection guidance within the Department of Defense similarly treats acquisition, budget, legal compliance, operations and security as interconnected oversight areas rather than isolated functions, reinforcing the expectation that effective governance depends upon integrating these perspectives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/saf_ig/publication/dafi90-302/dafi90-302.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 15, 2026 — 15 Mar 2023 — assess MGAs and specific oversight areas such as program acquisition functions, contract oversight, budget...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2026</span></span></span>

## The central blind spot

The principal danger is rarely that engineers ignore law or budgets intentionally. Instead, small, technically focused compartments can create environments where everyone present is highly competent yet collectively unable to perceive risks lying outside their shared expertise.

Within special-access programmes, the strongest protection against this form of blind spot is not broader disclosure to the general workforce. It is ensuring that a carefully authorised set of professionals from legal, financial, acquisition and oversight communities participates early enough to challenge assumptions before technical momentum turns manageable governance questions into difficult institutional problems.
