## Introduction

10 U.S.C. §119 is the central public statute governing congressional oversight of Department of Defense special access programmes, or SAPs. Its practical effect is simple but important: DoD SAP secrecy does not remove the programme from [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }})’s view. The law requires structured reporting on SAP [budgets]({{ 'budgets/' | relative_url }}), status, new designations and declassification-related changes, while allowing the Secretary of Defense to withhold particular information only on a case-by-case national-security basis and only from the ordinary report, not from the senior congressional leaders specified in the statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

That balance is why §119 matters in the narrower world of waived and [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}). An [unacknowledged SAP]({{ 'unacknowledged-7fdfef/' | relative_url }}) may keep even its existence classified outside authorised channels. A waived SAP goes further by narrowing the standard congressional reporting route. But the statutory design is not “no oversight”; it is controlled oversight, routed through named defence committees and, for waived information, their chair and ranking minority members.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

## What §119 makes DoD report each year

The recurring reporting duty is built around the defence budget cycle. Section 119 says that, not later than 1 March each year, the Secretary of Defense is to submit a report to the defence committees on special access programmes. The report must state the total amount requested for DoD SAPs in the President’s budget for the next fiscal year, and it must provide programme-level information for each SAP in that budget.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The programme-level items are deliberately budget-and-management focused rather than operationally expansive. The statute calls for:

* a brief description of each programme;
* a brief discussion of major [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }});

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* actual costs for previous fiscal years during which the programme has been conducted;
* estimated total cost;
* estimated costs for the current fiscal year, the budget year and the next four fiscal years in which the programme is expected to continue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

</div>

That list shows the governance purpose of the annual report. It is not designed to make Congress a public clearing house for classified technical details. It is designed to let the relevant committees see whether a SAP exists, what it is broadly for, what it is expected to cost, how far along it is, and whether its future funding profile is changing.

A DoD training guide from the Center for Development of Security Excellence summarises the same rule for security personnel: existing SAPs are reported to Congress by 1 March, with information including budget estimates, programme description, milestones and actual costs. The same guide says new or proposed SAPs are handled through a separate SAP listing due by 1 February.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SA001 Student Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SA001 Student Guide</span></span></span>

## The annual report is also an internal revalidation tool

The statutory report is only one layer of the process. Inside DoD, annual SAP reporting has long doubled as a revalidation exercise: a chance to ask whether a programme should remain under SAP controls at all. Older DoD security-regulation material described the annual reports as the vehicle for revalidation and approval for continuation of all DoD SAPs by the Deputy Secretary of Defense; a SAP not approved for continuation was to be terminated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://irp.fas.org/doddir/dod/5200-1r/chapter_8.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irp.fas.org">[Intelligence Resource Program]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Intelligence Resource Program Chapter 8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Intelligence Resource Program Chapter 8</span></span></span>

Current DoD policy uses a more elaborate governance vocabulary, but the same basic idea remains. The 2024 DoD Instruction 5205.11 requires officials responsible for SAPs to conduct annual reviews of all DoD SAPs concerning the continued need for SAP security protection, and to provide recommendations through the Senior Review Group for Special Access Program Oversight Committee approval.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The 2024 instruction also assigns the Special Access Program Oversight Committee, or SAPOC, an annual review role. That review assesses whether critical programme information still needs SAP controls, whether SAP-protected capabilities are ready for broader warfighting apportionment, whether sharing with interagency partners or foreign governments is appropriate, whether a SAP can be disestablished, and whether waived SAPs still need waived status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

This matters because SAPs can otherwise become organisationally sticky. Once a programme is compartmented, [access lists]({{ 'access-lists/' | relative_url }}), special facilities, classification guides and contractor arrangements may all create inertia. The annual report-and-review cycle gives DoD and Congress a recurring checkpoint: not just “is this secret?” but “does this still need the special-access wrapper?”

## New SAPs face an earlier reporting gate

Section 119 separates existing-programme reporting from new-programme notification. Not later than 1 February each year, the Secretary of Defense must submit a report on each new SAP, giving notice of the designation and a justification for why the programme is being made a SAP. The report must also include the current estimate of total programme cost and identify existing programmes or technologies with similar technology or mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

That last requirement is easy to overlook, but it is one of the statute’s sharper anti-duplication tools. Congress is not merely told “a new compartment exists”. It is supposed to receive enough information to ask whether DoD is creating another protected channel for work that overlaps with something already funded or fielded.

The legal definition of a “new special access program” is also practical: it means a SAP that has not previously been covered in a notice and justification under this subsection. That avoids a loophole in which a programme could be treated as old for internal purposes while never having received the statutory new-programme explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The statute then adds a start-up brake. A SAP may not be initiated until the defence committees are notified and 30 days have passed after they receive that notification. This is not a public-comment period; it is a congressional oversight interval. But in a compartmented environment, even that interval is significant because it prevents an ordinary SAP from being fully launched before Congress has received formal notice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

## Budget and status information are the heart of the report

The distinctive feature of §119 is that it does not try to solve SAP oversight by demanding operational transparency. Instead, it concentrates on budget, milestones and lifecycle status. This is a realistic governance choice: the most sensitive programme details may remain tightly controlled, but money, schedule and continuation status still create oversight handles.

For Congress, the annual SAP report can support several questions:

**Is the programme growing?** Actual prior-year costs and future estimates make it possible to see whether a SAP is expanding, contracting or becoming more expensive than expected.

**Is the programme still on track?** Milestone reporting gives committees a way to compare progress against stated objectives, even if technical details are classified.

**Is the programme duplicative?** The new-SAP report’s requirement to identify similar technologies or missions helps committees detect whether compartmentation is hiding overlap.

**Should the SAP status continue?** Annual internal review and congressional reporting create a repeated opportunity to ask whether the programme still needs special access controls, rather than ordinary classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The same budget logic appears in §119’s special rule for multiyear budgeting. If the President’s budget for a year does not include a full DoD budget request because of multiyear budgeting, the report must still set out the amount already appropriated for SAPs for the next fiscal year, any additional amount requested, and the same programme-level information otherwise required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

That provision prevents the reporting duty from disappearing just because the budget format changes. In other words, the statute follows the money even when the annual budget presentation is not a full ordinary request.

## Classification changes and public disclosure trigger separate notice

Section 119 also covers moments when the secrecy posture of a SAP changes. Whenever DoD plans to change the classification of a SAP, or plans to declassify and make public classified information about a SAP, the Secretary must submit a report to the defence committees describing the proposed change, explaining the reasons, and giving notice of any planned public announcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

The ordinary timing rule is at least 14 days before the change or public announcement. If exceptional circumstances make that impossible, the Secretary may submit the report any time before the change or announcement, but must explain the exceptional circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

This matters for acknowledged and unacknowledged SAPs in different ways. For an acknowledged SAP, a public disclosure may involve technical or budgetary details rather than the fact of existence. For an unacknowledged SAP, any movement towards acknowledgement or public discussion can itself be a major classification event. Section 119 gives Congress advance notice before DoD changes the public boundary around a programme.

The statute also requires prompt notification if DoD modifies or terminates the policy and criteria used to designate programmes as SAPs. That provision is broader than a single programme. It gives Congress visibility into changes in the rules by which DoD decides what enters the SAP system in the first place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

## Waivers narrow the channel, not the oversight premise

The most sensitive part of §119 is subsection (e), the waiver authority. It allows the Secretary of Defense to waive a requirement under the annual report, new-programme report or classification-change report that certain information be included, if the Secretary determines that including the information in the report would adversely affect national security. The waiver must be made case by case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

Two limits are crucial.

First, the waiver is not a blanket exemption for a category of programmes. The statute says “certain information” may be withheld from a report, and that the waiver must be case by case. That is narrower than saying the Secretary can remove an entire class of SAPs from congressional oversight.

Second, the information is not simply buried. If the Secretary uses the waiver authority, the Secretary must provide the information about the SAP, along with the justification for the waiver, jointly to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

That is the legal basis for the common description of “waived SAPs” as programmes with more restricted congressional reporting. The Federation of American Scientists has described waived SAPs as the most sensitive DoD SAPs, with reporting limited to eight senior members of the congressional defence committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/dod_saps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Spotlight on Do D Special Access Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American ScientistsSpotlight on DoD Special Access Programs - Federation of American Scientists...</span></span></span>

DoD policy reflects that limited-channel model. A 2024 DoD directive assigns the Director of DoD SAPCO responsibility for supporting SAP congressional meetings, hearings and responses to congressional inquiries, coordinating annual SAP reviews and reports, accrediting congressional SAP facilities, and maintaining records of SAP information provided to Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

## Who receives the reports

Section 119 defines “defence committees” specifically. They are the Senate Armed Services Committee, the Senate Appropriations Committee and its Defence Subcommittee, the House Armed Services Committee, and the House Appropriations Committee and its Defence Subcommittee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

That definition is important because SAP oversight is not routed to all members of Congress or all committees with an interest in defence. It is channelled to the committees that authorise and appropriate defence activity. Those committees are the places where classified oversight, budget control and defence policy most directly intersect.

For waived information, the channel narrows further to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee. That is why waived SAPs sit at the most sensitive end of the public SAP framework: Congress is still notified, but not through the ordinary committee-wide process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

## What the statute does not make public

A recurring misunderstanding is that a statutory report to Congress is the same thing as public disclosure. It is not. Section 119 creates reporting duties inside the classified oversight system. It does not require DoD to publish a list of SAPs, disclose SAP budgets to the public, or confirm the existence of unacknowledged programmes outside authorised channels.

That distinction is central to unacknowledged and waived SAPs. An unacknowledged SAP may be reported to Congress while remaining publicly unconfirmed. A waived SAP may be reported through an even narrower congressional route while still remaining under statutory oversight.

The statute therefore creates a layered [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) model rather than a transparency model. Public readers can know the reporting architecture: deadlines, committees, required categories of information and waiver limits. They generally cannot know the classified contents of the annual SAP reports, the identities of unacknowledged SAPs, or the details of waived programmes.

## Why §119 still matters for SAP governance

The strongest public evidence for §119’s continuing importance is that DoD’s current SAP governance documents still build around annual review, reporting coordination and controlled congressional access. DoD Directive 5205.07 says all DoD SAPs must be assigned to oversight authorities, places the Director of DoD SAPCO directly under the Deputy Secretary of Defense for day-to-day SAP Enterprise management, and makes that office the main point of contact for SAP matters with the legislative branch unless another law or regulation says otherwise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

DoD Instruction 5205.11 then turns that structure into a review process. Oversight authorities must ensure SAP work stays within approved scope, avoid duplication or overlap, and provide input to SAP reports to Congress. The SAPOC’s annual review includes not only whether a SAP still needs protection, but whether waived SAPs still need waived status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

This is the core governance intervention behind §119. It does not eliminate secrecy, and it does not settle every controversy over classified programmes. But it gives SAP secrecy a recurring statutory rhythm: annual reporting, new-programme notice, cost and milestone data, classification-change notice, committee-defined recipients, and a narrow waiver pathway that still sends the information and justification to specified congressional leaders.
