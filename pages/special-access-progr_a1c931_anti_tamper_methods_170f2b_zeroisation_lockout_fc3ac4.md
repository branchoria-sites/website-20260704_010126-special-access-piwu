## Introduction

Zeroisation and lockout are [anti-tamper]({{ 'anti-tamper/' | relative_url }}) responses designed to ensure that, if a sensitive device is captured or opened without authorisation, the most valuable information becomes unavailable rather than merely hidden. Instead of attempting to make hardware indestructible, these mechanisms focus on protecting the critical programme information inside it: cryptographic keys, firmware secrets, calibration data, authentication credentials and other information that would enable reverse engineering or unauthorised operation. This approach is particularly relevant to systems associated with highly classified defence programmes, including special-access programmes (SAPs), where preventing the compromise of embedded knowledge can be more important than preserving the equipment itself.

Unlike dramatic fictional portrayals of self-destructing hardware, real anti-tamper systems usually aim for controlled responses. They may erase sensitive memory, invalidate encryption keys, permanently disable secure functions, or refuse to boot until authorised maintenance procedures are completed. These responses reduce the [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) value of captured equipment while avoiding unnecessary destruction of the entire platform.

## What zeroisation protects

Zeroisation (also spelled zeroization) is the deliberate erasure of sensitive information stored inside electronic systems. In modern secure devices, the information being erased is usually cryptographic rather than operational. Once the encryption keys protecting classified software or mission data have been destroyed, the remaining data often becomes computationally useless.

Typical targets for zeroisation include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Encryption and authentication keys.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.digi.com/blog/post/the-fips-140-3-standard-and-use-cases" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: digi.com">[digi.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">digi.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FIPS 140-3 Encryption: Standards and Use CasesStrong physical tamper resistance; Automatic zeroization of cryptographic keys upon tamperi...</span></span></span>
* Secure boot credentials.
* Firmware signing certificates.
* Mission-specific configuration data.
* Calibration values that reveal classified performance.
* Passwords or other critical security parameters.

</div>

This distinction matters because the hardware itself may remain physically intact while the information that gives it intelligence value disappears. An adversary may still examine the circuit boards, packaging and components, but encrypted storage without its associated keys often reveals little about the protected capability.

The concept is embedded in widely recognised security standards rather than existing solely within military programmes. NIST's FIPS 140 family of cryptographic standards requires higher-security cryptographic modules to incorporate tamper detection and response mechanisms capable of zeroising critical security parameters when physical compromise is detected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Presentations/FIPS-140-3-Section-5-Physical-Security/images-media/physecpre18.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.gov">[NIST Computer Security Resource Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page 13. ## How zeroisation is triggered

Zeroisation is normally designed as an automatic response rather than relying on an operator making a decision under pressure.

Common trigger mechanisms include:<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://csrc.nist.rip/publications/fips/fips140-3/fips1403Draft.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.rip">[csrc.nist.rip]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.rip</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NIST Computer Security Resource CenterFIPS 140-2by F PUB · Cited by 17 — The physical security mechanisms may include the use of strong e...</span></span></span>


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* Detection that an enclosure has been opened.
* Removal of protective covers or seals.
* Electrical characteristics indicating drilling, probing or cutting.
* Unexpected voltage, temperature or clock conditions suggesting fault-injection attacks.
* Authentication failures during secure maintenance.
* Explicit commands from authorised operators when equipment is at risk of capture.

</div>

Exactly which sensors are used is generally protected information in military systems. Public documentation instead describes the principle: a secure device monitors for conditions associated with physical compromise and, if thresholds are met, erases designated secrets before an attacker can extract them.

Modern secure cryptographic modules frequently combine several sensors so that no single bypass defeats the protection. FIPS 140-3 guidance similarly describes tamper-response mechanisms whose purpose is immediate zeroisation of sensitive security parameters after detection of physical attack.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://csrc.nist.gov/CSRC/media/Presentations/FIPS-140-3-Section-5-Physical-Security/images-media/physecpre18.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csrc.nist.gov">[NIST Computer Security Resource Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csrc.nist.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Page 13. ## Lockout, degraded mode and refusal to boot

Erasing secrets is only one possible response. Many systems instead—or additionally—enter a state in which they deliberately refuse normal operation.

Lockout mechanisms can take several forms:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Permanent refusal to boot secure firmware.
* Disabling classified operating modes while allowing limited diagnostics.
* Rejecting future authentication attempts.
* Invalidating digital certificates needed for trusted operation.
* Requiring factory or depot-level recovery procedures.
* Limiting functionality to an unclassified maintenance mode.

</div>

The objective is to deny an adversary repeated opportunities to analyse a functioning system. If sensitive firmware can never execute after tampering, opportunities for dynamic analysis become much more limited.

Designers often distinguish between **fail-secure** and **fail-operational** behaviour. Equipment intended to protect especially sensitive information may prioritise secrecy over continued availability, accepting permanent disablement if compromise appears likely. Systems whose uninterrupted operation is essential may instead enter carefully controlled degraded modes that preserve safety while withholding protected capabilities.

For highly classified equipment, this balance is a significant engineering decision. Excessively aggressive responses risk destroying friendly capability through accidental activation, while responses that are too conservative may leave valuable information exposed.

## Why destroying secrets is often better than destroying hardware

At first glance, physically destroying a device might seem the strongest anti-tamper response. In practice, controlled zeroisation is often more effective.

Sensitive electronics increasingly rely on encrypted storage. If the encryption keys are irretrievably erased:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Stored firmware cannot be decrypted.
* Mission data becomes unreadable.
* Authentication material disappears.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://docs.amd.com/r/en-US/wp543-fips-140-3-primer/Physical-Security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.amd.com">[docs.amd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.amd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AMD DocumentationPhysical Security - WP54328 Aug 2024 — A tamper response should involve the immediate zeroization of all the unprotected...</span></span></span>
* Software updates can no longer be validated.
* Protected algorithms remain inaccessible even if memory chips are removed.

</div>

By contrast, physically destroying the electronics may still leave partially recoverable memory devices or provide clues about [construction]({{ 'construction/' | relative_url }}) techniques.

This reflects a broader security principle: protecting information is often more valuable than protecting hardware. A captured circuit board without usable cryptographic secrets may reveal manufacturing techniques but not necessarily the software logic or operational capabilities that those secrets protect.

## Limits and risks of destructive responses

Zeroisation is not a guarantee that every secret disappears.

Several practical limitations influence its effectiveness.

First, the response must occur before an attacker extracts the protected information. Sophisticated physical attacks may attempt to bypass sensors or isolate memory before the anti-tamper circuitry activates.

Second, not every type of information is equally erasable. Cryptographic keys stored in rewritable memory can usually be destroyed rapidly, but physical characteristics such as specialised integrated circuits, packaging methods or material compositions remain available for examination even after successful zeroisation.

Third, accidental activation carries operational costs. Sensors must distinguish genuine attacks from legitimate maintenance, transport shocks or environmental extremes. A false trigger could permanently disable expensive equipment or erase mission data unnecessarily.

Finally, zeroisation only protects information that designers identified in advance as requiring protection. Engineering decisions about what constitutes critical programme information therefore remain fundamental to effective anti-tamper planning.

## What zeroisation does—and does not—prevent

Successful zeroisation changes the intelligence value of captured equipment rather than making analysis impossible.

It can substantially reduce opportunities to recover:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Cryptographic secrets.
* Protected firmware.
* Secure boot information.
* Authentication credentials.
* Mission-specific operational data.

</div>

However, it cannot erase every observable characteristic. Analysts may still learn from:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* Mechanical construction.
* Packaging technologies.
* Component selection.
* Manufacturing quality.
* Thermal management.
* Circuit layout.
* Connector design.
* Electromagnetic characteristics.

</div>

For this reason, zeroisation is normally only one element within a broader anti-tamper strategy. It complements other protective measures by ensuring that, even if the physical device survives capture, the information most likely to reveal classified capabilities has either been destroyed or rendered cryptographically inaccessible.
