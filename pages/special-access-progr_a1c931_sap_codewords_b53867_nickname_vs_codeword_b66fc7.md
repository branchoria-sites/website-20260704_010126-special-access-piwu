## Introduction

In discussions of special access programmes (SAPs), people often use *nickname*, *codeword* and *codename* as though they mean the same thing. Official US policy does not. The distinction matters because a programme's label and the programme itself can have different classification status and different handling rules. A seemingly harmless two-word [title]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}) may be unclassified even though it identifies a highly restricted SAP, while a single-word codeword may itself carry classified meaning because the association between the word and the protected activity is sensitive. Understanding this distinction helps explain why official documents use precise terminology and why leaked programme names are often misunderstood.

## How official rules separate nicknames from codewords

The modern US Department of Defense (DoD) manages official names through the Code Word, Nicknames, and Exercise Terms ([NICKA]({{ 'nicka/' | relative_url }})) system overseen by the Joint Staff. Rather than treating all secret labels alike, NICKA assigns different categories with different purposes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Joint_Staff/16-F-1538_DOC_01_CJCSM_3150-29E.pdf?ver=2019-03-15-113927-773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1) The home page will display the following information: What is. NICKA, Definitions of a Nickname, Code Word, or Exercise;...Read more...</span></span></span>

The core distinction is straightforward:

* **Nickname:** normally a combination of unclassified words with an unclassified dictionary meaning. It is assigned as an official label for a programme, activity or operation without describing what it actually does.
* **Codeword:** normally a single word that has been assigned a classified meaning by authorised officials. The protected association between that word and the classified activity is itself part of the security system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/navy_nicknames_and_codeword_ma/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American ScientistsNavy Nicknames and Codeword ManagementThe U.S. Navy has issued updated instructions on the use of nickna...</span></span></span>

This difference is deliberate. A nickname is intended to give authorised personnel a recognisable administrative label while avoiding descriptive content. A codeword, by contrast, functions as a protected security term whose meaning cannot safely be discussed outside authorised channels. Joint Staff guidance therefore treats [codewords]({{ 'codewords/' | relative_url }}) more restrictively than nicknames, including limitations on discussing them over unclassified communications even after they become inactive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Joint_Staff/16-F-1538_DOC_01_CJCSM_3150-29E.pdf?ver=2019-03-15-113927-773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1) The home page will display the following information: What is. NICKA, Definitions of a Nickname, Code Word, or Exercise;...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why the label and the programme can have different classification

One of the most confusing aspects of SAP security is that the **name** and the **programme** do not necessarily share the same classification.

A SAP may involve highly classified technology or [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}) while carrying an officially assigned nickname composed entirely of ordinary English words. The nickname is simply the recognised handling label. It does not reveal the programme's technical purpose, sponsor or operational details by itself. Conversely, a codeword may itself require protection because revealing which classified activity it identifies would disclose sensitive information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">banner line. A hyphen (-) without interjected spaces shall be used to separate “SAR” and the program&#x27;s nickname or code word. The banner...</span></span></span>

This separation explains several otherwise puzzling practices:

* A programme nickname may appear in prescribed document markings without revealing operational content.
* Personnel may know that a nickname exists without being authorised to know the programme's substance.
* The same document can contain an unclassified-looking programme label while remaining classified because of everything else it contains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">banner line. A hyphen (-) without interjected spaces shall be used to separate “SAR” and the program&#x27;s nickname or code word. The banner...</span></span></span>

The distinction reflects a broader principle of compartmented security: access is controlled by authorised association, not by whether individual words sound secret.

## Why document markings treat nicknames and codewords differently

SAP marking rules illustrate the practical consequences of these definitions.

DoD marking guidance requires classified SAP documents to display the document classification, the caveat **SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED (SAR)**, followed by the programme's authorised nickname or codeword. The programme's abbreviated programme identifier (PID) is used for [portion markings]({{ 'portion-markings/' | relative_url }}) inside the document but is not substituted for the full nickname or codeword in the document banner.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">banner line. A hyphen (-) without interjected spaces shall be used to separate “SAR” and the program&#x27;s nickname or code word. The banner...</span></span></span>

For example, official guidance uses illustrative examples such as:

* **SAR–TAXI GREY** (nickname)
* **SAR–DAGGER** (codeword)

These examples demonstrate formatting rather than real operational significance. The marking tells authorised handlers which protected programme governs the material; it is not intended as a descriptive title.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">banner line. A hyphen (-) without interjected spaces shall be used to separate “SAR” and the program&#x27;s nickname or code word. The banner...</span></span></span>

This handling function is more important than the wording itself. The label routes information into the correct compartment, alerts security personnel to applicable restrictions, and helps prevent accidental disclosure across programme boundaries.

## Common mistakes in calling every secret label a "codename"

Popular books, films and news reports frequently describe every classified project name as a "codename". In ordinary English this causes little confusion, but it blurs distinctions that matter inside the US security system.

Common misconceptions include:

* **Assuming every secret programme name is a codeword.** Many SAPs instead use officially assigned nicknames consisting of ordinary words.
* **Assuming unusual words indicate higher secrecy.** The appearance of an unusual word does not by itself show whether it is a nickname, codeword or another administrative identifier.
* **Treating leaked names as descriptive clues.** Official naming conventions are specifically designed to avoid revealing programme content through the name alone.
* **Assuming the name's classification matches the programme's classification.** Official policy deliberately separates these questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/Joint_Staff/16-F-1538_DOC_01_CJCSM_3150-29E.pdf?ver=2019-03-15-113927-773" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">16 F 1538 DOC 01 CJCSM 3150 29E</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1) The home page will display the following information: What is. NICKA, Definitions of a Nickname, Code Word, or Exercise;...Read more...</span></span></span>

Another source of confusion is historical usage. Earlier reporting, memoirs and even some government publications sometimes use "codename" informally to describe almost any protected project label. Modern administrative guidance, however, preserves the technical distinction because different categories carry different handling requirements.

## Why the distinction matters for special-access programmes

Within SAP administration, the difference between a nickname and a codeword is not merely semantic. It affects how information is marked, transmitted and protected.

A nickname primarily supports programme identification and administration while remaining suitable for controlled use as part of required document markings. A codeword adds another layer of protection because its classified association must itself be safeguarded. The result is a security system in which access decisions depend not only on a document's overall classification but also on precisely which authorised programme label governs it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507-V4p.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507 V4p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">banner line. A hyphen (-) without interjected spaces shall be used to separate “SAR” and the program&#x27;s nickname or code word. The banner...</span></span></span>

Understanding this distinction also explains why discovering a programme name in a historical document or public leak rarely reveals much by itself. In the SAP system, the significance lies not in whether the words sound mysterious, but in the controlled relationship between the authorised label, the protected information and the people cleared to understand that relationship.
