## Introduction

Contractors are not peripheral to special-access-programs (SAPs). In many [acquisition SAPs]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}), they are where much of the design, manufacturing, software, test support, facilities work and security administration actually happens. The point of the SAP wall is not simply to hide a whole programme from the public; it is to divide access so that a cleared company, a subcontractor, a plant, a laboratory or a specialist team receives only the programme information needed for its task. That compartmented model can protect fragile military advantages, especially in stealth, space, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) and advanced weapons work. It can also raise costs, duplicate inspections, reduce competition and make oversight harder when contract data, facilities and personnel are deliberately kept outside ordinary visibility. Official policy treats this as a managed industrial-security problem, not an informal secrecy privilege: SAP contractors still need contractual authority, facility and personnel eligibility, programme access approval, tailored security guidance and government oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/forms/dd/dd0254-Inst.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

## How cleared industry gets through the SAP wall

A contractor’s ordinary security clearance is only the starting point. Under the National Industrial Security Program, a company may need a facility security clearance, cleared employees, approved safeguarding arrangements and a classified contract or subcontract before it can receive classified information. A SAP then adds a narrower programme gate on top: access is tied to the specific programme, the person’s role, the facility’s approval and the contract language that authorises the work. The federal NISPOM rule defines a SAP as a programme created to control access and distribution, and to protect particularly sensitive classified information beyond the normal requirements for Confidential, Secret or Top Secret information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-32/subtitle-A/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-117" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ecfr.gov">[eCFR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ecfr.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">32 CFR Part 117 -- National Industrial Security Program...SAP means any program that is established to control access and distributi...</span></span></span>

For contractors, the central document is usually the [DD Form 254]({{ 'dd-form-254/' | relative_url }}), the Contract Security Classification Specification. DoD instructions describe it as the principal authorised means for giving a contractor classification guidance and security requirements for a contract or agreement requiring access to classified information. It is not a ceremonial form: it tells the contractor what clearance level is required, what information may be accessed, what safeguarding rules apply, what performance activities involve classified information, and which government or security office is responsible for oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/forms/dd/dd0254-Inst.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

That same mechanism extends down the supply chain. CDSE training material explains that when a prime contractor uses a subcontractor for classified work, the prime must issue a DD Form 254 to the cleared subcontractor conveying only the security classification requirements applicable to that subcontractor’s performance. This is the practical heart of compartmented industry: the prime may know the broader system integration problem, while a subcontractor may only see a component, test method, material, software interface or manufacturing tolerance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/IS128-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdse.edu.</span></span></span>

In SAP contracting, the DD Form 254 is also a control against over-sharing. The current DoD SAP Security Manual says the form should be tailored to the support being sought, and it requires explicit security guidance for contract execution. It also directs programme security officials to notify higher SAP security channels if a prime contractor imposes security requirements on a subcontractor that exceed the authorised SAP security framework. That rule matters because compartmentation can otherwise drift into contractor-made secrecy: extra barriers may look prudent locally but still create unnecessary cost, delay or confusion across the industrial base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

## Compartmented contracts split work without giving everyone the whole picture

SAP contracting often works like a deliberately incomplete map. One company may hold the integration contract; another may make a sensor, coating, propulsion element, software module, enclosure, antenna, test rig or secure facility system. Even inside one large defence company, access can be divided by programme area, building, vault, network, laboratory, team and individual billet. People may be highly cleared and still be unable to discuss adjacent work unless they are read into the same compartment.

The logic is strongest in acquisition SAPs, where the government is trying to protect a capability before adversaries understand it. CDSE’s public SAP overview notes that earlier “black programmes” were largely used to protect DoD acquisition programmes and gives Skunk Works and the F-117A [stealth]({{ 'stealth/' | relative_url }}) fighter as a well-known example. That example is useful because stealth did not depend on one secret fact; it depended on a cluster of design, materials, modelling, manufacturing, testing and operational practices. Compartmenting the industrial work helped keep that cluster from becoming visible too early.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SA001 Student Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SA001 Student Guide</span></span></span>

The same pattern appears in later stealth-era procurement. Open accounts of the B-2 programme describe a security structure that included document control, background checks, physical access controls and plant-security measures, with subcontractors also required to follow the black programme’s standards. That is a concrete example of how SAP-like industrial controls become a production environment, not just a filing rule: people wait for access approvals, facilities are configured around programme security, and ordinary supplier relationships become more rigid because each outside company must be brought inside the wall only as far as its task requires.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2012/January%202012/0112bomber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span></span></span>

Compartmentation also shapes what can be competed. A solicitation may need to be unclassified if possible, but some bidders may require access before they can understand the technical requirement. DoD DD Form 254 guidance says that if classified access is required in the pre-award phase, a DD Form 254 is required with the solicitation. In a SAP, that means acquisition officials must balance two competing goals: reveal enough for serious competition, but not so much that the programme’s purpose, vulnerability or technical method becomes visible to a wider industrial audience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/IS128-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdse.edu.</span></span></span>

This is why SAP contracting can narrow the supplier pool even when no one is trying to exclude competitors. A company may be technically capable but lack an approved facility, cleared staff, a need-to-know path, cyber controls, storage authority or the time needed to process access. Smaller firms may find that the burden of [secure rooms]({{ 'secure-rooms/' | relative_url }}), classified IT, visitor control, record-keeping and inspections is disproportionate to their workshare. The result is a structural tilt towards incumbents and large primes that already possess cleared infrastructure, programme-security staff and experience operating under classified contract requirements.

## The paperwork is also the boundary of accountability

Compartmented industry depends on paperwork because the contract must define what each private actor is authorised to know, hold, generate, discuss and retain. The DD Form 254 instructions emphasise that the form is part of the contract and is the source of security guidance provided by the government. They also warn that classification guidance should be specific to the contract and should not simply copy general NISPOM requirements, because the NISPOM gives general safeguarding procedures rather than programme-specific classification guidance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/forms/dd/dd0254-Inst.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

That distinction is important. General industrial-security rules say how classified information must be protected; SAP guidance says what this programme’s sensitive information is, who may access it, how it may move, where it may be stored and what must happen when work ends. In a compartmented supply chain, vague guidance can create two opposite failures. If it is too broad, subcontractors may receive more information than they need. If it is too narrow or unclear, they may mishandle derivative classified information or be unable to perform the work efficiently.

Close-out rules show how seriously the system treats contractor possession. The 2025 DoD SAP Security Manual says contractors may not retain SAP information at contract close-out unless specifically authorised in writing by the government contracting activity. It also requires inventories, disposition actions and termination plans covering residual hardware, software, documentation, SAP facilities and personnel accesses. In plain terms, leaving a SAP contract is itself a security event: the government has to decide what returns, what is destroyed, what can be retained and which access approvals are removed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The same manual allows SAP information to be used in contractor independent research and development only with specific written permission, with a government-contractor relationship document and a DD Form 254 applied to the effort. This matters because defence companies often develop technology between formal [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}). In ordinary commercial innovation, a company might reuse know-how fluidly. Inside a SAP wall, even internally funded research can require formal permission if SAP information is involved, because the government is trying to prevent sensitive programme knowledge from leaking sideways into unauthorised corporate work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520507m1.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

## Why compartmented industry raises costs

SAP security is not cost-free. It can require separate facilities, access-control systems, secure storage, classified networks, visitor procedures, courier arrangements, personnel-security administration, programme-security officers, document-control staff, special training, inspections and sometimes duplicated versions of otherwise similar engineering environments. These costs may be justified for genuinely fragile capabilities, but they are real acquisition costs, not administrative background noise.

The B-2 is the clearest public example of how industrial secrecy can become expensive. An Air & Space Forces Magazine account, citing later estimates by programme participants, reported that security alone added roughly 10 to 20 per cent to the overall B-2 programme cost, broadly consistent with the F-117 experience. The same account described workers staying on the payroll while awaiting clearances and subcontractors having to meet the black programme’s tight standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airandspaceforces.com/PDF/MagazineArchive/Documents/2012/January%202012/0112bomber.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airandspaceforces.com">[Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airandspaceforces.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air &amp; Space Forces Magazine</span></span></span>

Security costs also appear through duplication. The 1994 Joint Security Commission report warned that contractors with multiple SAPs could be inspected programme by programme, with each project having its own requirements. It gave the example of a contractor with six SAPs facing six separate inspections, and reported contractor complaints that redundant re-inspections were time-consuming, costly, confusing and added little extra security. That finding remains one of the most useful public descriptions of the industrial burden created when each compartment becomes its own compliance world.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.governmentattic.org/32docs/RSrptJSC_1994.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: governmentattic.org">[Government Attic]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">governmentattic.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on governmentattic.org.</span></span></span>

There is a second kind of duplication: parallel development. When programmes cannot easily see each other, and contractors cannot easily compare related work across compartments, different teams may solve similar problems separately. Some duplication is intentional; it can protect alternatives or prevent a single compromise from revealing the whole capability. But excessive compartmentation can prevent reuse of lessons, tools, test data or design approaches. The reader should not imagine SAP contractors as one giant hidden company with perfect internal knowledge. In practice, the wall that protects a secret can also block efficiency.

## What oversight can and cannot see

SAP contracting creates a visibility problem for ordinary acquisition databases. GAO reported in 2012 that DoD SAPs use the national security exception and generally do not report data to the Federal Procurement Data System–Next Generation, the public-facing system used to track federal procurement. GAO added that determining the extent of SAP use of the exception was not feasible because of the limited access these programmes allow, although some officials reported aggregate competition and obligation data internally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-12-263.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO-12-263, DEFENSE CONTRACTING: Improved Policies and Tools Could Help Increase Competition on DOD&#x27;s National Security Exception Proc...</span></span></span>

That does not mean SAP contracts are ungoverned. It means the normal public trail is thinner. Oversight moves into narrower channels: programme offices, SAP central offices, inspectors general, classified congressional notifications, cleared auditors and security-review structures. The DoD Inspector General’s classified-complaint guidance, for example, explicitly lists breaches of classified systems involving SAPs among issues to be reported through secure means, and identifies acquisition, intelligence, and operations and support SAP categories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dodig.mil/Components/Administrative-Investigations/DoD-Hotline/Classified-Complaint/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dodig.mil">[dodig.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dodig.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Do D Hotline</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Do D Hotline</span></span></span>

The problem is that narrower oversight can become dependent on whether the right people have access at the right time. GAO’s work on industrial security has repeatedly shown that oversight quality depends on usable data, not just formal authority. In 2004, GAO found that the then-Defense Security Service could not provide adequate assurance that its oversight of contractor facilities reduced compromise risk because it did not analyse security-violation information in a way that identified systemic vulnerabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-04-332.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

More recent cyber concerns reinforce the point. GAO reported in 2026 that DoD relies on hundreds of thousands of private companies and that malicious cyber actors have targeted defence contractors’ networks and systems to access sensitive DoD data. SAP facilities may apply stronger controls than ordinary contractor environments, but the broader lesson still applies: industry is part of the attack surface. Compartmentation reduces how much one breach should reveal, but it does not eliminate the need for strong, measurable contractor-security performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107955/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[files.gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">External Factors That Could Impede Program Implementation...</span></span></span>

## The accountability trade-off is not secrecy versus openness

The realistic choice is not between total secrecy and total transparency. SAP contracting exists because some defence work would lose much of its value if adversaries learned the technical approach, supplier base, testing pattern, vulnerability or production timeline. A stealth aircraft, a specialised sensor, a space capability or a sensitive intelligence platform may require private industry expertise while still needing a tightly limited knowledge circle.

The real trade-off is between protective compartmentation and governable compartmentation. Protective compartmentation asks: who truly needs this information, at which facility, for which task, under which safeguards? Governable compartmentation asks a different question: can authorised officials still see enough to compare costs, prevent duplication, investigate violations, audit performance, test security, and stop primes or programme offices from creating unnecessary barriers?

A healthy SAP industrial structure has several features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Tailored access rather than blanket access.** Subcontractors receive only the classification guidance and programme material needed for their workshare, not the full programme story.
* **Contract-based security rules.** SAP requirements are written into the contract, DD Form 254 and programme guidance rather than passed informally through custom practice.
* **Clear government ownership of decisions.** Prime contractors help administer the wall, but the government remains responsible for authorising requirements, access, retention and close-out.
* **Data that can be reviewed inside classified channels.** Public procurement systems may omit SAP details, but cleared oversight bodies still need meaningful cost, competition, violation and performance data.
* **Resistance to unnecessary duplication.** Separate compartments may be essential, but repeated inspections, incompatible local rules and avoidable rework can weaken both security and value.

</div>

The central insight is that contractors make SAPs operational. Government can create the programme and control the access list, but industry often supplies the engineers, plants, test ranges, software teams, specialised materials and maintenance pathways. That makes contractor SAP access one of the most consequential parts of the whole special-access system: it is where secrecy becomes a workplace, a supply chain, a cost structure and an oversight challenge.
