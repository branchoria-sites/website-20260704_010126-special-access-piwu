## Introduction

Secrecy is not just a label on a document. In the Moynihan critique, it is a hidden system of government regulation: it controls who may know, who may ask, who may compare costs, and who may challenge official choices. That critique is especially sharp for special-access-programmes, including waived and [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}), because these programmes add extra access barriers on top of ordinary classification. The problem is not that all secrecy is illegitimate. The problem is that secrecy creates costs that are often invisible to the public, difficult for [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) to measure, and easy for agencies or programme managers to treat as someone else’s budgetary burden.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_moynihan_secrecy_cos_29a4b8-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Secrecy Costs" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The 1997 Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy, chaired by Senator Daniel Patrick Moynihan, put the central point plainly: secrecy should be reserved for what genuinely needs protection, because excessive secrecy can weaken oversight, distort policy judgement, waste money, and erode respect for the secrets that truly matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## Secrecy as government regulation

The Moynihan Commission’s most durable idea was that secrecy functions like regulation. It limits behaviour, imposes compliance duties, creates administrative structures, and shifts costs across government and industry. The difference is that ordinary regulation is visible enough to be debated, while secrecy regulation is often opaque: the public cannot easily know its full scope, its justification, or its accumulated cost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

That framing matters for SAPs because a special-access-programme does not merely say “this information is classified”. It creates a more restrictive access regime. Executive Order 13526 says SAPs should be kept to an “absolute minimum” and used only where the vulnerability or threat to specific information is exceptional and ordinary access rules are not sufficient. It also requires [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) systems, oversight by the Information Security Oversight Office, and annual review of whether each SAP still meets the required standard.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>

The democratic cost appears when the regulatory effect of secrecy outruns the ability to test its necessity. In ordinary public administration, a costly rule can be challenged by affected parties, audited, amended, or repealed. In a SAP, the people best placed to challenge a requirement may not be read into the programme. For unacknowledged programmes, even the existence of the programme may be controlled. For waived SAPs, normal congressional reporting channels are narrowed, though current Department of Defense policy still states that waived SAPs remain subject to congressional oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

Moynihan’s critique is therefore not an argument for careless disclosure. It is an argument for disciplined secrecy. The Commission repeatedly distinguished between secrets that protect lives, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) sources, military capabilities and diplomacy, and secrecy that mainly protects bureaucratic habits, programme autonomy or embarrassment. Its reform instinct was to make officials ask not only “could disclosure cause harm?” but also “what does protection cost, what benefit does secrecy provide, and how long should this restriction last?”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## Hidden administrative costs

Secrecy has visible costs: secure facilities, background investigations, classified networks, access-control systems, guards, safes, secure transport, contractor compliance, classification training, and declassification review. It also has less visible costs: slower collaboration, duplicated work, delayed oversight, narrower technical review, and weaker public learning from past decisions.

The Moynihan Commission found that cost accounting for secrecy had improved but was still incomplete. In the mid-1990s, government surveys estimated roughly $2.7 billion in annual federal security classification costs for fiscal years 1995 and 1996, excluding some intelligence data, while a 1996 industry sample estimated more than $2.9 billion in industry costs connected to protecting national-security information. Taken together, the Commission cited more than $5.6 billion spent in 1995 to protect classified national-security information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

Those figures were not presented as a complete bill. The Commission warned that even improved accounting did not capture intangible costs: reduced review, less informed judgement, diminished public debate, and the friction created when information cannot move to people who need it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span> That remains important because current cost data are still hard to interpret. [ISOO]({{ 'isoo/' | relative_url }})’s FY 2024 report says agency-reported cost data for classified national-security information have fluctuated enough year to year that it is difficult to draw reliable conclusions. For security-clearance investigations and reinvestigations alone, agencies reported about $1.5 billion in FY 2021, about $714 million in FY 2022, about $300 million in FY 2023, and just over $983 million in FY 2024; ISOO said the swings may reflect changing accounting methods rather than real operational changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2024-annual-report-final-508.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span></span></span>

For SAPs, the hidden-cost problem is sharper because the security wrapper can become its own management system. The Moynihan Commission heard from industrial contractors that SAP security practices were often redundant, expensive and inconsistent. It noted that special-access controls could range from access rosters to separate facilities, added physical security, elaborate concealment and operational-security plans. The Commission also warned that programme funding and management could be split across agencies, making it difficult even to count programmes and their costs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

One concrete example from the Commission is mundane but revealing: a contractor security officer produced a thick set of supplemental forms, each prepared by different programme managers and often requesting the same information before employees could receive access to certain SAPs. That is the Moynihan critique in miniature. The cost is not only the form itself; it is duplicated vetting, delayed staffing, inconsistent standards, and the creation of a culture in which additional controls are rarely forced to justify their marginal value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## Why SAPs sharpen the concern

SAPs are designed for cases where ordinary classification is not enough. That is a legitimate purpose. The issue is that, once a programme is inside a tight compartment, many ordinary correction mechanisms become weaker. Fewer people can compare the programme with alternatives. Fewer auditors can see the full record. Fewer technical experts can test assumptions. Fewer legislators can ask informed questions. The very structure that protects a sensitive capability can also protect bad estimates, [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}) or managerial failure.

The Commission’s discussion of SAPs focused on this trade-off. It described acknowledged, unacknowledged and waived programmes, noting that “black” programmes have [classified existence]({{ 'hidden-existence/' | relative_url }}) and that waived programmes are exempt from standard congressional reporting requirements, with notification limited to a very small set of senior congressional figures. It also found that reviews of SAPs had improved, but often focused on programme cost, schedule and performance without paying enough attention to the cost and value of the special security measures themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

The A-12 Avenger II, a Navy stealth aircraft programme cancelled in 1991, is a useful cautionary example because it shows how secrecy and acquisition risk can interact. A Department of Defense Inspector General review found cost, schedule and technical problems so serious that the programme’s continued viability was in doubt. The review also described failures in cost and schedule control mechanisms, including a finding that the programme team should have reported projected cost increases earlier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.defense.gov/1991/Feb/28/2001714477/-1/-1/1/91-059.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.defense.gov">[U.S. Department of War]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.defense.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U.S. Department of War Review of the A-12 Aircraft Program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U.S. Department of War Review of the A-12 Aircraft Program</span></span></span>(https://media.defense.gov/1991/Feb/28/2001714477/-1/-1/1/91-059.pdf)

Steven Aftergood later described the A-12 as a “special access” programme and a paradigm of a secret programme running out of control, not because every SAP fails in that way, but because restrictive access can narrow the circle of people able to detect failure early.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu/wps/rej02/rej02b.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu">[CIAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ciaotest.cc.columbia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on columbia.edu.</span></span></span> The lesson is not that SAPs are inherently wasteful. It is that SAPs need unusually strong internal challenge functions because external challenge is deliberately constrained.

Moynihan’s remedy was not simply “make more public”. It was to make secrecy compete against other values at the point of decision. The Commission recommended that classification decisions, including the creation of SAPs, should not rest only on possible national-security damage. Officials should also weigh cost of protection, vulnerability, threat, risk, value of the information, and public benefit from release.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

## The democracy cost is not only money

The financial cost of secrecy is easier to count than the democratic cost, but the democratic cost is central to the Moynihan critique. The Commission argued that excessive secrecy can leave policymakers less informed, prevent government from being held accountable, and block the public from informed debate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

This has three practical effects in the SAP setting.

First, secrecy can reduce the quality of decision-making. Narrow [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) may protect sensitive technical facts, but they can also exclude outside expertise, competing programme offices, independent cost analysts or operational users who would spot weaknesses. The Commission warned that secrecy can limit input, review and criticism, allowing institutional assumptions to go unchallenged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>

Second, secrecy can weaken oversight. Congress can receive classified briefings, and SAPs are not beyond law. But oversight quality depends on who is informed, how much detail is supplied, whether staff can compare programmes, and whether cost, schedule and technical assumptions can be tested. That is why 10 U.S.C. § 119 requires annual Department of Defense reporting on SAPs to the defence committees and separate reporting on new SAPs, including justification and cost estimates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span>

Third, overuse of secrecy can damage respect for necessary secrecy. The Commission argued that when too much information is classified, leaks become more common, public trust declines, and it becomes harder to distinguish genuine protection from bureaucratic concealment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span> This concern still appears in modern classification debates. In a 2023 Senate hearing, Elizabeth Goitein of the Brennan Center testified that insiders have estimated 50 to 90 per cent of classified information could safely be public, and that overclassification impedes democracy, oversight and national security information-sharing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CHRG-118shrg52497/html/CHRG-118shrg52497.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CHRG 118shrg52497</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">CHRG 118shrg52497</span></span></span>

## The Moynihan test for a SAP

A Moynihan-style assessment of a SAP would not begin by assuming that secrecy is wrong. It would begin by asking whether the extra secrecy is doing enough work to justify its cost. For waived and unacknowledged SAPs, that test should be especially demanding because the accountability channel is narrower.

The practical questions are:

* **What exceptional vulnerability justifies the SAP?** Executive Order 13526 requires more than sensitivity in general; it requires exceptional vulnerability or threat and a finding that ordinary access rules are insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>
* **How many people actually need access?** SAPs are supposed to be limited to a reasonably small access population, but too small a circle can also degrade review and execution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>
* **What is the marginal cost of the special controls?** The Moynihan Commission singled out the need to weigh security costs against benefits, especially where contractors face redundant forms, inconsistent standards or programme-specific facility requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/chap2.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span>
* **When should the compartment end?** Current DoD policy calls for lifecycle planning, including criteria for disestablishing SAP compartments or transferring critical programme information to ordinary classified protection when SAP controls are no longer necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>
* **Who can challenge the classification decision?** ISOO’s FY 2024 reporting shows that even ordinary classification infrastructure remains administratively demanding: it reviewed security classification guides and found all but one deficient in some element of the applicable requirements, including missing classification levels, declassification dates or clear reasons for classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2024-annual-report-final-508.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span></span></span>

This is where the Moynihan critique remains useful. It turns secrecy from a moral abstraction into a governance question: what is the rule, who pays for it, who checks it, when does it expire, and what public or institutional value is lost while it remains in force?

## The continuing reform problem

Many of Moynihan’s concerns have been absorbed into official policy language. Executive Order 13526 requires limits, accounting, ISOO oversight and annual review for SAPs. Department of Defense policy now describes SAP lifecycle management, oversight authorities, and the possibility of moving information out of SAP protection when the extra controls are no longer needed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>

Yet the deeper problem remains: secrecy systems are easier to expand than to prune. The Public Interest Declassification Board warned in 2020 that the government needs a new model for accurately measuring classification and declassification costs across the system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/declassification/pidb/recommendations/pidb-vision-for-digital-age-may-2020.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives20A Vision for the Digital Age: Modernization</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives20A Vision for the Digital Age: Modernization</span></span></span> ISOO’s later difficulty interpreting cost fluctuations reinforces the same point: even decades after Moynihan, the government still struggles to measure the full administrative burden of secrecy in a way that supports confident management decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2024-annual-report-final-508.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span></span></span>

For SAPs, the lasting lesson is that oversight cannot stop at asking whether a programme is important. It must ask whether the special access architecture is still necessary, proportionate and cost-effective. A programme may deserve protection while some of its surrounding secrecy practices deserve reform. Conversely, a programme may be so sensitive that extraordinary restrictions are justified, but then the need for disciplined internal audit, lifecycle review and congressional confidence becomes greater, not smaller.

The Moynihan critique endures because it does not deny the need for secrets. It insists that secrecy itself is a costly instrument of government power. In the SAP world, especially for waived and unacknowledged programmes, that means secrecy should be treated as a scarce protective resource, not an unlimited administrative habit.
