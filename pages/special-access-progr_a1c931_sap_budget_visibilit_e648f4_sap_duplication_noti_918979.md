## Introduction

One of the least visible safeguards against waste in highly classified defence spending is a simple statutory requirement: when the Department of Defense proposes a new special access programme (SAP), it is expected to identify existing programmes or technologies that are similar in mission or technology. That requirement exists because compartmentation, while protecting sensitive capabilities, also makes it harder for officials to see whether different organisations are independently funding overlapping work.

The mechanism is not designed to eliminate [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}) on its own. Rather, it gives the small group of congressional committees and defence officials who receive classified budget information a prompt to ask whether a genuinely new capability is being proposed or whether an existing classified effort already addresses the same problem. In a budgeting system where many programmes cannot be openly compared, these similarity notices act as one of the few formal anti-duplication checks built into the oversight process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional...(B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to...</span></span></span>

## Can Secret Programmes Accidentally Buy the Same Thing?

Compartmentation deliberately limits who knows about a programme. That protects sensitive technologies, operational methods and [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) sources, but it also creates an organisational problem: people responsible for one classified programme may not know enough about another to recognise overlap.

In ordinary government procurement, duplication can often be identified through public budget documents, programme reviews, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) databases and independent analysis. Special access programmes restrict many of those comparison tools. Different offices may pursue related technologies while sharing information only with narrowly defined groups that have both the appropriate clearance and a specific need to know.

The risk is not necessarily that two programmes are identical. More commonly, multiple organisations may separately invest in similar sensors, communications systems, software, propulsion concepts or intelligence capabilities because each programme is isolated from broader visibility. Without an intentional comparison process, overlap may remain hidden until significant funds have already been committed.

This institutional problem has been recognised in law for decades. Rather than assuming secrecy alone prevents waste, [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) required additional reporting precisely because ordinary market-style transparency is unavailable for SAPs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional...(B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to...</span></span></span>

## How Similarity Notices Are Supposed to Help

The statutory reporting requirements in 10 U.S.C. §119 go beyond asking for programme cost estimates. When a new Department of Defense SAP is reported to Congress, the submission must include:

* the estimated total programme cost;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[uscode.house.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to the...Read more...</span></span></span>
* the programme's mission and major [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }});
* and an identification of existing programmes or technologies that use similar technology or perform a similar mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional...(B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to...</span></span></span>

This requirement performs several oversight functions simultaneously.

First, it forces programme sponsors to demonstrate that they have considered whether similar work already exists. Preparing the notification is therefore more than an administrative exercise; it requires at least some internal comparison before requesting protected funding.

Second, it provides congressional defence committees with context that would otherwise be missing from a classified budget request. If reviewers learn that a proposed SAP resembles another classified effort, they can ask why both are necessary, whether one should replace the other, or whether the missions have been properly differentiated.

Third, similarity notices create a record that can be compared over time. Even if individual programmes remain compartmented, recurring classified reporting gives authorised reviewers opportunities to identify patterns of repeated investment across multiple budget cycles.

The mechanism therefore substitutes structured disclosure inside the classified oversight system for the broad public comparison that is impossible in genuinely sensitive programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional...(B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to...</span></span></span>

## Why This Mechanism Matters More Than It Appears

The requirement may look modest, but it addresses a structural weakness rather than a technical one.

Secrecy naturally fragments knowledge. Engineers, programme managers and contracting officials may each possess deep expertise within their own compartment while lacking visibility into adjacent efforts. Even senior officials can face information barriers if they have not been read into particular programmes.

By requiring explicit identification of similar programmes, Congress attempts to counteract that fragmentation without dismantling compartmentation itself. The reporting obligation encourages authorised officials to ask questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Is this genuinely a new capability or another implementation of an existing idea?
* Does another classified programme already solve the same operational problem?
* Are multiple organisations funding parallel research because they cannot see one another's work?
* Could two programmes be coordinated or consolidated?

</div>

Those questions are especially important during the early stages of acquisition, when duplication is less expensive to prevent than to unwind after [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}) and facilities have already been established.

## Where the Rule May Still Fall Short

Although the statutory requirement is valuable, it cannot completely eliminate duplication.

### It depends on what programme managers know

A similarity notice is only as comprehensive as the information available to the officials preparing it. If another highly compartmented programme lies outside their authorised access, they may be unable to identify it accurately even if it performs comparable work.

### Reviewers still face information limits

Congressional oversight of SAPs is concentrated within a relatively small number of committees and, for the most sensitive waived programmes, sometimes within an even smaller group of congressional leaders. While this protects security, it also limits the number of independent reviewers who can recognise overlaps across organisational boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Code10 USC 119: Special access programs: congressional...(B) an identification of existing programs or technologies that are similar to...</span></span></span>

### Similar does not necessarily mean duplicative

Two programmes may share technologies while serving different operational needs. For example, comparable sensing technologies might support entirely different military missions or operate under different security constraints. Similarity notices therefore identify possible overlap rather than proving unnecessary duplication.

### Bureaucratic incentives remain

Programme offices may emphasise distinctions between projects to justify continued funding, while reviewers may lack sufficient technical detail to determine whether those distinctions represent meaningful operational differences or merely different implementations of the same concept. Because much of the evidence remains classified, outside analysts cannot independently test these judgements.

## The Mechanism's Practical Value

The similarity-notice requirement illustrates how oversight adapts when transparency is impossible. Instead of relying on public scrutiny, it builds a mandatory comparison exercise into the classified budget process itself.

Its effectiveness depends less on the statutory language than on the willingness of programme sponsors to identify comparable efforts honestly and on the ability of authorised reviewers to challenge those comparisons. It cannot fully overcome the information barriers created by compartmentation, particularly for the most restricted programmes, but it provides one of the few formal mechanisms specifically aimed at preventing secret programmes from unknowingly—or unnecessarily—buying the same capability twice.
