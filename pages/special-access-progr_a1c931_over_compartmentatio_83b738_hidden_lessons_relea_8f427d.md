## Introduction

One of the least visible costs of highly compartmented special-access-programs (SAPs), including waived and unacknowledged programmes, is not simply duplicate spending. It is duplicate learning. When technical failures, security incidents, contractor performance issues or operational discoveries remain confined to separate [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}), later programmes can unknowingly repeat work that another team has already paid to do. The result is that organisations may solve the same engineering problem twice, rediscover the same design limits, or repeat the same [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) mistakes despite having already generated the knowledge elsewhere.

This risk is difficult to measure because the evidence itself is often compartmented. Public oversight documents therefore focus less on identifying individual duplicated lessons than on the governance problem that allows them to occur: fragmented oversight, restricted information sharing and insufficient mechanisms for comparing programmes across compartment boundaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520001m_vol3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoDM 5200.01, Volume 3, &quot;DoD Information Security...July 28, 2020 — 24 Feb 2012 — sensitive compartmented information (SCI), and Spec...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2020</span></span></span>

## How lessons get trapped inside compartments

Compartmentation deliberately limits who can access sensitive information. That protects [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) sources, military capabilities and advanced technologies, but it also limits the audience that can benefit from experience.

Several kinds of knowledge can become trapped:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Technical failures.** Engineers may discover that a manufacturing process, sensor architecture or software approach cannot meet operational requirements. If the finding remains inside one compartment, another programme may invest years reaching the same conclusion.
* **Contractor experience.** Programme offices often accumulate detailed knowledge about supplier performance, integration problems or unrealistic cost estimates. Those observations may never reach another compartment managing a related effort.
* **Operational feedback.** Testing may reveal unexpected vulnerabilities, maintenance burdens or environmental limitations that remain inaccessible outside the programme.
* **Security practice.** Investigations into compromises or insider-risk events frequently produce procedural improvements, but strict access controls can prevent wider adoption where comparable risks exist.

</div>

Unlike ordinary government programmes, SAPs cannot always rely on enterprise-wide knowledge repositories or broad technical communities. Even individuals with appropriate security clearances frequently lack the specific "need to know" required to access another compartment's experience. The Department of Defense's security framework therefore balances protection with policies intended to promote appropriate information sharing and efficient management rather than secrecy for its own sake.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520001m_vol3.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoDM 5200.01, Volume 3, &quot;DoD Information Security...July 28, 2020 — 24 Feb 2012 — sensitive compartmented information (SCI), and Spec...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 28, 2020</span></span></span>

### Why experience is harder to transfer than documents

Lessons learned are rarely a single report.

They often consist of accumulated judgement: why a requirement changed, why a prototype failed repeatedly, which assumptions proved unrealistic, or why particular integration choices created long-term maintenance costs. Much of this knowledge exists in programme reviews, technical discussions and the experience of personnel rather than in a standalone document.

When personnel rotate out of highly compartmented assignments, their ability to transfer that experience may remain constrained by continuing security obligations. As a result, organisational memory can become fragmented even inside the same department.

## Why duplicate learning is different from duplicate hardware

Duplicate programmes are often imagined as two offices unknowingly building similar aircraft, satellites or sensors. Duplicate learning is usually more subtle.

Two programmes may legitimately pursue different missions while independently encountering the same underlying engineering or management problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* validating similar materials under comparable operating conditions;
* discovering identical cybersecurity weaknesses;
* repeating unsuccessful integration strategies;
* relearning manufacturing tolerances already established elsewhere;
* repeating unrealistic schedule assumptions for advanced technologies.

</div>

None of these situations necessarily represents wasted hardware procurement. Instead, they represent repeated investment in discovering facts that already existed within another protected compartment.

Because advanced defence programmes frequently involve long development timelines and specialised expertise, rediscovering the same technical constraints can consume years rather than months. The cost therefore appears as delayed capability, repeated testing and avoidable programme risk instead of an obviously duplicated procurement line.

## Why oversight struggles to detect repeated mistakes

Traditional programme oversight assumes reviewers can compare similar efforts across an organisation.

Highly compartmented programmes complicate that process because reviewers themselves may not possess access to every relevant compartment simultaneously. Congressional reporting requirements, internal reviews and audit mechanisms exist for SAPs, but the challenge is ensuring that someone has sufficient visibility to recognise when two isolated programmes are independently relearning the same lesson.

Historical Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office (GAO) work on SAP oversight repeatedly identified concerns that increasing compartmentation and restricted access limited effective oversight and reduced the ability of reviewers to evaluate programmes comprehensively. While those reports do not claim that every compartment duplicates work, they explain why fragmented visibility creates conditions in which [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}) and repeated mistakes become harder to detect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/nsiad" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOD Special Access Programs: Administrative Due...GAO reviewed the due process procedures and practices for individuals whose access...</span></span></span>

## Ways oversight can move knowledge without exposing secrets

The objective is not to eliminate compartmentation. Sensitive programmes often require exceptional protection. The challenge is allowing essential knowledge to move while protecting operational details.

Several governance approaches seek that balance.

**Cross-compartment technical review.** A small number of specially authorised reviewers can compare programmes for overlapping technical risks without broadly expanding access.

**Sanitised lessons learned.** Some findings can be rewritten to remove operational details while preserving engineering or programme-management knowledge. For example, a lesson about unrealistic testing assumptions may be transferable even if the platform involved remains classified.

**Central SAP oversight.** Department of Defense policy assigns oversight bodies responsibilities that include reviewing SAPs, assessing continued need for enhanced protection and helping ensure programmes do not unnecessarily duplicate or overlap. Those reviews provide one mechanism for recognising recurring problems that individual compartments cannot easily see themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Student Guide Course: Special Access Program (SAP)...In this course, you will learn why we have SAPs, how they are authorized, and t...</span></span></span>

**Knowledge management for cleared communities.** Experience from other high-consequence organisations shows that structured lessons-learned systems, searchable repositories and incentives for sharing institutional knowledge reduce repeated mistakes. Although SAP environments operate under much stricter security constraints, the underlying management principle remains relevant: protecting information does not require losing organisational memory. ## The enduring trade-off

The central tension is not whether compartmentation is necessary, but how narrowly it should be applied after information has been generated.

Every additional compartment reduces the probability of unauthorised disclosure. At the same time, it can also reduce the probability that legitimate reviewers, engineers and acquisition officials will recognise that another protected programme has already encountered—and solved—the same problem.

For highly classified SAPs, the greatest hidden cost of over-compartmentation may therefore be invisible in budget tables. It appears instead as repeated technical dead ends, recurring acquisition mistakes and institutional memory that remains isolated inside compartments long after its most valuable lesson could have been shared safely.
