## Introduction

The modern system for waived Department of Defense Special Access Programs (SAPs) emerged from a broader political struggle over the United States' "black budget"—the portion of defence spending devoted to highly classified activities. Rather than eliminating secret programmes or requiring full disclosure to [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}), reforms created a compromise. Most SAPs became subject to more structured reporting requirements, while a small category of exceptionally sensitive programmes could still be "waived" from normal congressional notification. The result was the eight-member oversight model: a narrow statutory exception intended to preserve operational secrecy without removing Congress entirely from the oversight process. This balance remains central to debates over classified defence spending because it reflects an enduring tension between democratic [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) and the protection of exceptionally sensitive national security capabilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rch 1 of each year, the Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense...</span></span></span>

## Black programmes before stronger reporting rules

Before the late 1980s, congressional oversight of highly classified defence programmes was considerably less formalised than it is today. The rapid expansion of covert technology development during the Cold War—including advanced aircraft, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) systems and other classified procurement programmes—raised recurring concerns that large sums of money could move through the defence budget with only limited scrutiny by the broader membership of Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/crs/natsec/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security and Defense Policy A Defense Budget Primer,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security and Defense PolicyA Defense Budget Primer, December 9, 1998; Special Access Programs and the Defense Budget: Understand...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 9, 1998</span></span></span>

The issue was not simply that programmes were secret from the public. Many legislators argued that secrecy also limited Congress's own ability to compare projects, detect [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}), assess cost growth or determine whether extraordinary security measures remained justified. This concern became part of wider debates about the "black budget", especially as classified procurement expanded during the Reagan administration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/crs/natsec/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Security and Defense Policy A Defense Budget Primer,</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Security and Defense PolicyA Defense Budget Primer, December 9, 1998; Special Access Programs and the Defense Budget: Understand...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 9, 1998</span></span></span>

Congress responded by placing statutory reporting requirements into the Fiscal Year 1988 National Defense Authorization framework, later codified as 10 U.S.C. §119. Instead of relying primarily on informal executive-branch briefings, the law required annual reporting on Department of Defense SAPs and notification when new programmes were established. The objective was to institutionalise oversight without dismantling legitimate secrecy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/94-261.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportIntelligence Spending: Public Disclosure IssuesLegislative provisions regarding the reporting of budgetary data for speci...</span></span></span>

## How waived SAPs preserve a narrow exception

The statutory waiver mechanism was deliberately crafted as an exception rather than the rule.

Under 10 U.S.C. §119, the Secretary of Defense may determine that providing the full programme information normally required for congressional reporting would itself damage national security. In those circumstances, certain details may be withheld from the wider defence committees. However, the omitted information and the justification for withholding it must still be provided to the chairs and ranking minority members of the relevant House and Senate armed services and defence appropriations committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rch 1 of each year, the Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense...</span></span></span>

This is the legal foundation for what is commonly described as the "eight-member" oversight model.

The reform therefore achieved two goals simultaneously:

* it ended the idea that exceptionally sensitive programmes could simply operate without any congressional notification;
* it preserved an emergency mechanism for protecting information considered unusually vulnerable to compromise.

The waiver is therefore better understood as a narrowing of oversight rather than an elimination of oversight. Congress remains informed, but knowledge is concentrated among a very small bipartisan group of senior legislators instead of the committees as a whole.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">rch 1 of each year, the Secretary of Defense shall submit to the defense...</span></span></span>

## Why the exception survived reform

From a policy perspective, the survival of the waiver reflects competing constitutional priorities.

Supporters argue that certain capabilities—particularly advanced military technologies, intelligence support systems or exceptionally sensitive operational methods—could suffer severe damage if awareness spread beyond the smallest practical circle. Under this view, the waiver exists precisely because ordinary committee reporting cannot always accommodate the highest levels of operational sensitivity.

Critics have long argued that concentrating oversight creates its own risks. When only a handful of legislators receive detailed briefings, opportunities for [committee staff]({{ 'staff-access/' | relative_url }}) analysis, cross-programme comparison, sustained [budget review]({{ 'budget-review/' | relative_url }}) and institutional memory become more limited. Oversight remains legally present but can become more dependent upon the judgement, available time and technical expertise of a very small number of elected officials. These concerns have formed part of broader debates over government secrecy since the 1990s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Report Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Public Intelligence &#124;COMMISSION ON PROTECTING AND REDUCING...1995, Congress established a commission to both protect and reduce governme...</span></span></span>

Importantly, subsequent reform efforts have generally sought to improve management and oversight of special-access systems rather than abolish the waiver altogether. ## Reform legacy

The legacy of the black budget reforms is therefore not the elimination of secrecy but the creation of a structured hierarchy of congressional visibility.

Most Department of Defense SAPs became subject to formal reporting requirements established by [statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}). Waived SAPs remained available only as an exceptional category, preserving the executive branch's ability to restrict dissemination when it concluded that broader reporting would itself endanger national security. The eight-member oversight model should therefore be understood as the residual exception left after Congress successfully pushed for much stronger reporting across the wider classified defence budget.
