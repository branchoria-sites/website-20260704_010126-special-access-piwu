## Introduction

A special access programme, or SAP, is supposed to exist only when ordinary classification controls are not enough. Executive Order 13526 defines a SAP as a programme for a specific class of classified national security information that adds safeguarding and access requirements beyond those normally required at the same classification level. The order then narrows when such a programme may be created: the information must face an exceptional vulnerability or threat, normal access rules must be insufficient, the access population must ordinarily remain reasonably small, and the agency must keep the programme visible to internal [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) and oversight systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesThe President Executive Order 1352629 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classifying, safeguarding, a...</span></span></span>

That matters because SAPs are often discussed as if they were simply “deeper secrecy”. The executive-order rule is more specific. It treats a SAP as a governance intervention: a controlled exception to the normal classification system, justified by a particular protection problem and constrained by review, record-keeping and oversight. In the wider world of acknowledged, unacknowledged and waived SAPs, this creation rule is the first safeguard against turning compartmentation into a default way of running sensitive government work.

## The Threshold Is Exceptional Risk, Not Mere Sensitivity

Executive Order 13526 does not allow a SAP merely because information is classified, embarrassing, politically sensitive, expensive or operationally important. The core test is whether “the vulnerability of, or threat to, specific information is exceptional” and whether the normal criteria for access to information at the same classification level are not sufficient to prevent unauthorised disclosure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information29 Dec 2009 — (1) the vulnerability of, or threat to, specific information is except...</span></span></span>

This is the key distinction between classification and special access. A document may be Secret or Top Secret because its unauthorised disclosure could damage national security. A SAP adds another layer only when the usual clearance-and-need-to-know model is judged inadequate for a defined class of information. In plain terms, the question is not “is this important?” but “why would ordinary classified controls fail here?”

Defence training material explains the same rule in practical terms: SAPs are established only when required by [statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}), or when there is an exceptional vulnerability or threat to specific information and normal access criteria are insufficient. It also stresses that SAPs still use the standard classification levels — Confidential, Secret and Top Secret — rather than creating a new level above Top Secret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Student Guide Course: Special Access Program (SAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Student Guide Course: Special Access Program (SAP)...February 4, 2020 — Executive Order 13526, “Classified National Security Informa...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 4, 2020</span></span></span>

For the Department of Defense, the current policy language is even more restrictive. DoD Directive 5205.07 says SAP protection is to be used for the department’s most sensitive classified information relating to advanced systems, capabilities, technologies and [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}), and only when the vulnerability or threat is exceptional and ordinary “collateral” classified controls are not enough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod directive 5205.07 special access program policySeptember 11, 2024 — 12 Sept 2024 — to safeguard the DoD&#x27;s most sensitive classifie...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 11, 2024</span></span></span> DoD training summarises the operational standard as “absolutely necessary” to protect the most sensitive DoD capabilities, information, technologies and operations, or when required by statute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA002-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special Access Program Security Annual Refresher</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Program Security Annual Refresher</span></span></span>

The policy logic is therefore defensive rather than decorative. A SAP is not supposed to be a prestige label, a bureaucratic moat, or a way to avoid difficult oversight. It is supposed to answer a concrete protection failure: for example, a technology whose compromise would reveal a capability before it can be fielded, an operation whose exposure would endanger people or sources, or a tightly integrated system where fragmentary disclosures could still reveal the whole.

## The Access Population Must Stay Small

The second creation rule is about scale. Executive Order 13526 says SAPs must be limited to programmes in which the number of people who ordinarily have access will be “reasonably small” and proportionate to the objective of enhanced protection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/es/sla/ddi/docs/EUA4%20Executive%20Order%2013526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span></span></span> This requirement is easy to overlook, but it is central to the design of SAP governance.

The point is not that a SAP must have a tiny membership in all circumstances. Major acquisition, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) or operational programmes may involve government staff, military personnel, contractors and oversight officials. The rule is that the population must remain small enough to make the special controls meaningful. If thousands of people need routine access, the argument for a highly compartmented regime becomes weaker unless the programme is divided into narrower [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) with clearly justified access boundaries.

This small-population rule also explains why “need to know” is not just a slogan inside SAPs. A person may hold the right clearance and still have no access unless they are formally admitted to the programme and need the information for an authorised function. SAP controls can therefore restrict access horizontally among people who are otherwise cleared at the same level.

The same principle appears in agency-level implementations. The Air Force, for example, states that access to SAPs should be limited to the minimum number of personnel necessary to meet mission requirements, and that SAPs should be established and maintained only when absolutely necessary and consistent with Executive Order 13526.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://static.e-publishing.af.mil/production/1/saf_aa/publication/afpd16-7/afpd16-7.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: static.e-publishing.af.mil">[E-Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">static.e-publishing.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">pd16 7</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">pd16 7</span></span></span> This is the practical version of the executive-order test: if the mission cannot be done with a narrow access population, the programme must justify why SAP controls still make sense.

The access-population rule has a governance benefit as well as a security benefit. Smaller [access lists]({{ 'access-lists/' | relative_url }}) make it easier to know who is read in, who left, who needs continuing access, and which offices are accountable for programme decisions. In a waived or [unacknowledged SAP]({{ 'unacknowledged-7fdfef/' | relative_url }}), where even the programme’s existence may be tightly controlled, those internal access records become one of the few ways to keep secrecy administratively traceable.

## Creation Triggers an Accounting Duty

Executive Order 13526 does not stop at the decision to create a SAP. It requires each agency head to establish and maintain a system of accounting for SAPs, consistent with directives issued under the order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.oas.org/es/sla/ddi/docs/EUA4%20Executive%20Order%2013526.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: oas.org">[Organization of American States]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">oas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EUA4 Executive Order 13526</span></span></span> That means the executive-order framework expects agencies to know what SAPs they have, who is responsible for them, and whether they continue to satisfy the rule that justified their creation.

This accounting duty is a quiet but important limit on the idea of a “black” programme. A SAP may be unacknowledged to the public and inaccessible to most cleared officials, but it is not supposed to be unrecorded within the executive branch. The accounting system is what allows annual review, senior-agency responsibility and external oversight bodies to ask whether a SAP still belongs in that category.

The implementing rules under 32 CFR Part 2001 reinforce that SAPs must be evaluated at least annually against the executive-order requirements for accounting and continued validity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2010-title32-vol6/pdf/CFR-2010-title32-vol6-part2001.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on govinfo.gov.</span></span></span> Executive Order 13526 itself requires the agency head or principal deputy to review each SAP annually to decide whether it still meets the order’s requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information29 Dec 2009 — (1) the vulnerability of, or threat to, specific information is except...</span></span></span>

That annual-review requirement matters because the original reason for a SAP can decay. A weapon system may move from development into ordinary fielding. A sensitive operation may end. A technology may become known to adversaries. A contractor structure may change. In those cases, the governance question is not whether the information remains classified, but whether the additional SAP layer is still justified.

The historical concern is not theoretical. The Moynihan Commission on government secrecy reported that, in 1994, DoD created the Special Access Program Oversight Committee to standardise and formalise approval, termination, revalidation and restructuring procedures for DoD SAPs, and that Executive Order 12958 required annual review and validation of identified DoD SAPs for continued SAP status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/MoynihanReportGovernmentSecrecy.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Moynihan Report Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Moynihan Report Government Secrecy</span></span></span> The current Executive Order 13526 retains the same basic discipline: creation must be paired with revalidation.

## Oversight Is Built Into the Rule, Even for Highly Sensitive SAPs

The executive-order framework makes SAPs subject to oversight, not exempt from it. Executive Order 13526 states that SAPs are subject to the agency oversight programme established under the order and that the Director of the Information Security Oversight Office, or ISOO, must be afforded access to SAPs in accordance with the security requirements of each programme. For extraordinarily sensitive and vulnerable programmes, an agency head may limit [ISOO access]({{ 'isoo-access/' | relative_url }}) to the Director only, or to the Director and no more than one other ISOO employee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information29 Dec 2009 — (1) the vulnerability of, or threat to, specific information is except...</span></span></span>

This creates a careful balance. The order recognises that some programmes are so sensitive that even oversight access should be tightly channelled. But it does not let agencies turn sensitivity into total insulation from classification oversight. The default is access for ISOO; the exception is a narrower ISOO access channel, not no access at all.

ISOO’s wider role is to oversee the executive branch security classification system and report to the President on its status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/isoo/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[FAS Project on Government Secrecy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fas.org.</span></span></span> Its recent annual reports show that this role is still active. The FY 2024 report says ISOO conducts on-site inspections of executive-branch agencies to evaluate classified national security information programmes and enforce compliance with presidential directives, including Executive Order 13526.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2024-annual-report-final-508.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2024 annual report final 508</span></span></span> The FY 2023 report also noted an interagency effort, led by National Security Council staff, to modernise and reform rules governing classified and controlled unclassified information, including SAPs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/files/isoo/reports/isoo-fy-2023-annual-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">isoo fy 2023 annual report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">isoo fy 2023 annual report</span></span></span>

The annual-review and ISOO-access rules are especially important for unacknowledged SAPs. If a programme’s existence is itself not publicly confirmed, normal democratic feedback is limited. Oversight therefore has to operate through authorised institutional channels: agency heads, principal deputies, SAP central offices, inspectors, ISOO, the National Security Council process and, for DoD programmes, Congress under statutory reporting rules.

## Waived SAPs Narrow Reporting, But Do Not Rewrite the Creation Test

Waived SAPs are often the most misunderstood category. They are not a separate executive-order standard for creating SAPs. They are a narrower reporting status, mainly relevant to Department of Defense SAPs under 10 U.S.C. § 119, layered on top of an already unacknowledged SAP.

Section 119 requires the Secretary of Defense to report DoD SAPs to the congressional defence committees, including annual budget information and notice and justification for each new SAP. It also requires reporting on planned changes in classification or public disclosure of classified information concerning DoD SAPs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=2000&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-2000-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span> The waiver provision allows the Secretary of Defense, case by case, to omit certain information from those reports if including it would adversely affect national security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

That statutory waiver affects the reporting channel; it does not erase the executive-order creation rules. A waived SAP should still satisfy the same basic threshold: exceptional vulnerability or threat, insufficient ordinary access controls, a reasonably small access population, accounting, annual review and oversight. DoD Instruction 5205.11, updated in 2024, describes its purpose as establishing policy, responsibilities and procedures for the management, administration and oversight of all DoD SAPs in accordance with Executive Order 13526 and DoD Directive 5205.07.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The distinction matters because “waived” can sound like “outside oversight”. In law and policy, it is narrower than that. Federation of American Scientists commentary describes waived SAPs as the most sensitive DoD SAPs and notes that, in such cases, notification may be limited to a small group of senior members of the congressional defence committees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/dod_saps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">dod saps</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod saps</span></span></span> But even that restricted channel exists because the programme remains inside a statutory oversight structure, not outside government.

## Why These Rules Exist

The creation rules are designed to solve two opposing problems at once. On one side, some information genuinely needs more protection than ordinary classification provides. On the other, every extra compartment can raise costs, slow information-sharing, fragment accountability and make programmes harder to audit.

The Moynihan Commission captured the institutional concern in its discussion of SAP costs and redundancy. It reported that industrial contractors repeatedly raised concerns about redundant and costly security practices in SAPs, and it recommended measures to standardise security practices in such programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/sum.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[FAS Project on Government Secrecy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on fas.org.</span></span></span> That is one reason the executive-order rules focus not only on secrecy, but also on necessity, accounting and review.

The most useful way to read the creation rule is as a gatekeeping test:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Is the information already properly classified?** A SAP is an added regime for classified national security information, not a substitute for classification.
* **Is the threat or vulnerability exceptional?** Ordinary sensitivity is not enough.
* **Are normal access rules insufficient?** The agency must be able to explain why clearance, need-to-know and standard safeguarding do not solve the problem.
* **Can access remain reasonably small?** If the programme requires broad routine access, SAP protection may be ill-suited or must be tightly compartmented.
* **Can the programme be accounted for and reviewed?** A SAP must be administratively visible to authorised officials even if it is publicly unacknowledged.
* **Will oversight still function?** ISOO, agency leadership and, for DoD SAPs, Congress retain roles, although the access path may be restricted for the most sensitive programmes.

</div>

These criteria do not make SAPs uncontroversial. They do, however, show that the executive-order model is not “classify it harder whenever convenient”. It is a narrower governance model: create a SAP only when the protection problem is exceptional, keep the access circle small, record the programme, review it annually, and preserve oversight even when public acknowledgement is impossible.
