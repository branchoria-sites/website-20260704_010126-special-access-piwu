## Introduction

A zero-day vulnerability is a software flaw that the vendor does not yet know about or has not had the opportunity to fix. For cyber capabilities protected under highly classified or special-access programmes (SAPs), such vulnerabilities can provide rare opportunities to collect [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}), disrupt hostile systems, or support military [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}). At the same time, every day that a vulnerability remains undisclosed may leave governments, businesses, hospitals and ordinary users exposed if another actor independently discovers or steals the same flaw.

The central policy question is therefore not whether zero-days are valuable—they clearly are—but when that value is outweighed by the wider public interest in disclosure. Modern vulnerability governance attempts to balance these competing interests through structured decision-making processes rather than treating either disclosure or retention as the automatic default. Within the context of [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) capability SAPs, this balancing exercise is one of the most significant points where operational secrecy directly intersects with public safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

## What a Zero-Day Gives a Cyber SAP

Unlike conventional military capabilities, many cyber operations depend on technical conditions that disappear once defenders understand them. A previously unknown vulnerability may allow access to a target network without triggering existing security tools, making it especially valuable for intelligence collection or precisely targeted military operations.

For a classified cyber programme, retaining a zero-day may preserve several advantages simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Continued access to a foreign intelligence target.
* Confidence that defensive monitoring will not yet recognise the technique.
* Reduced operational cost compared with developing an entirely new capability.
* The ability to support multiple authorised operations before the vulnerability is patched.

</div>

This operational value explains why governments sometimes decide not to disclose newly discovered vulnerabilities immediately. The decision is rarely about the software flaw alone; it concerns the broader intelligence picture that the vulnerability enables. Once disclosure occurs, vendors can issue patches, defenders can create detection signatures, and the operational advantage may disappear permanently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

Research by the RAND Corporation also found that many zero-day vulnerabilities remain technically usable for surprisingly long periods because independent rediscovery is less common than often assumed. That finding suggests retention may sometimes preserve operational capability for months or even years rather than only days or weeks. At the same time, RAND emphasised that [uncertainty]({{ 'uncertainty/' | relative_url }}) remains high because governments cannot know whether another actor has independently found the same vulnerability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-day_vulnerability" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Zero-day vulnerability</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Zero-day vulnerability</span></span></span>

## Why Disclosure Can Protect the Wider Ecosystem

The strongest argument for disclosure is straightforward: once a vendor understands a vulnerability, it can usually develop a patch or mitigation that protects millions of users simultaneously.

The benefits extend well beyond consumer devices. Modern critical infrastructure—including hospitals, financial institutions, transport systems and energy networks—often depends upon commercial software that may contain the same vulnerabilities used in intelligence operations. A retained flaw therefore represents a shared risk rather than one confined to a foreign target.

The United States' published Vulnerabilities Equities Process (VEP) reflects this principle explicitly. Its stated policy begins from the position that disclosure generally serves the public interest by improving cybersecurity across the wider digital ecosystem. Retention requires a demonstrable overriding intelligence, law-enforcement or national-security justification rather than simply offering some operational benefit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

The policy also recognises options between immediate disclosure and indefinite secrecy, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Delayed disclosure after an operational window closes.
* Providing classified mitigation information to selected government partners.
* Limiting how frequently a vulnerability may be used.
* Using indirect methods to alert vendors without exposing sensitive operational details.

</div>

These intermediate options illustrate that vulnerability management is rarely a simple binary choice between secrecy and openness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

## How Mission Value Competes with Public Risk

The hardest decisions arise when the same vulnerability simultaneously supports an important intelligence mission and threatens widely deployed civilian technology.

Officials evaluating retention typically consider several questions together rather than relying on a single rule:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **How widespread is the affected software?** A flaw affecting millions of systems generally creates greater public risk than one limited to specialised equipment.
* **How severe would exploitation be?** Remote code execution affecting critical infrastructure raises different concerns from a flaw requiring extensive local access.
* **Is the intelligence unique?** If equivalent information can be obtained through another lawful method, retaining the vulnerability becomes harder to justify.
* **How likely is independent discovery?** The greater the chance that criminals or rival governments will also find the vulnerability, the weaker the case for continued secrecy.
* **Can the operational objective tolerate delayed disclosure?** Some missions are temporary, allowing disclosure after the capability has served its purpose.

</div>

These factors illustrate why vulnerability governance resembles risk management more than technical decision-making. It requires weighing uncertain future harms against uncertain future intelligence gains.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

## Lessons from High-Profile Incidents

Public debate intensified after several prominent incidents demonstrated how retained or stolen cyber capabilities can affect civilian systems.

The most frequently cited example is the exploitation tools associated with the U.S. National Security Agency that were later leaked by the group known as the Shadow Brokers. One exploit, commonly known as EternalBlue, targeted a vulnerability in Microsoft's Server Message Block protocol. Although Microsoft had already released a security update before the public leak, many organisations had not installed it. The exploit subsequently became a key component of the WannaCry ransomware outbreak, which disrupted hospitals, manufacturers and public services worldwide.

The episode highlighted several policy concerns simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* A retained capability can eventually escape government control.
* Disclosure alone does not eliminate risk if organisations delay patching.
* Offensive tools may later be repurposed by criminal actors.
* Operational security surrounding retained capabilities becomes part of public safety itself.

</div>

Importantly, the incident does not demonstrate that every retained vulnerability inevitably causes widespread harm. Rather, it illustrates that retention decisions also depend upon protecting associated exploits, limiting operational exposure and ensuring robust security around classified toolsets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/future-vulnerabilities-equities-processes-around-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Lawfare">[Lawfaremedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Lawfare</span><span class="citation-popover-title">future vulnerabilities equities processes around world</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DefaultThe Future of Vulnerabilities Equities Processes Around...by S Herpig · 2023 · Cited by 6 — A decision to retain a zero day vulne...</span></span></span>

## Governance Rather Than Absolute Rules

Most contemporary policy discussions reject two extreme positions: disclosing every vulnerability immediately or retaining every useful vulnerability indefinitely.

Instead, governments increasingly rely on formal vulnerability equities processes that bring together intelligence, military, law-enforcement, diplomatic and cybersecurity officials. Their purpose is to ensure that both offensive and defensive interests are represented before a decision is made.

The published U.S. VEP charter describes an interagency review process that considers technical severity, operational value, affected populations, defensive implications, commercial impact and broader national interests. Although critics continue to argue that greater transparency and external [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) are needed, publication of the charter represented a significant move away from entirely opaque decision-making.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

Other countries have also begun formalising similar processes, reflecting growing recognition that offensive cyber capability and national cyber defence are not separate policy domains. The same government may simultaneously seek to exploit vulnerabilities abroad while protecting identical technologies used by its own citizens and critical infrastructure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/future-vulnerabilities-equities-processes-around-world" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Lawfare">[Lawfaremedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Lawfare</span><span class="citation-popover-title">future vulnerabilities equities processes around world</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DefaultThe Future of Vulnerabilities Equities Processes Around...by S Herpig · 2023 · Cited by 6 — A decision to retain a zero day vulne...</span></span></span>

## The Enduring Policy Tension

Within cyber capability SAPs, zero-day retention illustrates a fundamental governance dilemma rather than a purely technical one. Retaining a vulnerability may enable lawful intelligence collection or military operations that cannot be achieved by other means. Disclosing that same vulnerability may strengthen the security of millions of systems and reduce the likelihood of criminal exploitation.

No universal formula resolves this conflict. Every decision depends upon the characteristics of the vulnerability, the operational mission, the potential civilian impact, the likelihood of independent discovery and the government's confidence that it can securely manage the capability while it remains undisclosed. The continuing evolution of vulnerability equities processes reflects recognition that both national security and public cybersecurity are legitimate public interests that must be weighed together rather than treated as mutually exclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>
