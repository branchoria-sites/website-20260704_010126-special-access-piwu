## Introduction

Special-access-programmes (SAPs) create a hard accountability tradeoff: the same secrecy that protects sensitive military, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) and technology work can also make it harder for the public, Congress, auditors and even parts of government to judge whether a programme is lawful, affordable, necessary and well run. The strongest public evidence does not show SAPs as a law-free zone. It shows a system with statutory reporting, executive-branch accounting, annual reviews and specialised oversight channels. But it also shows why the tradeoff remains contentious: access is deliberately narrow, public budget lines may be disguised, normal [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) reporting can be limited, and waived SAPs can restrict congressional notification to a very small group.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: A high threshold for creation.">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">A high threshold for creation.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_public_accountabilit_ee83d5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Accountability" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The accountability question, then, is not simply “secret or open?” It is whether the oversight mechanisms inside the classified system are strong enough to compensate for the loss of ordinary public scrutiny. SAPs may be justified when disclosure would expose exceptional vulnerabilities, intelligence sources, sensitive [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}) or advanced capabilities. They become riskier when secrecy turns from a protective measure into a management environment where weak records, duplicated work, inflated costs or questionable legal choices are harder to challenge in time.

## Why legality is harder to verify when the programme itself is secret

A SAP does not sit above the law. Executive Order 13526 limits who may create a SAP, requires an exceptional vulnerability or threat to the information, says normal access controls must be insufficient, and requires agencies to keep an [accounting]({{ 'accounting/' | relative_url }}) system for SAPs. It also requires annual review of each SAP and gives the Information Security Oversight Office access for oversight, although access may be tightly limited for extraordinarily sensitive programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: A high threshold for creation.">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">A high threshold for creation.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

That framework matters because it answers one common misunderstanding: “unacknowledged” does not mean “unknown to government”. It means the existence of the programme is not publicly confirmed and is restricted to authorised channels. “Waived” does not mean “unreviewable” either. Current Department of Defence instruction says the Secretary of Defense may designate selected [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}) as waived when ordinary congressional reporting would harm national security, but it also states that waived SAPs remain subject to congressional oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Routine reassessment.">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Routine reassessment.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>

The public-accountability problem is that legality becomes harder for outsiders to test. When a programme is acknowledged, the public may at least debate its broad purpose, cost category or policy implications. When it is unacknowledged, that wider debate may be impossible without exposing classified facts. When it is waived, even the normal classified reporting circle narrows. The legal check shifts from open argument to trust in a small group of cleared officials, internal lawyers, inspectors general, specialised auditors and congressional leaders.

That shift can be defensible, but it is not neutral. Public law often depends on adversarial testing: legislators ask questions, journalists investigate, civil society challenges assumptions, and courts or oversight bodies examine records. SAPs compress that accountability into closed rooms. The system can still work, but only if those closed-room actors have enough access, independence, time, technical competence and institutional courage to challenge the programme rather than merely receive briefings.

## The public-trust problem: secrecy protects capabilities, but also withholds reasons

The strongest case for SAP secrecy is practical. Some information is valuable precisely because adversaries do not know it exists: a collection method, a vulnerability, a [stealth]({{ 'stealth/' | relative_url }}) technology, a cyber capability, a sensitive operational plan, or the true performance of a weapon system. Executive Order 13526 reflects that logic by allowing SAPs only where the threat or vulnerability is exceptional and normal access rules are not enough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: A high threshold for creation.">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">A high threshold for creation.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

But democratic accountability is also practical. The public is asked to fund programmes, accept policy risks and trust officials who may not be able to explain what they are doing. That creates a legitimacy gap. A citizen cannot assess whether a secret programme is wise, proportionate or lawful if even its existence is denied. Congress may be informed through classified channels, but most members may still lack direct access to the most sensitive details, and the wider public cannot compare official claims with independent evidence.

This is why SAP accountability depends heavily on substitution. Since broad disclosure is unavailable, the system substitutes narrower mechanisms:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **cleared congressional access**, especially through defence and intelligence committees;
* **agency accounting systems and annual reviews**, required under classification rules;
* **DoD SAP governance bodies and central offices**, which track access, facilities, reporting and lifecycle decisions;
* **inspectors general, audit agencies and specialised review processes**, where cleared access can be arranged;
* **declassification and downgrading reviews**, when information no longer requires SAP-level protection.

</div>

The weakness of substitution is that each mechanism can fail quietly. A public hearing that goes badly is visible. A classified briefing that omits a key risk may not be. A public budget overrun can become a political issue. A disguised budget line may be questioned only by a small number of cleared staff. The trust problem is therefore not merely philosophical; it is a management risk created by limited visibility.

## Cost and duplication risks are not imaginary

SAPs are often discussed as if their main accountability risk is legality, but cost control may be the more persistent problem. GAO’s early-1990s review of special access acquisition programmes found that the programmes it sampled had been properly established and that their funds were subject to the same resource justification, execution, review and reprogramming processes as comparable non-SAP defence acquisition programmes. That is important: secrecy did not automatically mean money was uncontrolled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Independent cost and performance checks.">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Independent cost and performance checks.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

The same GAO review also found the other side of the tradeoff. DoD had provided less oversight over sampled SAPs than over similarly sized non-SAP acquisition programmes; waivers and exemptions limited review, documentation and reporting; and prior audits had found weak internal controls, poor internal communication and inadequate record-keeping. The sampled programmes experienced cost increases, schedule delays and performance problems similar to other defence acquisition programmes, meaning SAP status did not immunise them from ordinary procurement failure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Independent cost and performance checks.">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Independent cost and performance checks.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gao.gov.</span></span></span>

This matters because SAPs can make ordinary failure modes harder to detect. A classified acquisition programme may have fewer external reviewers, a smaller contractor pool, special facilities, bespoke information systems and barriers to sharing technical lessons across programmes. Even when all participants act in good faith, compartmentalisation can reduce the number of people able to spot [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}), challenge unrealistic assumptions or compare a secret effort with a less secret alternative.

There is also a broader government-efficiency issue. GAO’s long-running duplication and cost-savings work is built on the premise that overlap, fragmentation and duplication across federal programmes can waste money and weaken performance; by 2026, GAO reported 2,148 matters and recommendations from this body of work and about $774.3 billion in financial benefits from actions taken since 2011.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/duplication-cost-savings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Duplication &amp; Cost Savings &#124; U.S. GAO</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Duplication &amp; Cost Savings &#124; U.S. GAO</span></span></span> SAPs are not the central target of that public database, but they sit in tension with the same principle: duplication is easiest to find when programmes can be compared across offices, agencies and budget lines. SAP compartmentalisation narrows that comparison space.

The cost tradeoff is therefore not “classified programmes are wasteful”. It is sharper: SAPs may be necessary for security, but they increase the burden on internal portfolio management because public budget transparency and broad interagency comparison are reduced.

## Yellow Fruit shows why “trust us” was not enough

A useful accountability anchor is the “Yellow Fruit” episode, because it appears in official SAP training as a turning point rather than only in outside criticism. The Defense Counterintelligence and Security Agency’s SAP student guide says that after Yellow Fruit, congressional oversight increased significantly and reports on every DoD SAP became required annually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">SA001 Student Guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SA001 Student Guide</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that every SAP resembles Yellow Fruit. The lesson is that SAP oversight rules were strengthened because compartmentalised programmes had already shown vulnerability to weak controls and public suspicion. When a programme’s existence, funding and personnel channels are highly restricted, misconduct or mismanagement can be harder to detect through ordinary organisational friction. People outside the compartment may not know enough to ask the right question. People inside may lack independent reporting routes or may assume that secrecy itself prevents escalation.

That history helps explain why modern DoD SAP policy stresses centralised governance. The 2024 DoD SAP policy assigns the DoD SAP Central Office responsibilities that include annual SAP reviews and reports, records of SAP information provided to Congress, congressional SAP facility accreditation, and specialised processes for activities such as Defense Contract Audit Agency audits and joint inspections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507p</span></span></span> These are not decorative procedures. They are attempts to build accountability channels inside a secrecy regime that cannot rely on ordinary public contestation.

## Oversight without broad disclosure: what has to work

The central accountability challenge is to make secret oversight more than a ritual. A briefing is not the same as scrutiny. A report is not the same as independent verification. A cleared room is not automatically an accountable room.

For SAP oversight to compensate for secrecy, several conditions matter.

**First, access must be real, not nominal.** Oversight bodies need enough programme information to test legality, cost, schedule, performance and risk. DoD policy recognises this problem indirectly by requiring records of SAP information provided to Congress and by creating processes that allow specialised audit and inspection personnel to access information needed for their duties while limiting unnecessary detailed briefings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507p</span></span></span>

**Second, oversight must cover the whole lifecycle.** A SAP may be justified at creation but unnecessary later. DoD Instruction 5205.11 says SAP lifecycle planning should include criteria for disestablishing SAP compartments or moving critical programme information back to ordinary classified protections when SAP controls are no longer necessary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Routine reassessment.">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Routine reassessment.</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> This is crucial for accountability: secrecy should not become permanent merely because a programme began under exceptional conditions.

**Third, cost review must be portfolio-aware.** A single SAP may look justified in isolation while duplicating work elsewhere. The more compartmented the system, the more senior portfolio managers need mechanisms to compare SAP-protected capabilities with collateral programmes and with other SAPs. DoD’s 2024 directive assigns senior officials roles in science and technology alignment, acquisition portfolio reviews, policy integration and apportionment of SAP capabilities into operational planning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">520507p</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">520507p</span></span></span>

**Fourth, classification oversight must remain independent enough to matter.** ISOO’s public reports explain that it gathers statistical data on agency classification programmes and reports annually to the President, while Executive Order 13526 gives ISOO an oversight role over SAPs subject to programme security requirements.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives ISOO Reports &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives ISOO Reports &#124; National Archives</span></span></span> But the same order allows access to extraordinarily sensitive SAPs to be limited to the ISOO director alone. That may protect secrets, but it also shows how narrow the oversight aperture can become.

## The “waived SAP” dilemma is the sharpest version of the tradeoff

Waived SAPs concentrate the accountability issue because they are both unacknowledged and subject to restricted reporting. Under 10 U.S.C. §119, the Secretary of Defense may waive certain reporting requirements case by case if including the information in a report would adversely affect national security. DoD’s current instruction repeats that authority while stressing that waived SAPs remain subject to congressional oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span>

The policy logic is understandable. Some information may be so sensitive that even normal classified reporting creates unacceptable exposure: too many readers, too many storage points, too many staff processes, or too much risk that the existence of the programme will leak. For a genuinely exceptional capability or operation, a narrower channel may be the least bad option.

The accountability danger is equally clear. The narrower the channel, the more the system depends on a handful of people asking hard questions on behalf of everyone else. That creates several practical risks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Information asymmetry:** programme officials may know far more than the overseers and can frame the issue narrowly.
* **Capacity limits:** senior congressional leaders may be briefed on many sensitive matters and lack time for programme-level interrogation.
* **Staff exclusion:** elected leaders often rely on specialised staff, but the most restricted programmes may limit which staff can assist.
* **Weak public correction:** if a waived SAP is misguided, the public may not learn enough to challenge it until years later, if ever.

</div>

The right question is therefore not whether waived SAPs should exist in theory. It is whether each waiver is genuinely necessary, periodically reassessed, and accompanied by compensating checks strong enough to replace the ordinary reporting that has been removed.

## Classification can create operational costs, not just secrecy benefits

SAPs are usually justified as protection against compromise, but heavy classification can also make programmes slower, costlier and less innovative. A 2023 article in Air University’s Æther journal argued that overclassification, limited access to modern information technology systems for contracting professionals, and barriers to small-business participation were harming US Space Force procurement speed, cost, innovation and international partnerships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Portals/10/AEtherJournal/Journals/Special-Edition_Winter2023/James.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airuniversity.af.edu">[Air University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airuniversity.af.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Air University The Cost of Space System Classification</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air University The Cost of Space System Classification</span></span></span>

That point is especially relevant to SAPs because they often require specialised facilities, [access lists]({{ 'access-lists/' | relative_url }}), information systems and contracting arrangements. Those controls may be necessary, but they are not free. They can reduce competition by excluding firms without cleared facilities. They can slow acquisition by limiting who can review requirements or contract documents. They can make allied cooperation harder when a partner has a relevant capability but cannot be given enough programme detail. They can also discourage technical challenge because the people best placed to identify a flaw may not be read into the programme.

This is not an argument for casual disclosure. It is an argument for proportional secrecy. If a SAP protects a real operational advantage, the cost may be justified. If it protects information long after the exceptional vulnerability has passed, it may become a drag on performance and accountability at the same time.

## What accountable secrecy looks like in practice

Accountable secrecy is not the absence of secrecy. It is a disciplined system for deciding what must remain secret, who may know it, how long the restriction should last, and how outsiders with proper authority can test the programme’s claims.

For SAPs, the most credible model has several features:



<div class="youtube-embed-container youtube-embed-fallback"><div class="youtube-embed-card"><div class="youtube-embed-frame"><iframe src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/vt7eHgiEB_Q" title="How the Government Hides Programs for Decades" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" allowfullscreen></iframe></div><div class="youtube-embed-footer"><p class="youtube-embed-title"><a href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vt7eHgiEB_Q" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">How the Government Hides Programs for Decades</a></p><p class="youtube-embed-meta">Channel: Behind the Curtain (Declassified)</p><p class="youtube-embed-actions"><a class="youtube-embed-watch-link" href="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vt7eHgiEB_Q" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer" title="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vt7eHgiEB_Q">Open on YouTube</a></p></div></div></div>

1. **A high threshold for creation.** Executive Order 13526 requires an exceptional vulnerability or threat and a finding that normal access rules are insufficient. That threshold should be treated as a substantive test, not a paperwork formula. [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html)

2. **Routine reassessment.** Annual review should ask whether SAP status remains necessary, whether compartments can be collapsed, and whether information can move to normal classified channels. DoD policy’s lifecycle language on disestablishment and transfer to collateral protection points in this direction. [ESD](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf)

3. **Independent cost and performance checks.** GAO’s historical findings show that SAPs can experience the same cost, schedule and performance problems as ordinary acquisition programmes while also facing weaker review and documentation in some cases. [gao.gov](https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf) That makes cleared audit access and portfolio comparison essential.

4. **Congressional access that includes expertise.** Senior members matter, but complex programmes often require staff, auditors and technical specialists. The narrower the reporting channel, the more deliberate the system must be about giving cleared experts enough access to assist meaningful oversight.
5. **Records of what was disclosed to whom.** DoD’s 2024 directive requires the SAP Central Office to maintain records of DoD SAP information provided to Congress and to accredit congressional SAP facilities. [ESD](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf) Such records matter because accountability can fail when no one can later reconstruct what overseers were actually told.
