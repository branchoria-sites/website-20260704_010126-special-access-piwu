## Introduction

Military organisations can classify documents, restrict access to planning systems and compartment sensitive [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}), yet still reveal valuable information through entirely public data. Modern operational security is increasingly challenged by open-source information such as fitness-app records, aircraft tracking websites, satellite imagery and publicly visible movement patterns. None of these sources necessarily expose classified documents, but together they can reveal what analysts call a "pattern of life"—the routines, timing and movements that allow observers to infer sensitive military activity. This creates a particular challenge for especially sensitive operations and special-access programmes, where the mission itself may remain classified even while supporting activities leave a public digital footprint.

## What the Strava heat-map incident showed

The best-known example emerged in early 2018, when researchers and journalists realised that the global heat map published by the fitness application Strava highlighted exercise routes around remote military installations. In densely populated cities, millions of users blended together. ## Why aircraft tracking worries military operators

Aircraft present a similar challenge. Modern aviation relies heavily on systems such as Automatic Dependent Surveillance–Broadcast (ADS-B), through which aircraft broadcast their position, altitude and identity to support safe air traffic management. Large volunteer networks collect these signals and make much of the resulting information publicly accessible.

Many military aircraft either disable these transmissions during sensitive operations or use other procedures when operational requirements permit. Nevertheless, transport aircraft, aerial refuelling tankers, government aircraft and support flights are often visible for at least part of their journeys. Even when individual flights reveal nothing remarkable, repeated observation can expose broader operational patterns.

For example, open-source observers have documented:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Increased transport activity into particular air bases before major exercises.
* Regular tanker or logistics flights supporting overseas deployments.
* Changes in operating tempo suggesting the start or conclusion of sustained operations.
* Temporary concentrations of aircraft at staging locations.

</div>

None of these observations automatically reveal classified missions, and responsible reporting should avoid drawing unwarranted conclusions. However, repeated collection over weeks or months can allow analysts to identify trends that individual flight records would not reveal.

The rapid growth of crowdsourced flight-tracking networks illustrates how capabilities once limited to governments have become widely available to researchers, journalists and hobbyists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06254" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OpenSky Report 2025: Improving Crowdsourced Flight Trajectories with ADS-C DataMay 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## How public clues complicate operational secrecy

The central operational-security problem is not any single dataset but the combination of many independent public sources.

An analyst investigating military activity today might compare:

* Commercial satellite imagery.
* Public aircraft movements.
* Ship-position broadcasts where applicable. * Fitness-app activity.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.lemonde.fr/en/videos/article/2026/03/30/stravaleaks-how-le-monde-located-18-000-french-military-personnel-with-a-fitness-app_6751933_108.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lemonde.fr">[lemonde.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lemonde.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By identifying 18,599 user profiles active in or near around 100 French military bases, both domestically and abroad, Le Monde tracked mi...</span></span></span> * Social media photographs.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.wired.com/story/strava-heat-map-military-bases-fitness-trackers-privacy/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[wired.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Strava Heat Map and the End of SecretsJan 29, 2018 — The US military is reexamining security policies after fitness tracker data shar...</span></span></span>
* Procurement notices.
* Public [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}).
* Weather conditions.
* Local news reports.

Each source may be incomplete or ambiguous. Together they can produce increasingly reliable assessments about force posture, deployment timing or support activity.

Operational-security doctrine has long recognised this phenomenon through the concept of indicators—observable actions that allow adversaries to infer intentions even without access to classified information. Digital platforms dramatically increase both the quantity and persistence of those indicators. Information that previously disappeared after a patrol or training run may now remain searchable for years.

This means that secrecy increasingly depends not only on protecting classified documents but also on managing the cumulative exposure created by ordinary digital behaviour.

## The continuing evolution of open-source intelligence

The Strava case proved to be an early warning rather than a one-off anomaly. Subsequent reporting has continued to demonstrate how location-sharing services can expose sensitive government activity when users fail to apply appropriate privacy settings or follow operational guidance.

Investigations have identified additional examples involving military personnel at sensitive facilities, including public activity associated with Israeli security locations and later reporting concerning French military personnel, naval deployments and strategic bases. Although the precise operational significance of individual cases varies, together they illustrate that the underlying issue is persistent rather than confined to a single application or country.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strava" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

These episodes also show that technological change continually creates new sources of observable data. Commercial applications are often designed for convenience or social sharing rather than security, meaning risks may emerge long after systems have become widely adopted.

## Why this matters for sensitive military operations

For highly sensitive operations, protecting plans increasingly requires protecting the digital environment surrounding those plans. Mission secrecy can be undermined not by a classified document being stolen but by thousands of seemingly insignificant public observations that, when combined, reveal the existence, timing or support structure of an activity.

The principal lesson from open-source tracking incidents is therefore broader than any individual platform. Modern operational security must account for commercially generated location data, publicly available movement records and other digital traces that exist outside traditional classified information systems. In an era of powerful open-source [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}), the distinction between classified information and publicly observable evidence has become far less important than the ability to assemble many small clues into a coherent operational picture.
