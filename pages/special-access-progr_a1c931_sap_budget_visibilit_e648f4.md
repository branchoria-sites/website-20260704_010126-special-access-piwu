## Introduction

Special-access-programme budgets are not invisible to [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}), but they are visible through a narrow, classified channel rather than through the public budget documents most citizens, journalists and many legislators can read. Under 10 U.S.C. §119, the Secretary of Defense must give the congressional defence committees annual SAP budget information, including the total amount requested, programme descriptions, [milestones]({{ 'milestones/' | relative_url }}), past actual costs, and estimated costs for the budget year and following years. The same statute, however, also lets the Secretary withhold particular details case by case when including them would harm national security, with the withheld information and justification provided only to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_sap_budget_visibilit_e648f4-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Budgets" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That is the core tension of SAP budget visibility. The system is designed to let elected oversight occur without exposing sensitive capabilities, locations, methods or vulnerabilities. Yet the more a programme is compartmented, especially if it is unacknowledged or waived, the harder it becomes for anyone outside a very small circle to compare costs, spot [duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}), test assumptions, or challenge spending before it becomes locked in.

## What Congress is supposed to see

The most important public fact about SAP budgeting is that the law does not simply allow the Pentagon to hide SAP spending from Congress. The annual report required by 10 U.S.C. §119 must include the total amount requested for Department of Defense SAPs in the President’s next budget, and for each SAP it must include a brief description, major milestones, actual costs for past fiscal years, estimated total cost, the current-year estimate, the budget-year estimate, and estimates for four succeeding years where the programme is expected to continue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

That reporting model tries to solve a specific oversight problem: a budget line that says only “classified activity” is not enough to let Congress judge affordability. The [statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}) therefore asks for both totals and programme-level detail. It also requires notice and justification for each new SAP, including the current estimate of total programme cost and identification of similar existing programmes or technologies. That similarity requirement matters because duplication is one of the recurring risks in highly compartmented systems: two parts of the government can pursue overlapping work without ordinary cross-programme comparison.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

The visibility is still deliberately limited. SAP reports go to the armed services and appropriations committees and their defence subcommittees, not to the full public record. For waived SAPs, the restriction is tighter: normal reporting details can be withheld if the Secretary of Defense determines, case by case, that inclusion would adversely affect national security, with the information and waiver justification provided only to the chair and ranking minority member of each defence committee.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span> The Federation of American Scientists describes waived SAPs as the most sensitive DoD SAPs and notes that only eight senior members of the congressional defence committees may be advised of such programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fas.org/publication/dod_saps/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fas.org">[Federation of American Scientists]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federation of American Scientists Spotlight on Do D Special Access Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federation of American ScientistsSpotlight on DoD Special Access Programs - Federation of American Scientists...</span></span></span>

## Budget totals can exist while useful detail stays hidden

A common misunderstanding is that secrecy means there are no numbers. In reality, SAP budget oversight often separates **aggregate visibility** from **operationally revealing detail**. Congress may receive classified totals and programme estimates, while the public sees little or nothing that can be tied to a specific capability. This is why “black budget” debates can be confusing: a number can be known inside government, partly known to select legislators, estimated by outside researchers, or disclosed only in broad categories, while the programme logic behind it remains unavailable.

The public record shows how this split works. A 2025 Government [Accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) Office review of the Pacific Deterrence Initiative found that some funded requirements used in Indo-Pacific planning were absent from the public-facing PDI budget exhibit. One example was a next-generation national defence space architecture research and development programme, identified as a special access programme, totalling $1.3 billion in fiscal year 2025. GAO reported that those programmes were not included in the PDI budget exhibit because DoD guidance suggested that SAPs should be excluded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107698/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[files.gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">26-107698. DEFENSE BUDGET: Clearer Guidance Is Needed to Improve Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts...</span></span></span>

That example is valuable because it shows the problem without needing to know the classified substance of the programme. The money was real enough to appear in an independent assessment of Indo-Pacific requirements, but the normal budget exhibit intended to help Congress and readers track Pacific deterrence resources did not present it. GAO concluded that unclear guidance and inconsistent selection processes reduced budget transparency and Congress’s ability to exercise oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107698/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[files.gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">26-107698. DEFENSE BUDGET: Clearer Guidance Is Needed to Improve Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts...</span></span></span>

This is not proof that the programme was improper. It is evidence of a visibility gap: a programme can be funded, strategically relevant, and significant in size, yet still disappear from the budget tool a reader might expect to use to understand a regional defence initiative. For SAPs, that is often the practical compromise: secrecy protects the capability, but it can also make the spending picture fragmented.

## Where cost growth risks enter

SAPs are not automatically more wasteful than ordinary programmes. The cost risk comes from the environment around them: fewer people can see the programme, fewer auditors may have access, fewer outside comparisons are possible, and public pressure is largely absent. When the same programme is technically ambitious, contractor-heavy, or shielded by compartmented contracting arrangements, the normal warning signs of cost growth can be harder to detect early.

GAO’s older work on SAP acquisition oversight shows why this concern has a long history. In a 1992 unclassified summary of a classified review, GAO reported that contract administration for special access acquisition programmes had frequently been cited by auditors and inspectors as inadequate. It also described earlier DoD use of “carve-out” contracting, a practice that could keep Defense Investigative Service investigators from reviewing SAP contracts, though [approval authority]({{ 'approval-authority/' | relative_url }}) for such arrangements had been tightened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-93-78 Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased...</span></span></span>

The same GAO summary noted that the Defense Contract Audit Agency had increased its ability to audit SAPs by obtaining necessary clearances for about one-third of its auditors. That detail is important: audit capacity in this field is not just a question of accounting skill. Auditors need access, clearances, programme context, and enough information to test whether costs are allowable, reasonable and properly allocated. GAO also reported that services had not always requested DCAA audit services as often as they should have and had not always provided auditors all relevant SAP information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-93-78 Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased...</span></span></span>

More recent GAO work on special operations acquisitions, although not limited to SAPs, illustrates the same budget-control problem in a sensitive acquisition environment. In 2026, GAO found that SOCOM’s acquisition portal did not contain total cost estimate data for eight programmes that were supposed to maintain approved acquisition programme baselines; seven could provide latest estimates, while two could not do so until months later. GAO warned that without current cost estimates, officials may lack a full picture of cost-growth risk or opportunities to reallocate resources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108036.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 26 108036</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao 26 108036</span></span></span>

The broader lesson for SAPs is straightforward: secrecy does not eliminate the need for baseline discipline. A compartmented programme still needs current estimates, actual-cost tracking, milestone comparison, and independent challenge. If those controls are weak, classification can turn ordinary acquisition drift into a harder-to-see problem.

## Who inside the system is meant to challenge spending

SAP budget scrutiny is supposed to come from a layered system rather than from open public debate. DoD’s 2024 SAP policy directive establishes a governance structure for oversight and management of DoD SAPs, including the Special Access Program Oversight Committee, Senior Review Group, SAP Senior Working Group and DoD SAP Central Office.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> These bodies are meant to create internal visibility across programmes that, by design, cannot be freely discussed across the wider department.

The comptroller role is especially important for budget visibility. DoD Directive 5205.07 says the Under Secretary of Defense (Comptroller)/Chief Financial Officer leads budget and execution reviews for DoD-funded capabilities and information activities protected by SAPs, and provides budget-call guidance for SAP submissions, reprogramming actions, mid-year reviews and other execution reviews.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> That means SAP money is not supposed to float outside the department’s resource process; it is supposed to be reviewed through a classified version of that process.

Independent cost analysis is another internal check. The same directive says the Director of Cost Assessment and Program Evaluation serves as principal adviser to senior DoD officials on the cost of capabilities protected by SAPs, evaluates estimated costs and resource constraints, helps develop guidance for programme funding requirements, and prepares instructions for SAP programme objective memorandum submissions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span> In plain terms, SAPs still need people whose job is to ask whether the proposed cost makes sense.

Congress is the external constitutional check, but its effective challenge depends on access, staffing and usable information. The law gives defence committees SAP cost and milestone data, and it prevents a SAP from being initiated until the defence committees are notified and 30 days have elapsed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span> For the most sensitive waived programmes, however, the audience narrows sharply. That raises a practical question: a handful of senior legislators may be informed, but can they realistically test programme affordability, contractor performance, duplication and alternatives without broader cleared staff support?

## The hardest visibility problem is not a missing total

The most difficult SAP budget question is usually not “is there a number somewhere?” It is “who can connect the number to enough context to judge it?” A total programme estimate is useful only if an authorised reviewer can compare it with requirements, alternatives, milestones, actual costs, technical risk and similar programmes.

The 10 U.S.C. §119 reporting framework recognises this by requiring more than one figure: it asks for descriptions, milestones, prior actual costs, estimated total cost and future-year estimates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span> But those data are only as useful as their accuracy and circulation. If a programme’s estimates are stale, if comparable programmes are hidden in other [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}), or if oversight staff cannot ask follow-up questions, formal reporting can become compliance rather than real budget control.

GAO’s 2025 Pacific Deterrence Initiative finding shows this at portfolio level. A $1.3 billion SAP could be excluded from one budget exhibit while appearing in a different strategic assessment, making it harder to understand the full funded picture for Indo-Pacific priorities. GAO warned that this kind of misalignment can send mixed messages to Congress and complicate oversight of whether resources match strategic goals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107698/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[files.gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">26-107698. DEFENSE BUDGET: Clearer Guidance Is Needed to Improve Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts...</span></span></span>

GAO’s 2026 SOCOM acquisition review shows the same problem at programme-management level. Even where officials had a portal intended for monitoring acquisition programmes, GAO found that current cost information was not consistently maintained, and that better access to updated estimates would help officials answer congressional requests, determine consistency with budget priorities and identify programmes needing special attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108036.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 26 108036</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao 26 108036</span></span></span> For SAPs, where the pool of authorised reviewers is already smaller, stale or incomplete data can be especially damaging.

## Why secrecy can protect value and obscure waste at the same time

There is a legitimate reason SAP budgets are not fully public. Revealing a programme’s precise spending pattern can expose more than money. It may reveal the maturity of a weapon system, the existence of a sensor, the tempo of testing, the location of infrastructure, the scale of contractor involvement or the priority attached to a capability. For unacknowledged programmes, even confirming that a budget line belongs to a specific effort may reveal the existence of the effort itself.

But secrecy also has costs. The Moynihan Commission’s 1997 report framed secrecy as a form of government regulation with real consequences when policymakers are not fully informed and the government cannot be held accountable in normal ways. Its contents explicitly included “the intangible costs of secrecy” and efforts to quantify secrecy’s costs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sgp.fas.org/library/moynihan/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sgp.fas.org">[sgp.fas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sgp.fas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Report of the Commission on Protecting and Reducing Government Secrecy</span></span></span> That point applies sharply to SAP budgeting: the protective value of secrecy must be weighed against the governance cost of narrowing who can see, compare and challenge spending.

The best reading of the public evidence is therefore neither “SAPs are unauditable slush funds” nor “the existing controls solve the problem”. The law and DoD policy create real oversight channels. GAO and inspector-general history show that those channels can improve audit access and contract administration. But the same record shows recurring weaknesses: excluded budget exhibits, incomplete data, limited audit access, unclear responsibilities and narrow congressional notification for waived programmes.

## What credible budget visibility would look like

A workable SAP budget system does not require public disclosure of operational details. It requires enough classified visibility for authorised officials to test whether the money is justified. The strongest safeguards are practical rather than rhetorical:

* **Reliable classified totals and programme estimates.** Congress needs the aggregate SAP request, but also programme-level estimates, prior actual costs and future-year projections, as 10 U.S.C. §119 requires.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>
* **Current cost baselines.** Programmes need live comparisons between approved baselines and current estimates, because stale cost data can conceal growth until choices have narrowed. GAO’s 2026 SOCOM findings show how weak data maintenance can undermine monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/gao-26-108036.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 26 108036</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">gao 26 108036</span></span></span>
* **Independent cost challenge.** The Director of Cost Assessment and Program Evaluation has a formal SAP role precisely because programme offices should not be the only source of cost judgement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on whs.mil.</span></span></span>
* **Cleared audit capacity.** GAO’s 1992 review showed that SAP audit coverage improved when DCAA obtained clearances and oversight bodies gained access, but also that audit requests and information-sharing had been inconsistent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[GAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSIAD-93-78 Defense Acquisition: Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased...</span></span></span>
* **Portfolio-level reconciliation.** When SAPs are excluded from ordinary budget exhibits, there still needs to be a classified way to reconcile regional, mission or capability portfolios so Congress can understand the full funded picture. GAO’s PDI review shows why this matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107698/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.gao.gov">[files.gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">26-107698. DEFENSE BUDGET: Clearer Guidance Is Needed to Improve Visibility into Resourcing of Pacific Deterrence Efforts...</span></span></span>
* **A meaningful route to challenge waived programmes.** Waiver authority may be necessary for the most sensitive activities, but its oversight value depends on whether the small group informed has access to enough expert staff, audit findings and cost analysis to ask hard questions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/119" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Legal Information Institute10 U.S. Code § 119</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access programs: congressional oversight &#124; U.S. Code &#124; US Law &#124; LII / Legal Information Institute...</span></span></span>

## The real audit question

“Can secret programmes be audited?” is not a yes-or-no question. They can be audited if auditors, cost analysts and authorised legislators are read into the right compartments, given current data, and allowed to compare claims against [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}), baselines and alternatives. They become functionally difficult to audit when secrecy is paired with incomplete records, narrow access, weak update rules or fragmented budget displays.

The public evidence points to a guarded but critical conclusion: SAP budget secrecy is not the absence of oversight, but it is a stress test for oversight. The system depends on a small number of cleared people having unusually good information. When they do, secrecy and accountability can coexist. When they do not, the same compartments that protect national security can also shield cost growth, duplication and poor spending decisions from timely challenge.
