## Introduction

Hunt Forward [Operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}) illustrate one of the sharpest tensions in modern cyber policy: the same information that makes everyone safer can also reduce the value of sensitive cyber capabilities. Conducted at the invitation of partner governments, these operations place defensive cyber teams inside allied networks to identify malicious activity, remove threats and share indicators that improve collective security. Yet the [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) gathered during those missions may also reveal how hostile actors operate, what vulnerabilities they exploit, and how they are being monitored. Publicly releasing some of that information strengthens global cyber defence, while withholding other details preserves intelligence sources, operational access and future options. This balance—between rapid disclosure for defence and continued secrecy for operational advantage—is the disclosure paradox that makes Hunt Forward Operations particularly relevant to cyber capability special-access programmes (SAPs).

Unlike traditional classified military capabilities, [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) advantages can disappear almost immediately once technical details become widely known. Hunt Forward therefore sits at the intersection of two competing public interests: increasing collective resilience through information sharing while protecting sensitive methods whose exposure could undermine future defensive or intelligence missions.

## What hunt-forward operations reveal for defence

Hunt Forward Operations are a core element of the United States' "defend forward" cyber strategy. They are described by United States Cyber Command as defensive missions conducted only at the request of partner nations. Teams deploy onto host-government networks to search for malicious activity, identify vulnerabilities, analyse malware and share findings with the host so that remediation can occur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cybercom.mil/Media/News/Article/3390470/shared-threats-shared-understanding-us-canada-and-latvia-conclude-defensive-hun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cybercom.mil">[cybercom.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cybercom.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 10, 2023 — 10 May 2023 — A team of cyber experts from US Cyber Command&#x27;s Cyber National Mission Force (CNMF) recently returned from a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2023</span></span></span>

The distinctive value of these missions lies in their ability to discover adversary behaviour before it reaches American or allied networks elsewhere. Rather than waiting for an attack at home, analysts examine compromised or targeted foreign networks where hostile actors are already operating. Intelligence collected in one country can therefore improve defensive measures across many others.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2024/05/CyCon_2024_Kosseff-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccdcoe.org">[ccdcoe.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccdcoe.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ns are “strictly defensive operations” that Cyber Command conducts “at the request of partner nations.”.Read more...</span></span></span>

Public examples demonstrate this pattern:

* A Hunt Forward mission in Ukraine before the 2022 Russian invasion helped identify malicious activity on critical networks and supported Ukrainian defenders immediately before destructive cyber operations began alongside the military invasion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/sites/default/files/documents/rmsj3h-751x3/2022-11-28-CNMF-Before-the-Invasion-Hunt-Forward-Operations-in-Ukraine.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsarchive.gwu.edu">[National Security Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsarchive.gwu.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cyber professionals began a meticulous multi-prong hunt looking for suspected malicious cyber activity.Read more...</span></span></span>
* Joint operations with Latvia and Canada generated shared indicators of compromise and adversary tactics while strengthening local resilience against future attacks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cybercom.mil/Media/News/Article/3390470/shared-threats-shared-understanding-us-canada-and-latvia-conclude-defensive-hun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cybercom.mil">[cybercom.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cybercom.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 10, 2023 — 10 May 2023 — A team of cyber experts from US Cyber Command&#x27;s Cyber National Mission Force (CNMF) recently returned from a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2023</span></span></span>
* Senior Cyber Command officials have stated that Hunt Forward deployments have produced malware samples and technical intelligence later released publicly so software vendors, governments and network defenders could improve protection worldwide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2024/05/CyCon_2024_Kosseff-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccdcoe.org">[ccdcoe.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccdcoe.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ns are “strictly defensive operations” that Cyber Command conducts “at the request of partner nations.”.Read more...</span></span></span>

This model reflects an important change in cyber defence. Instead of treating intelligence solely as something to be consumed within classified systems, Hunt Forward frequently converts technical discoveries into defensive knowledge that benefits a much wider community.

## Why offensive access may require silence

The same operations that encourage information sharing also generate information whose disclosure could reduce future operational effectiveness.

Public descriptions of Hunt Forward emphasise that findings are used to expose adversary tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs), identify malware and strengthen resilience. Yet those findings may also reveal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* how intelligence services recognised the intrusion;
* which indicators led analysts to hidden malware;
* what intelligence collection capabilities first detected the activity;
* which classes of vulnerabilities remain useful for future monitoring;
* how long adversaries remained undetected before discovery.

</div>

Releasing every technical detail would improve transparency but could simultaneously teach hostile operators how to evade future detection.

This creates an implementation dilemma rather than a simple classification decision. Defensive organisations generally benefit from publishing indicators of compromise, malware signatures and recommended mitigations as quickly as possible. Intelligence and military organisations, however, may judge that some supporting details should remain classified because they expose ongoing collection methods or sensitive operational knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2024/05/CyCon_2024_Kosseff-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ccdcoe.org">[ccdcoe.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ccdcoe.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ns are “strictly defensive operations” that Cyber Command conducts “at the request of partner nations.”.Read more...</span></span></span>

Within the broader context of cyber capability SAPs, this distinction is particularly significant. The protected secret is often not merely a software exploit but an intelligence ecosystem that includes collection sources, analytic methods, operational timing and partner relationships. Even if malware samples become public, preserving those surrounding capabilities may justify continued classification of related material.

## How shared indicators can conflict with protected capabilities

The disclosure paradox becomes most visible when deciding exactly what should be shared after a Hunt Forward mission.

Three broad categories of information often emerge.

**Information that strongly favours disclosure.** Malware hashes, network indicators, defensive signatures, observed command-and-control infrastructure and recommended mitigations help defenders worldwide. Their value generally increases as they are distributed rapidly across governments, industry and security vendors. Cyber Command has repeatedly described Hunt Forward as producing insights intended to improve collective defence through broader sharing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cybercom.mil/Media/News/Article/3390470/shared-threats-shared-understanding-us-canada-and-latvia-conclude-defensive-hun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cybercom.mil">[cybercom.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cybercom.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 10, 2023 — 10 May 2023 — A team of cyber experts from US Cyber Command&#x27;s Cyber National Mission Force (CNMF) recently returned from a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2023</span></span></span>

**Information that may require continued protection.** Intelligence about how adversaries were first discovered, classified collection sources, operational planning, partner-specific network architecture and unique analytical methods may remain sensitive long after defensive indicators have been released. Disclosure could enable future evasion even if immediate threats have been removed.

**Information requiring selective release.** Some material falls between these extremes. Governments may publish technical indicators while withholding contextual information that would identify intelligence capabilities or reveal the extent of existing access to adversary activity.

The resulting process resembles the broader policy logic behind vulnerability disclosure decisions. The objective is not maximum secrecy or maximum openness, but determining which specific information produces greater overall security when disclosed and which remains operationally valuable only while protected.

## Why partner trust shapes disclosure decisions

Hunt Forward depends fundamentally upon consent from partner nations. Governments invite foreign cyber specialists onto selected national networks because they expect both technical assistance and responsible handling of sensitive information. Publicly releasing findings therefore involves more than technical judgement; it also requires maintaining diplomatic trust.

Partners may have different priorities. One government may favour rapid publication of malware indicators to strengthen international resilience. Another may worry that extensive disclosure could expose weaknesses in its critical infrastructure or reveal intelligence cooperation that it prefers to keep confidential.

Public descriptions of Hunt Forward consistently stress collaborative analysis, partner-directed network selection and joint decision-making regarding defensive remediation. This cooperative model helps explain why disclosure decisions are often negotiated rather than automatic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cybercom.mil/Media/News/Article/3390470/shared-threats-shared-understanding-us-canada-and-latvia-conclude-defensive-hun/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cybercom.mil">[cybercom.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cybercom.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 10, 2023 — 10 May 2023 — A team of cyber experts from US Cyber Command&#x27;s Cyber National Mission Force (CNMF) recently returned from a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 2023</span></span></span>

For cyber capability SAPs, trusted partnerships become another protected asset. Revealing too much about how operations are conducted, where they occur or what access partners provide could discourage future cooperation, reducing opportunities to detect emerging threats before they reach other allied networks.

## Why the paradox is likely to persist

The growth of Hunt Forward Operations suggests that governments increasingly view forward defensive activity and rapid information sharing as complementary elements of cyber resilience. At the same time, the expansion of these missions increases the volume of sensitive operational knowledge generated through allied cooperation.

This means the disclosure paradox is unlikely to disappear. Successful cyber defence increasingly depends on publishing actionable technical information quickly enough for defenders to act. Successful intelligence and military cyber operations often depend on preserving enough secrecy that adversaries cannot immediately adapt.

Hunt Forward demonstrates that these objectives are not mutually exclusive, but neither are they perfectly aligned. The practical challenge is to separate information whose public release strengthens collective defence from information whose continued protection preserves future defensive and intelligence capabilities. That distinction explains why some cyber secrets are deliberately shared while others remain closely held within highly protected programmes.
