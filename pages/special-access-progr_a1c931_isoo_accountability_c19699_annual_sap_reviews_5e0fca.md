## Introduction

Special Access Programs (SAPs) are intended to provide exceptional protection for exceptionally sensitive national security information—not to create permanent [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) that survive through institutional habit. For that reason, the United States classification system requires agencies to conduct recurring reviews of both their classification programmes and, where SAPs exist, the continuing justification for maintaining those programmes under enhanced controls. The annual review process is therefore a critical [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) mechanism. It asks a simple but consequential question: does this programme still require extraordinary protection, or has the original justification weakened, changed or disappeared? Executive Order 13526, ISOO implementing directives, and agency self-inspection requirements all reflect the principle that enhanced secrecy should remain exceptional, proportionate and continually justified rather than automatically renewed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security...August 15, 2016 — This Directive sets forth guidance to agenc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## When should a SAP stop being special?

The governing principle is straightforward. A SAP exists because ordinary classification controls are judged insufficient to protect particularly vulnerable information. If that judgement is no longer accurate, the legal and policy basis for maintaining special access becomes weaker.

Executive Order 13526 establishes that special access programmes should be kept to an "absolute minimum" and used only where normal access controls are inadequate. At the same time, the order requires agencies to review classification guidance to ensure it reflects current circumstances and to identify information that no longer requires protection. Information must be declassified as soon as it no longer satisfies the standards for classification. Although the order distinguishes between declassification decisions and SAP administration, both requirements embody the same policy logic: protection should be continually revalidated rather than presumed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security Information29 Dec 2009 — Agencies conducting a mandatory review for declassification shall decl...</span></span></span>

This means that annual SAP reviews are not intended merely to confirm administrative compliance. They should test whether the original reasons for extraordinary compartmentation still exist.

## The legal test for keeping SAP status

Annual reviewers are not deciding whether a programme remains important. They are deciding whether it still requires exceptional access restrictions beyond those already provided by Top Secret, Secret or Confidential classification.

In practice, reviewers should reassess several related questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Has the information retained the exceptional vulnerability that originally justified a SAP?
* Would disclosure today create the level of damage originally anticipated?
* Are ordinary classified handling rules now sufficient?
* Has the technology become operationally routine or widely distributed within government?
* Has the threat environment changed?
* Has equivalent information become publicly available or overtly acknowledged?
* Does maintaining the compartment continue to produce security benefits that outweigh its administrative and operational costs?

</div>

[ISOO]({{ 'isoo/' | relative_url }})'s implementing directive reinforces this approach by requiring agencies with SAPs to include those programmes within their annual self-inspection processes. Those [inspections]({{ 'inspections/' | relative_url }}) are expected to evaluate management, safeguarding, classification practices, declassification activities, training, security violations and overall programme oversight rather than assuming that existing arrangements remain appropriate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security...August 15, 2016 — This Directive sets forth guidance to agenc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## Questions annual reviewers should answer

A meaningful annual review extends well beyond checking paperwork.

### Is the original threat still current?

Many SAPs are established because an adversary lacks knowledge of a capability or because premature disclosure would significantly undermine an operation or technology. Years later, that assumption may no longer be true.

Capabilities may have entered regular military service. Foreign [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) services may already understand their existence. Scientific developments may have reduced the uniqueness of once-sensitive technologies. These changes do not automatically justify terminating a SAP, but they require reviewers to reconsider whether extraordinary access restrictions remain proportionate.

### Has the programme expanded beyond necessity?

Executive Order 13526 emphasises that SAPs should remain limited exceptions. Reviewers therefore should examine whether additional compartments, sub-compartments or access restrictions have accumulated over time without a clear security rationale.

Compartment growth often occurs gradually. New security requirements may be added in response to isolated incidents or evolving organisational practices. Unless periodically challenged, these additions can become permanent despite offering diminishing security value.

### Are overlapping compartments creating duplication?

Large defence and intelligence organisations frequently manage numerous compartmented programmes simultaneously.

Annual reviews provide an opportunity to identify:

* duplicate security structures;
* overlapping access controls;
* [multiple SAPs]({{ 'multiple-saps/' | relative_url }}) protecting substantially similar information; and
* opportunities to merge or simplify compartments without increasing security risk.

Reducing unnecessary duplication improves both security governance and operational efficiency because fewer compartments generally mean fewer administrative barriers to legitimate information sharing.

## How obsolete compartments persist

The greatest challenge is rarely identifying an obsolete compartment in theory. It is overcoming the organisational forces that make retirement difficult.

Several recurring patterns encourage unnecessary continuation.

**Institutional inertia.** Once personnel, facilities, security procedures and reporting structures are built around a SAP, dismantling them requires effort and leadership attention. Continuing the programme often becomes administratively easier than justifying change.

**Risk asymmetry.** Decision-makers may perceive greater personal risk in ending a compartment than in maintaining it. If protection is reduced and a future disclosure occurs, reviewers may fear criticism. By contrast, unnecessary continuation usually produces diffuse costs that are less immediately visible.

**Loss of historical knowledge.** Personnel turnover can leave reviewers without a complete understanding of why a SAP was originally established. Without clear historical documentation, existing restrictions may simply be treated as inherited requirements.

**Mission expansion.** Programmes frequently evolve beyond their original purpose. New technologies, missions or partner organisations become attached to an existing compartment, making it progressively harder to determine whether the entire structure still deserves enhanced protection.

These pressures help explain why periodic review is a substantive oversight requirement rather than a procedural formality.

## Why annual review matters for accountability

The annual review process serves as one of the few recurring opportunities to challenge accumulated secrecy before it becomes permanent.

ISOO's oversight framework depends heavily upon agency self-inspections conducted at least annually. Agencies with SAPs are specifically expected to evaluate those programmes as part of broader classification management reviews. These inspections examine not only security compliance but also management effectiveness and adherence to Executive Order 13526.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security...August 15, 2016 — This Directive sets forth guidance to agenc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

Public reporting suggests that this oversight function has practical importance. Following a National Security Council request for information concerning active SAPs and Controlled Access Programs, ISOO reported identifying inconsistencies in agency compliance with Executive Order 13526 and 32 CFR Part 2001. ISOO recommended re-evaluating the criteria used for establishing and maintaining such programmes and called for additional oversight to ensure agencies were administering them appropriately. That finding indicates that questions about continuing justification are not merely theoretical but remain active governance concerns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/isoo-implementing-directive.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives32 CFR Parts 2001 and 2003 Classified National Security...August 15, 2016 — This Directive sets forth guidance to agenc...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 15, 2016</span></span></span>

## The broader obsolescence problem

Obsolescence rarely means that a programme has become unimportant. More commonly, it means the *special* protections no longer provide security benefits proportionate to their costs.

A capability may remain highly classified while no longer requiring a SAP. A technology may still warrant Top Secret protection even if exceptional access restrictions have ceased to add meaningful security. Likewise, portions of a programme may continue to justify compartmentation while others could be downgraded or incorporated into ordinary classified management.

This distinction is important because the decision is not binary. Annual reviews can recommend retaining, narrowing, restructuring or terminating special access rather than treating the compartment as an all-or-nothing choice.

Within the broader system of Information Security Oversight Office accountability, the annual review therefore functions as the principal safeguard against "once special, always special" thinking. Its purpose is to ensure that exceptional secrecy remains tied to current national security needs rather than institutional memory or administrative convenience.
