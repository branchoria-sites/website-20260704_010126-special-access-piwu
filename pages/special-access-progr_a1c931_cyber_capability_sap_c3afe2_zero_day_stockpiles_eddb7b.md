## Introduction

One of the hardest questions in [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) capability programmes is not whether a software vulnerability is valuable, but how long that value is likely to last. A government that discovers or acquires a previously unknown software flaw—a zero-day vulnerability—must decide whether to disclose it so it can be patched or retain it for [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}), military or law-enforcement purposes. That decision becomes especially important for highly compartmented cyber capabilities because the operational advantage may disappear once the vulnerability is independently discovered or publicly exposed.

The most influential empirical evidence on this question comes from RAND Corporation's 2017 study *[Zero Days]({{ 'zero-days/' | relative_url }}), Thousands of Nights*. Rather than relying on hypothetical models, the researchers analysed a large real-world dataset of more than 200 zero-day vulnerabilities and exploits to estimate how long they remained usable, how often other parties independently found the same flaw, and what those findings imply for vulnerability stockpiles. The study remains one of the few publicly available datasets that directly informs debates over whether retaining undisclosed vulnerabilities creates a durable operational advantage or an increasingly risky liability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RR1751.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rand.org">[RAND Corporation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rand.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAND CorporationZero Days, Thousands of Nights: The Life and Times...9 Mar 2017 — This report provides findings from real-world zero-day...</span></span></span>

## What does the RAND dataset actually show?

RAND examined a unique collection of zero-day vulnerabilities that had been developed and maintained for operational purposes over many years. Because these vulnerabilities had known discovery and disclosure timelines, the researchers could estimate their practical lifespan rather than relying solely on expert opinion.

Several findings have become particularly influential:

* The average lifetime of a zero-day vulnerability in the dataset was approximately **6.9 years**.
* Roughly **5.7%** of a stockpile would be expected to be independently discovered and disclosed by someone else within a single year.
* Once the underlying vulnerability had been identified, developing a functioning exploit was generally much faster than discovering the vulnerability itself, with a median development time measured in weeks rather than years.
* The researchers found relatively little evidence that simple technical characteristics reliably predicted whether a vulnerability would survive for a particularly long or short period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR1700/RR1751/RAND_RR1751.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rand.org">[RAND Corporation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rand.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAND CorporationThe Life and Times of Zero-Day Vulnerabilities and Their...by L Ablon · 2017 · Cited by 212 — The results of this resear...</span></span></span>

These findings challenged two common assumptions at opposite ends of the debate.

First, zero-days are not necessarily fleeting assets that disappear within months. Many remain unknown for years.

Second, they are not permanent strategic assets either. Every retained vulnerability carries a continuing possibility that another researcher, security company, criminal group or foreign intelligence service will independently discover the same flaw.

## Why stockpiles create control and timing risks

The RAND evidence changes how vulnerability stockpiles should be understood. A stockpile is not simply a collection of offensive tools. It is a portfolio of assets whose value steadily changes over time.

Each retained vulnerability faces several possible outcomes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Continued exclusivity.** The government remains the only known holder, preserving operational advantage.
* **Independent discovery.** Another party finds the same vulnerability without knowledge of the government's possession.
* **Public disclosure.** The vulnerability becomes known, vendors issue patches and the offensive value rapidly declines.
* **Operational compromise.** Use of the exploit reveals technical indicators that help defenders detect both the vulnerability and associated tools.

</div>

The important point is that the government's own secrecy does not determine the lifetime of the vulnerability. The software itself exists everywhere it is deployed, meaning multiple independent researchers may eventually identify the same defect.

This differs from many traditional classified capabilities. A secret aircraft design or intelligence source normally becomes public only through disclosure or espionage. A software flaw can disappear as an operational advantage simply because someone else discovers it independently.

RAND therefore frames vulnerability retention as a probabilistic rather than deterministic decision. Every additional month of retention increases the chance that exclusivity will end for reasons entirely outside the owner's control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR1700/RR1751/RAND_RR1751.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rand.org">[RAND Corporation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rand.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAND CorporationThe Life and Times of Zero-Day Vulnerabilities and Their...by L Ablon · 2017 · Cited by 212 — The results of this resear...</span></span></span>

## Why long average lifetimes do not automatically justify retention

The frequently quoted 6.9-year average is sometimes misunderstood.

An average does not mean every vulnerability survives for nearly seven years. Some disappear quickly, while others remain undiscovered for much longer. The distribution is uneven, with a relatively small number of very long-lived vulnerabilities increasing the overall average. RAND also cautioned that its dataset represented a specific operational collection rather than every zero-day in existence, so the findings should inform policy rather than serve as universal prediction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR1700/RR1751/RAND_RR1751.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rand.org">[RAND Corporation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rand.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAND CorporationThe Life and Times of Zero-Day Vulnerabilities and Their...by L Ablon · 2017 · Cited by 212 — The results of this resear...</span></span></span>

Equally important, operational usefulness may end before public disclosure.

A vulnerability can remain technically undisclosed while becoming unusable because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* target systems are upgraded;
* software architectures change;
* defensive monitoring improves;
* operational methods become detectable;
* mission priorities shift.

</div>

For cyber capability programmes, the relevant question is therefore not merely "Has the vulnerability been patched?" but "Does it still provide unique operational access?"

## How the evidence informs vulnerability-equities decisions

The RAND findings have become closely associated with debates surrounding the United States' Vulnerabilities Equities Process (VEP), the interagency framework used to decide whether newly discovered vulnerabilities should be disclosed or temporarily retained.

The published VEP charter establishes disclosure as the general default because fixing vulnerabilities strengthens the broader security of public and private systems. However, it also recognises that some vulnerabilities may justify temporary retention when there is an overriding intelligence, military or law-enforcement interest. Those decisions require weighing both offensive and defensive consequences rather than assuming either disclosure or retention is always preferable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[Trump White House Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trump White House Archives Vulnerabilities Equities Policy and Process</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

Empirical evidence such as RAND's contributes to those deliberations by replacing speculation with measurable estimates:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* How likely is independent rediscovery?
* How long might exclusive access realistically last?
* What is the expected operational value over time?
* How quickly does the defensive risk grow if disclosure is delayed?

</div>

Although no dataset can answer those questions perfectly, quantified estimates provide a stronger basis for decision-making than intuition alone.

## What this means for cyber capability special-access oversight

Within highly classified cyber capability programmes, vulnerability stockpiles present an oversight challenge that differs from many other forms of secret technology.

Traditional oversight often focuses on preventing unauthorised disclosure. For retained software vulnerabilities, oversight must also address whether continued secrecy remains justified as conditions evolve.

Questions informed by the RAND evidence include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Is the expected operational benefit still greater than the growing probability of independent discovery?
* Has the affected software become more widely deployed, increasing potential defensive costs if the vulnerability remains unpatched?
* Has the capability already achieved its intended intelligence or operational objective?
* Have changes in technology reduced the exploit's practical value despite continued secrecy?
* Is there a defined process for periodically re-evaluating retained vulnerabilities rather than assuming indefinite retention?

</div>

These questions align with the broader principle reflected in the VEP: retention is not intended to be permanent by default, but should be revisited as technical, operational and strategic circumstances change.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/whitehouse.gov/files/images/External%20-%20Unclassified%20VEP%20Charter%20FINAL.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trumpwhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Trump White House Archives Vulnerabilities Equities Policy and Process</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Trump White House ArchivesVulnerabilities Equities Policy and ProcessNovember 14, 2017 — 15 Nov 2017 — This document describes the Vulner...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 2017</span></span></span>

## The main lesson from the RAND evidence

The RAND study did not conclude that governments should always stockpile zero-days or always disclose them. Instead, it demonstrated that the decision can be informed by measurable evidence rather than assumption.

Its most enduring contribution is showing that undisclosed vulnerabilities often remain useful for substantially longer than many observers once believed, while simultaneously demonstrating that exclusivity steadily erodes through independent discovery and changing operational conditions. For cyber capability programmes operating under enhanced secrecy, this means vulnerability stockpiles should be treated as perishable strategic assets whose value changes over time, requiring continual reassessment rather than one-time classification decisions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rand.org/content/dam/rand/pubs/research_reports/RR1700/RR1751/RAND_RR1751.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rand.org">[RAND Corporation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rand.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAND CorporationThe Life and Times of Zero-Day Vulnerabilities and Their...by L Ablon · 2017 · Cited by 212 — The results of this resear...</span></span></span>
