## Introduction

Special-access programmes (SAPs) are designed to keep exceptionally sensitive activities known only to a tightly controlled group of people. That security model does not eliminate the need for financial [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}). On the contrary, secret programmes often involve expensive research, advanced procurement and long-term [contracts]({{ 'contracts/' | relative_url }}) that require especially careful oversight. The challenge is that auditors can only test whether money has been spent lawfully and efficiently if they are given timely access to the relevant records, contracts and programme personnel.

The historical record shows that this has not always happened. Congressional inquiries and Government Accountability Office (GAO) reviews have repeatedly found that restrictive access arrangements limited the ability of oversight bodies to evaluate costs, schedules and management practices in some highly classified [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) programmes. The result was not simply less public transparency, but weaker internal control over programmes whose costs could reach billions of dollars.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — 21 Dec 1992 — Congress also expressed dissatisfaction with the application of special access controls to a number of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

## What the GAO found in SAP auditing

The strongest evidence comes from congressional concern during the late Cold War and early post-Cold War period, when several expensive defence acquisition programmes operated under special-access controls. In its 1992 classified review, later summarised publicly, the GAO reported that [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) had become dissatisfied with the use of SAP restrictions for several large acquisition programmes because the security controls also limited congressional oversight. The report specifically noted concerns surrounding programmes such as the B-2 bomber and observed that restricted access contributed to broader management and oversight failures highlighted by the Navy's cancelled A-12 aircraft programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — 21 Dec 1992 — Congress also expressed dissatisfaction with the application of special access controls to a number of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

The issue was not that every classified programme performed poorly. Rather, the GAO found that excessive compartmentalisation could interfere with ordinary acquisition oversight mechanisms by making it harder for reviewers to compare programme performance, verify cost estimates and assess whether management controls were functioning as intended. Congress responded by pressing the Department of Defense (DoD) to strengthen central oversight of SAPs while preserving necessary security protections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — 21 Dec 1992 — Congress also expressed dissatisfaction with the application of special access controls to a number of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>

The same tension appears in later GAO testimony concerning [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) oversight. In 2001, the GAO explained that although it possessed broad statutory audit authority, practical access to some intelligence activities depended heavily on agency cooperation. The agency stated that it had discontinued routine CIA auditing decades earlier because it could not obtain sufficient access to perform meaningful work, illustrating that an audit right is less valuable when essential evidence remains unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access...Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access to Information...</span></span></span>

## Why partial access weakens cost control

Financial auditing depends on testing evidence rather than accepting management assurances. Auditors typically need to examine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* contract files and modifications;
* independent cost estimates;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.nationaldefensemagazine.org/articles/2026/6/15/gao-report-finds-disconnect-between-pentagon-and-special-ops-acquisitions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationaldefensemagazine.org">[nationaldefensemagazine.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationaldefensemagazine.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao report finds disconnect between pentagon and special ops acquisitions</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GAO Report Finds Disconnect Between Pentagon and...15 Jun 2026 — GAO found that officials for eight of the selected programs did not mai...</span></span></span>
* programme schedules and milestone decisions;
* invoices and payment records;
* explanations for significant cost growth; and
* discussions with programme managers and contracting officials.

</div>

When auditors receive only summary briefings or selected documents, several risks emerge.

First, cost growth can become harder to detect. A headline programme budget may appear stable while significant obligations move between classified contract lines or supporting activities that the auditor cannot fully examine.

Second, contract management becomes more difficult to evaluate. Without access to supporting documentation, auditors cannot determine whether overruns resulted from legitimate technical challenges, poor requirements management or weak contractor oversight.

Third, comparisons across programmes become limited. One purpose of government auditing is identifying patterns—whether several projects are experiencing similar procurement failures or whether one programme's practices differ significantly from comparable efforts. Heavy compartmentalisation reduces that comparative perspective.

Finally, restricted access weakens independent challenge. Auditors are expected to test management's assertions rather than simply record them. If evidence cannot be independently verified, assurance about programme performance necessarily becomes weaker, even when no misconduct is suspected. These limitations reflect general government auditing principles as well as GAO's documented experiences with classified programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access...Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access to Information...</span></span></span>

## Access restrictions do not always mean no oversight

It is important to distinguish between limited access and absent oversight. SAPs are not intended to operate outside government control.

Current DoD policy establishes governance structures for SAP oversight and explicitly recognises circumstances under which GAO personnel may receive SAP access when appropriate security conditions and approvals are satisfied. Modern policy also places SAP governance within a formal oversight framework rather than treating [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) as isolated organisations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">dod directive 5205.07 special access program policySeptember 11, 2024 — 12 Sept 2024 — Purpose: This issuance: • Establishes policy, a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 11, 2024</span></span></span>

Even so, the existence of an access mechanism does not guarantee that auditors receive sufficiently broad, timely or practical access to evaluate programme costs. The historical evidence suggests that the effectiveness of oversight depends less on whether an audit channel exists on paper than on whether reviewers can obtain the detailed information necessary to perform professional audit work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T/html/GAOREPORTS-GAO-01-975T.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: govinfo.gov">[GovInfo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">govinfo.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access...Central Intelligence Agency: Observations on GAO Access to Information...</span></span></span>

## What meaningful audit access requires

Experience from SAP oversight points to several characteristics of effective financial auditing without undermining security.

**Need-to-know should include oversight responsibilities.** Personnel whose statutory role is auditing or financial review require enough programme access to verify evidence independently rather than relying solely on management summaries.

**Access must be timely.** Audits conducted after major contracts have been awarded or programmes have accumulated years of cost growth are less effective than reviews performed during programme execution.

**Auditors need original supporting evidence.** Budget totals alone rarely reveal whether contracting decisions, schedule changes or technical risks are driving increased costs.

**Security controls should protect information, not prevent verification.** Documents can remain compartmented while still being examined by properly cleared auditors working inside secure facilities.

## The enduring lesson

The recurring lesson from GAO reviews is that secrecy and accountability are not mutually exclusive, but they require deliberate institutional design. Special-access programmes may legitimately restrict who knows operational details, yet financial oversight becomes unreliable if auditors cannot inspect the evidence behind reported costs and contractual decisions.

Historical disputes over programmes such as the [B-2 and A-12]({{ 'b-2-and-a-12/' | relative_url }}) demonstrated that overly restrictive access could contribute to management [blind spots]({{ 'blind-spots/' | relative_url }}). Subsequent reforms sought to preserve compartmented security while ensuring that authorised oversight bodies—including properly cleared auditors—could examine enough information to test whether classified spending remained lawful, accurately reported and effectively controlled.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/assets/nsiad-93-78.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[gao.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Oversight of Special Access Programs Has Increased</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">December 21, 1992 — 21 Dec 1992 — Congress also expressed dissatisfaction with the application of special access controls to a number of...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 21, 1992</span></span></span>
