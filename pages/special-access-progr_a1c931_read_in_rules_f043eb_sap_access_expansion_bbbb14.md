## Introduction

Special Access Program (SAP) [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) are intentionally kept small because every additional person granted access changes the programme's security profile. The obvious risk is that another individual could intentionally or accidentally disclose protected information. Less obvious is the cumulative effect of expansion: each new [read-in]({{ 'read-in/' | relative_url }}) generates additional records, digital accounts, meetings, storage locations, contractor interactions and administrative processes that leave traces of the compartment's existence and activities. For highly sensitive programmes—particularly unacknowledged or waived SAPs—those indirect traces can become almost as revealing as the classified material itself. Executive Order 13526 reflects this principle by requiring agencies to prevent unnecessary access and ensure that the number of people granted access meets genuine mission needs while maintaining security.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[whitehouse.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security InformationDecember 29, 2009 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

## How larger access lists expand exposure

Need-to-know is often described as a rule about who may receive classified information, but it is equally a tool for limiting the size of the population that can create security risk. Every successful programme access request produces administrative consequences that must themselves be managed securely, including access records, indoctrination documentation, training, auditing and eventual debriefing. As the access list grows, so does the amount of security administration required to maintain it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS ESDdod directive 5205.07 special access program policy12 Sept 2024 — Establishes policy, assigns responsibilities, and provides gover...</span></span></span>

Expansion increases exposure in several ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **More potential disclosure points.** Each authorised individual becomes another place where classified knowledge exists, whether in memory, authorised discussions or approved storage.
* **More administrative records.** Personnel databases, access rosters, visit requests and audit logs multiply as participation grows.
* **More organisational connections.** Additional contractors, government offices and support personnel may become aware that a protected activity exists, even if they do not know its full purpose.
* **Greater coordination demands.** Cross-team meetings, scheduling, secure communications and document handling become more frequent, creating more opportunities for mistakes.

</div>

None of these activities necessarily compromise classified information on their own. The concern is that they create an expanding network of observable relationships that becomes increasingly difficult to control over time. This is one reason SAP policy consistently emphasises access based on demonstrated need rather than status or seniority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Student Guide Course: Special Access Program (SAP)...Executive Order 13526, “Classified National Security Information,” defines a SA...</span></span></span>

## Why indirect leaks matter more than direct leaks

Security discussions often focus on the possibility of someone revealing classified technical details. In practice, programmes can also be weakened by the accumulation of seemingly harmless fragments of information.

An individual may never disclose protected specifications yet still reveal useful indicators through routine work. Examples include repeated visits to a secure facility, unexplained travel patterns, specialised procurement activity, unusual calendar entries, contract modifications or the appearance of unfamiliar [codewords]({{ 'codewords/' | relative_url }}) in authorised documentation. Individually these fragments may appear insignificant, but together they can help outside analysts reconstruct aspects of a compartment's size, timing or organisational structure.

This phenomenon is well understood within operational security (OPSEC): sensitive information is not always exposed through a single major disclosure but through the aggregation of multiple small observations. Expanding the number of authorised participants naturally increases the number of such observable activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/SA001-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Student Guide Course: Special Access Program (SAP)...Executive Order 13526, “Classified National Security Information,” defines a SA...</span></span></span>

## Administrative complexity creates its own vulnerabilities

A larger compartment also becomes harder to manage correctly.

Security officers must ensure that every individual remains eligible, receives required training, maintains appropriate documentation and is removed promptly when no longer requiring access. Access reviews, periodic audits and personnel changes all become more demanding as the authorised population increases. Each additional administrative action introduces opportunities for human error, including outdated access lists, incorrect permissions or delayed debriefings.

These risks do not imply that programme managers cannot securely administer large organisations. Rather, they illustrate why SAP governance aims to avoid unnecessary expansion in the first place. Keeping the authorised population limited reduces both the administrative burden and the number of opportunities for mistakes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/520507p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS ESDdod directive 5205.07 special access program policy12 Sept 2024 — Establishes policy, assigns responsibilities, and provides gover...</span></span></span>

## Why unacknowledged programmes face greater risk

The consequences of access expansion are especially significant for [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}).

In an acknowledged SAP, the existence of the programme may already be public even though its capabilities remain classified. In an unacknowledged programme, however, protecting the programme's existence is itself a security objective. A growing access population increases the chance that indirect evidence will reveal that such a programme exists.

Potential indicators include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* growth in specialised contracting activity;
* expansion of secure facilities;
* increasing numbers of personnel requiring unusual access;
* recurring references to compartment-specific identifiers in classified administration;
* observable patterns linking organisations that would not normally interact.

</div>

None of these indicators necessarily reveals operational details. Nevertheless, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) services routinely analyse patterns rather than isolated facts. A compartment that expands substantially becomes more visible simply because more people, systems and organisations interact with it. This is one reason why unacknowledged and especially waived SAPs place exceptional emphasis on restricting access to those whose contribution cannot reasonably be achieved without programme knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/executive-order-classified-national-security-information" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: obamawhitehouse.archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">obamawhitehouse.archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">executive order classified national security information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Order 13526- Classified National Security InformationDecember 29, 2009 — 29 Dec 2009 — This order prescribes a uniform system for classif...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 29, 2009</span></span></span>

## The security logic behind keeping compartments small

The goal of limiting read-ins is not administrative convenience but risk reduction. Each additional authorised person increases the programme's "attack surface" by creating another trusted endpoint where information exists and another set of supporting processes that must function perfectly.

Keeping compartments small offers several security advantages:

* it reduces the number of people capable of revealing sensitive information;
* it limits the amount of administrative data associated with the programme;
* it narrows the number of conversations and operational interactions requiring protection;
* it makes auditing and oversight more manageable;
* it preserves the [uncertainty]({{ 'uncertainty/' | relative_url }}) that compartmentation is designed to create.

This logic explains why SAP access decisions are tied to current mission requirements rather than broad organisational importance. A highly senior official without a direct programme role may present less overall risk by remaining outside the compartment than by being read in solely because of position. Conversely, a specialist with a narrowly defined technical responsibility may legitimately require access because their work cannot be separated from the protected information.
