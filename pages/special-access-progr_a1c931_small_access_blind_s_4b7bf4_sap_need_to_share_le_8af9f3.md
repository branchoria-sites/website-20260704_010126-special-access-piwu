## Introduction

The history of [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) reform after the 11 September 2001 attacks reshaped a long-standing assumption about secrecy: protecting information is not enough if the right people never see the fragments needed to recognise a threat. For special-access programmes (SAPs), including unacknowledged and waived programmes, this lesson does not mean abandoning strict compartmentation. Instead, it highlights the need to distinguish between protecting highly sensitive details and ensuring that essential warnings, risks and oversight information can reach those who genuinely need them. The challenge is not replacing "need to know" with unrestricted disclosure, but designing secure ways to share enough information to prevent dangerous [blind spots]({{ 'blind-spots/' | relative_url }}) while preserving the exceptional security that SAPs exist to provide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bja.ojp.gov/program/it/privacy-civil-liberties/authorities/statutes/1282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bja.ojp.gov">[ojp.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bja.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bureau of Justice AssistanceThe Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004...</span></span></span>

## The need-to-know versus need-to-share tension

For decades, classified systems were organised primarily around the principle of need to know. An individual required not only an appropriate security clearance but also a demonstrated requirement for the specific information. Within SAPs, this principle is applied even more rigorously through programme-specific access approvals.

After the intelligence failures exposed by the 9/11 Commission, however, investigators concluded that many agencies possessed relevant pieces of information but failed to connect them. The problem was often not the complete absence of intelligence but organisational barriers that discouraged or prevented sharing across institutional boundaries. This diagnosis helped drive the concept of a "responsibility to provide" alongside the traditional responsibility to protect information. Rather than assuming information should remain inside a compartment unless requested, agencies were increasingly expected to consider whether another authorised organisation required it to fulfil its mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.belfercenter.org/publication/intelligence-reform" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: belfercenter.org">[belfercenter.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">belfercenter.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belfer CenterIntelligence ReformIn late 2004, Congress passed intelligence reform legislation that led to the most significant reorganiza...</span></span></span>

This shift was reflected in the Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004 (IRTPA), which reorganised the intelligence community, created the Director of National Intelligence and established an Information Sharing Environment intended to improve the movement of terrorism-related information between authorised organisations. Later legislation implementing the 9/11 Commission's recommendations further expanded those information-sharing mechanisms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bja.ojp.gov/program/it/privacy-civil-liberties/authorities/statutes/1282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bja.ojp.gov">[Bureau of Justice Assistance]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bja.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bureau of Justice AssistanceThe Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004...</span></span></span>

For SAPs, however, the lesson is more nuanced than for ordinary classified programmes. The existence of a programme, its technical details or operational methods may still require exceptionally tight protection. The reform question therefore becomes how to share mission-critical knowledge without dismantling the compartment itself.

## Why networked risks defeat rigid compartments

The central lesson of post-9/11 reform is that modern national security problems rarely stay inside one organisational boundary.

Threats involving terrorism, [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) [operations]({{ 'operations/' | relative_url }}), proliferation, space systems or advanced weapons often develop through small indicators distributed across multiple agencies. No programme manager can know in advance which analyst, engineer, lawyer or operator will later need one apparently insignificant detail to recognise a larger pattern.

Rigid [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) therefore create several recurring risks.

* **Fragmented warning.** Multiple organisations may each possess accurate but incomplete information that never becomes actionable because nobody can legally or practically combine it.
* **Analytical isolation.** Small groups can unintentionally reinforce their own assumptions, reducing opportunities for independent challenge.
* **Oversight gaps.** Auditors, inspectors general, legal advisers and senior decision-makers may lack enough visibility to detect emerging problems until they become serious.
* **[Duplication]({{ 'duplication/' | relative_url }}).** Separate highly classified programmes can unknowingly pursue overlapping work because neither side knows enough about the other to identify redundancy.

These problems are not unique to SAPs, but the effects can be amplified because access lists are intentionally much smaller than those for conventional classified programmes. The fewer people who possess contextual knowledge, the more likely it becomes that important relationships remain invisible until much later.

Academic assessments of intelligence reform have also cautioned that "sharing" is not simply about moving larger quantities of information. Effective warning depends on providing enough context for recipients to understand why apparently minor facts matter. Flooding analysts with disconnected data can create new problems instead of solving old ones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02684520701415214" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Taylor &amp; Francis OnlineIntelligence reform: The logic of information sharingby C Jones · 2007 · Cited by 67 — &#x27;Information sharing&#x27; is a...</span></span></span>

## Can special-access programmes share safely?

The practical lesson drawn from post-9/11 reforms is not that every SAP should become broadly accessible. Rather, programmes need mechanisms that permit carefully controlled sharing while preserving exceptional protection for the most sensitive material.

Several approaches have emerged in policy and practice.

### Share risk, not necessarily every detail

Many oversight decisions do not require disclosure of every technical specification.

Programme managers can often communicate that a significant operational, financial, legal or safety issue exists while withholding the sensitive engineering, collection method or operational source that generated it. This allows senior decision-makers to understand the nature of the problem without unnecessarily expanding programme access.

### Build trusted integration points

Instead of opening access widely, governments increasingly rely on specifically authorised organisations whose role is to integrate information across compartments.

Following IRTPA, institutions such as the Office of the Director of National Intelligence and the National Counterterrorism Center were designed partly to improve cross-community integration while operating within established security controls. These organisations demonstrate that information can move through trusted institutional pathways rather than unrestricted circulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bja.ojp.gov/program/it/privacy-civil-liberties/authorities/statutes/1282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bja.ojp.gov">[ojp.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bja.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bureau of Justice AssistanceThe Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004...</span></span></span>

### Preserve oversight access

One of the strongest lessons from intelligence reform is that independent oversight requires meaningful access.

Inspectors general, designated legal advisers, congressional oversight bodies and other authorised reviewers cannot evaluate compliance, effectiveness or legality if they receive only heavily filtered summaries. The objective is not operational participation but informed oversight capable of identifying systemic risks before they escalate.

### Use graduated disclosure

SAPs often rely on layered disclosure rather than an all-or-nothing approach.

An individual may initially receive only the information necessary for a specific oversight, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) or operational task. Additional details are disclosed only if the task genuinely requires them. This preserves compartmentation while reducing unnecessary informational isolation.

## Lessons for waived and unacknowledged SAPs

The challenge becomes especially acute for waived and unacknowledged SAPs because even acknowledging the programme's existence may itself reveal sensitive capabilities or priorities.

These programmes therefore cannot easily rely on broad coordination mechanisms available elsewhere in government. Instead, successful governance depends on identifying a very small number of authorised officials who possess enough cross-programme visibility to recognise strategic risks.

Post-9/11 reforms suggest several enduring principles that remain relevant even under these extraordinary secrecy conditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* secrecy should protect capabilities, not prevent legitimate oversight;
* programme managers should assume that future risks may emerge outside today's access list;
* integration functions require institutional authority rather than informal personal relationships;
* secure sharing pathways should be designed deliberately before a crisis rather than improvised during one.

</div>

These principles do not weaken compartmentation. They aim to prevent compartmentation from becoming so rigid that the security system loses its ability to detect mistakes, integrate warnings or recognise interactions between highly sensitive activities.

## The enduring balance

The most important historical lesson is that "need to share" never replaced "need to know." Instead, intelligence reform sought to balance two legitimate security objectives that can sometimes pull in opposite directions.

Need to know protects exceptionally sensitive information from unnecessary exposure. Need to share recognises that complex national security failures often occur because critical knowledge never reaches authorised people capable of interpreting it.

For special-access programmes, the challenge is therefore not choosing between secrecy and sharing. It is designing compartments that remain secure while still allowing the carefully controlled movement of information needed for warning, oversight and informed decision-making. The experience of post-9/11 reform suggests that the strongest secret programmes are not those that share nothing, but those that know precisely what must remain hidden and what must safely reach the people responsible for protecting the wider national security system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bja.ojp.gov/program/it/privacy-civil-liberties/authorities/statutes/1282" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bja.ojp.gov">[ojp.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bja.ojp.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Bureau of Justice AssistanceThe Intelligence Reform and Terrorism Prevention Act of 2004...</span></span></span>
