## Introduction

Intelligence special-access controls exist because some secrets are not dangerous only in full. A name, a collection date, an unusual phrase in an intelligence report, a technical parameter, or the fact that a particular office is interested in a target can be enough for a hostile service to infer how the United States learned something. Within the wider world of special-access-programs, including waived and unacknowledged programmes, Intelligence Community versions are usually called Controlled Access Programs, or CAPs. They are designed to protect exceptionally sensitive intelligence sources, methods, activities and capabilities when ordinary classification and need-to-know rules are not enough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Director of National Intelligence ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2025 — 17 Oct 2015 — This Intelligence Community Directive (ICD) establishes the oversight and management of Intelligence Commun...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2025</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/special-access-progr_a1c931_intelligence_saps_87a6e0-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Intelligence" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The distinctive issue is source protection. Intelligence CAPs are not mainly about hiding a budget line or a weapons prototype, although they can involve technology. Their central purpose is to stop small clues from exposing human sources, technical collection systems, liaison relationships, [counterintelligence]({{ 'ci-operations/' | relative_url }}) operations or analytic access that would disappear if an adversary knew it existed. That is why these [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}) can be unacknowledged: sometimes confirming the existence of the compartment would itself tell a target that a particular intelligence stream, vulnerability or penetration exists.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Director of National Intelligence ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2025 — 17 Oct 2015 — This Intelligence Community Directive (ICD) establishes the oversight and management of Intelligence Commun...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why intelligence compartments protect clues, not just documents

The legal root of the source-protection mission is blunt. The Director of National Intelligence is required to protect intelligence sources and methods from unauthorised disclosure, and Intelligence Community policy builds access systems around that responsibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/50/3024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: law.cornell.edu">[Legal Information Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">law.cornell.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cornell.edu.</span></span></span> Under Executive Order 13526, a special access programme may be created only when normal classification controls are insufficient and access will be limited to a reasonably small number of people; within the Intelligence Community, ICD 906 implements this idea through CAPs, including both Sensitive Compartmented Information and non-SCI CAPs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/cnsi-eo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cnsi eo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cnsi eo</span></span></span>

The key difference from ordinary classification is that an intelligence compartment often protects the route by which knowledge was obtained. A finished intelligence judgement may say that a foreign unit moved, a missile system was tested, or a senior official issued an instruction. The most sensitive fact may be neither the judgement nor the event; it may be the hidden evidence trail behind it. Was the information from a human source in the room, a communications intercept, a satellite sensor, a [cyber]({{ 'cyber/' | relative_url }}) access, a foreign partner, a recovered document, or a combination of them? If a target can answer that question, it can close the channel.

This is why a heavily compartmented intelligence report may redact details that look harmless to a casual reader. The date of collection may narrow the possible witnesses. A place name may expose which facility is under observation. A technical measurement may reveal the resolution, frequency range or processing method of a collection system. A distribution marking may show which agencies are involved. Even an omission can become meaningful if an adversary knows enough about how US reporting usually looks.

## Sources and methods

“Sources and methods” is often used as a single phrase, but intelligence SAPs and CAPs protect several different things at once.

**Human sources.** The most obvious risk is the exposure of a person who supplied information. The Aldrich [Ames case]({{ 'ames-case/' | relative_url }}) shows the extreme end of that danger: the FBI says Ames compromised the identities of CIA and FBI human sources, some of whom were executed by Soviet authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/history/cases-and-criminals/aldrich-ames" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Aldrich Ames</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Aldrich Ames</span></span></span> A Senate assessment likewise stated that Ames was believed to have caused the death or imprisonment of a number of Soviet sources of the CIA and FBI.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/sites-default-filesations-10390.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[intelligence.senate.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on senate.gov.</span></span></span> In that context, source protection is not an abstract classification preference; it can be a matter of survival.

**Technical collection.** Intelligence compartments also protect systems and techniques: satellite reconnaissance, signals intelligence, measurement and signature intelligence, processing methods, exploitation tools and sensor capabilities. The Intelligence Community markings register, for example, associates TALENT KEYHOLE compartments with areas such as space-based imagery collection, signals, processing and exploitation techniques, and reconnaissance satellite design, [acquisition]({{ 'acquisition/' | relative_url }}) and operation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/jobaids/information/IC-Markings-System-Register.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cdse.edu.</span></span></span> The danger is not only that a device or platform might be named. It is that a target might learn what the system can see, hear, measure or miss.

**Liaison and partner access.** Intelligence often depends on foreign partners. An unauthorised disclosure can damage those relationships by showing that a partner shared information, allowed access, or cooperated in a sensitive field. Intelligence training material on unauthorised disclosures lists effects on international alliances as a recognised category of damage, alongside damage to sources and methods and potential loss of life.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.publicintelligence.net/NCIX-UnauthorizedDisclosures.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.publicintelligence.net">[Public Intelligence &#124;]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.publicintelligence.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on publicintelligence.net.</span></span></span>

**Analytic and operational patterns.** A source may be protected even when no single name appears. Repeated reporting cadence, phrasing, confidence language, geographic focus, or unusually precise knowledge can reveal an access pattern. Intelligence CAPs therefore restrict not just documents, but the population of people who can see enough related fragments to reconstruct the hidden channel.

## Counterintelligence sensitivities

Counterintelligence is the part of intelligence work concerned with detecting, understanding and defeating other intelligence services. It is especially dependent on compartmentation because a counterintelligence operation often works only while the adversary does not know what the defender knows.

A counterintelligence-sensitive CAP might protect a mole hunt, a double-agent operation, a foreign service’s tradecraft mistake, a compromised communications channel, or a method for detecting insider activity. In these cases, disclosure does more than reveal past information. It changes the behaviour of the opposing service. Targets can change communications, isolate suspected sources, feed false information, or set traps for investigators.

That is why damage assessments in the Intelligence Community explicitly look at compromised sources, methods and activities, including equipment, facilities, locations, partnerships, plans, strategies, technologies and programmes. ICD 732 says damage assessments are used by counterintelligence and security communities to evaluate actual or potential harm from unauthorised disclosure or compromise of classified national intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-732.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Director of National Intelligence ICD-732.pdf</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Director of National Intelligence ICD-732.pdf</span></span></span> The point is not merely to decide whether a document was mishandled. It is to understand what the adversary can now infer, what access may have been lost, and what defensive action is needed.

Counterintelligence also explains why some compartments may be unacknowledged. If the government confirms that a particular CAP exists around a target set, technology or penetration, the confirmation may reveal that an intelligence service has a live concern in that space. ICD 906 assigns the CAP Oversight Committee responsibility for oversight and management of CAPs, including unacknowledged CAPs necessary to protect intelligence sources, methods and activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Director of National Intelligence ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2025 — 17 Oct 2015 — This Intelligence Community Directive (ICD) establishes the oversight and management of Intelligence Commun...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2025</span></span></span>

## How partial disclosure creates risk

The logic behind source-protection compartments is sometimes called the [mosaic problem]({{ 'mosaic-problem/' | relative_url }}): separate fragments may look innocuous, but a skilled adversary can combine them with other information to reveal a sensitive picture. The Supreme Court’s decision in CIA v. Sims is a central public example. The case concerned whether the CIA had to disclose information connected to MKULTRA researchers under the Freedom of Information Act. The Court accepted a broad understanding of “intelligence sources” and recognised that apparently small pieces of information can expose protected intelligence relationships.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/471/159/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: supreme.justia.com">[Justia Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">supreme.justia.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on justia.com.</span></span></span>

The mosaic idea is powerful because adversaries are not passive readers. They may already possess travel records, surveillance footage, diplomatic gossip, cyber logs, call metadata, procurement data, public biographies and their own internal records. A disclosure that seems incomplete to the public can become decisive when matched against that private archive.

A simple example shows the mechanism. Suppose a report reveals that the United States knew within hours that a foreign official gave an instruction during a closed meeting. The report may not name a source. But the target service can ask: who attended, who had access to the meeting notes, which phones were present, which networks carried the message, which rooms were used, and which officials later spoke to foreigners? If the report also includes a timestamp, a distinctive phrase, or a dissemination caveat, the suspect list may shrink dramatically.

Partial disclosure can therefore create several kinds of risk:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Identification risk:** a human source, liaison officer, technical access point or insider suspect becomes discoverable.
* **Collection denial:** the adversary changes systems, routes, encryption, meeting habits, travel patterns or physical security to block future collection.
* **Deception risk:** once a target knows the channel, it can feed tailored false information into that channel.
* **Analytic distortion:** analysts may lose a source but still have to interpret later silence, changed behaviour or planted signals.
* **Oversight tension:** the more material is compartmented, the harder it becomes for outsiders to test whether secrecy is justified.

</div>

The last point matters. Mosaic reasoning can be valid, but it can also be overused. Legal scholars have criticised heavy judicial deference to national-security mosaic claims because agencies may be allowed to withhold information without showing the public much about the concrete risk.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://scholarship.law.missouri.edu/facpubs/393/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scholarship.law.missouri.edu">[University of Missouri School of Law]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scholarship.law.missouri.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on missouri.edu.</span></span></span> Intelligence SAPs therefore sit in a permanent tension: the same compartmentation that protects sources can also make [accountability]({{ 'accountability/' | relative_url }}) harder.

## What oversight is meant to do

Intelligence CAPs are not supposed to be private vaults outside government. ICD 906 establishes an oversight and management framework for Intelligence Community CAPs, including a CAP Oversight Committee, a Senior Review Group, annual validation, modification and termination recommendations, and responsibilities for programme managers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/documents/ICD/ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[Director of National Intelligence]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Director of National Intelligence ICD-906-Controlled-Access-Programs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2025 — 17 Oct 2015 — This Intelligence Community Directive (ICD) establishes the oversight and management of Intelligence Commun...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2025</span></span></span> Congressional Research Service material emphasises that CAPs are not classification levels; they are additional access controls for particularly sensitive classified information, and the DNI must validate each CAP at least annually.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IF12080.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: everycrsreport.com">[Every CRS Report]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">everycrsreport.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Every CRS ReportControlled Access Programs of the Intelligence Community20 Apr 2022 — The Controlled Access Programs (CAPs) Controlled Ac...</span></span></span>

Title 50 requires the DNI to brief congressional intelligence and appropriations committees and congressional leadership on each CAP in effect, and it restricts the creation of CAPs and transfers of capabilities out of CAPs without required notifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title50-section3091a" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on house.gov.</span></span></span> Recent legislation and committee materials have continued to focus on whether SAP and CAP oversight, access and information sharing are working properly, including proposed Inspector General review of reform efforts for special access programmes and controlled access programmes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.intelligence.senate.gov/2025/07/17/intelligence-authorization-act-for-fiscal-year-2026-as-reported-on-july-17-2025/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: intelligence.senate.gov">[intelligence.senate.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">intelligence.senate.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">intelligence authorization act for fiscal year 2026 as reported on july</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">intelligence authorization act for fiscal year 2026 as reported on july</span></span></span>

The oversight problem is delicate because source protection and accountability pull in different directions. Too much access can expose the very channels the system was built to protect. Too little access can hide duplication, poor performance, unlawful activity, waste, or unnecessary compartmentation. The practical aim is not maximum secrecy; it is controlled visibility for the right officials, auditors, inspectors, committees and mission users.

## Why this branch of SAP secrecy is different

Many special-access-programmes protect technology, military operations or acquisition plans. Intelligence SAPs and CAPs can do those things too, but their defining feature is inference control. They are built around the fear that a target can learn the hidden collection path from tiny clues.

That makes intelligence source protection both compelling and contestable. It is compelling because real cases show that exposed sources can be imprisoned or killed, collection systems can be defeated, and partners can lose confidence. It is contestable because the same logic can justify withholding material whose public release would be embarrassing, inconvenient or merely difficult to explain. The strongest public framework therefore treats intelligence CAPs as exceptional controls with documented need, limited access, continuing validation and specialised oversight — not as a blanket answer to every sensitive intelligence problem.
