## Introduction

One reason people believe some information is classified "above Top Secret" is the way classified documents are marked. A banner line such as **TOP SECRET//SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED** looks as though it is announcing a higher level of secrecy. In reality, it is combining different kinds of security information into a single label. The first part identifies the document's **classification level**. The remaining parts identify **additional access controls** that restrict who may see it. Those controls are powerful, but they do not create a new classification tier.

Understanding how these markings are constructed helps explain why the "above Top Secret" myth persists. The markings are intentionally prominent because they tell authorised users exactly how information must be handled, yet to outsiders they can resemble a hierarchy of ever-higher classifications.

## What banner lines actually combine

US classified documents use standardised [banner lines]({{ 'banner-lines/' | relative_url }}) at the top and bottom of each page. Rather than displaying only a classification level, the banner line combines several separate pieces of information into one structured marking.

A typical banner line may include:

* **The classification level** (for example, *TOP SECRET*, *SECRET* or *CONFIDENTIAL*).
* **Control markings**, identifying special handling systems or [compartments]({{ 'compartments/' | relative_url }}).
* **Dissemination controls**, such as restrictions on who may receive the information.
* **Programme-specific identifiers**, where applicable.

The Department of Defense marking manual makes this distinction explicit. It states that control markings are **"in addition to and separate from the level of classification"**, and that double slashes (`//`) separate the classification level from these additional controls. The manual treats the classification level and the access controls as different categories of information rather than a single escalating scale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520001m_vol2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAP control markings used in the banner line and at portions denote classified information that requires...Read more...</span></span></span>

For someone unfamiliar with the marking system, however, the entire banner line is often read as one label. A long string such as:

&gt; TOP SECRET//SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED-PROGRAM NAME//NOFORN<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/722743380/Public-CAPCO-Register-and-Manual-v5-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[scribd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CAPC O Classification Manual Overview &#124; PDF(U) SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U) Authorized Banner Line Marking...</span></span></span>

can easily be mistaken for a single super-classification instead of a combination of distinct security instructions.

## Special Access Required is a control marking, not a higher classification

The phrase **SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED** (often abbreviated **SAR**) is one of the strongest contributors to the "above Top Secret" misconception.

DoD guidance specifies that SAP documents carry:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the underlying classification level;
* the caveat **SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED** (or **SAR**);<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/722743380/Public-CAPCO-Register-and-Manual-v5-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[scribd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CAPC O Classification Manual Overview &#124; PDF(U) SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U) Authorized Banner Line Marking...</span></span></span>
* the programme nickname or codeword; and
* any applicable dissemination controls.

</div>

These elements appear together because every authorised reader must immediately recognise both the document's classification and its additional handling requirements. The guidance does **not** describe SAR as another classification level. Instead, it identifies [SAP markings]({{ 'markings/' | relative_url }}) as indicating information requiring **extraordinary protection** through programme-specific controls.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520001m_vol2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAP control markings used in the banner line and at portions denote classified information that requires...Read more...</span></span></span>

The visual effect can nevertheless be striking. A reader who sees "TOP SECRET//SAR-PROGRAM NAME" may naturally assume the second element elevates the first. In policy terms, the opposite is true: **Top Secret remains the classification level**, while SAR tells security personnel that normal Top Secret access is insufficient.

This distinction mirrors the broader structure of the US classification system. Classification answers *how sensitive the information is*. SAP controls answer *which specifically authorised people may receive it*.

## Why codewords and programme labels mislead outsiders

Programme nicknames and [codewords]({{ 'codewords/' | relative_url }}) reinforce the illusion because they appear immediately after the SAR marking.

DoD marking rules require the banner line to include the approved programme nickname or codeword rather than internal administrative identifiers. Training guidance likewise explains that the programme nickname follows the classification level and the SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED caveat in the prescribed order.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodm/520001m_vol2.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SAP control markings used in the banner line and at portions denote classified information that requires...Read more...</span></span></span>

To someone outside the system, a banner such as:

> TOP SECRET//SAR-SAMPLE

or

> SECRET//SAR-PROGRAM NAME

looks unlike ordinary classification markings and therefore appears to signal a secret category beyond familiar government classifications.

The codeword itself contributes to this perception because it usually has no obvious meaning. It functions as an administrative identifier for a specific programme, but its unfamiliar appearance encourages popular interpretations that it denotes an entirely different level of secrecy.

In practice, codewords perform a much narrower function. They identify which authorised programme a cleared individual has been admitted into, allowing security personnel to distinguish between separate compartments even when they share the same underlying classification.

## The order of the markings matters

Another source of confusion is the highly structured syntax used in classified markings.

Official guidance specifies the sequence in which markings appear. The classification level always comes first, followed by authorised control markings in a defined hierarchy. This ordering ensures consistency across agencies and allows security systems to interpret the markings correctly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/shorts/SAS0030-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Programs: Markings Short Student GuideControl markings in the banner lines, including SAP markings, must be in all upp...</span></span></span>

Because the entire banner line is presented as one continuous string, readers often overlook that each section serves a different purpose.

For example:

* **TOP SECRET** identifies the classification.
* **SAR-PROGRAM** identifies a Special Access Program.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.scribd.com/document/722743380/Public-CAPCO-Register-and-Manual-v5-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: scribd.com">[scribd.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">scribd.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CAPC O Classification Manual Overview &#124; PDF(U) SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">U) Authorized Banner Line Marking...</span></span></span>
* **NOFORN** restricts release to foreign nationals.
* Other authorised markings may identify [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) compartments or dissemination controls.

Each element answers a different security question. Together they produce a detailed handling instruction, not a new secrecy grade.

## Why the markings are designed to look prominent

The prominence of SAP markings is intentional.

Banner lines are required on every page precisely because anyone handling the material must recognise immediately that additional security procedures apply. Documents lacking complete banner markings risk being mishandled, copied incorrectly or shared with personnel who possess the necessary clearance level but lack programme authorisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cdse.edu/Portals/124/Documents/student-guides/shorts/SAS0030-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdse.edu">[CDSE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdse.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special Access Programs: Markings Short Student GuideControl markings in the banner lines, including SAP markings, must be in all upp...</span></span></span>

From a security perspective, making the markings conspicuous reduces administrative errors.

From a public perspective, however, the opposite effect can occur. Large, highly visible markings containing unfamiliar abbreviations and codewords naturally suggest an extraordinary form of classification. The formatting successfully communicates "handle this differently," but it also unintentionally reinforces the popular belief that a hidden level exists above Top Secret.

## The practical takeaway

SAP markings create the impression of an "above Top Secret" category because they combine multiple security controls into one highly visible banner line. Official policy treats these components separately:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the classification level measures the potential damage from unauthorised disclosure;
* SAP markings identify programme-specific access restrictions;
* codewords identify the relevant compartment or programme; and
* dissemination controls govern further sharing.

</div>

When read as intended, a banner such as **TOP SECRET//SPECIAL ACCESS REQUIRED-PROGRAM NAME** does not announce a classification higher than Top Secret. It states that the information is classified **at the Top Secret level** and is additionally protected by special access controls that limit access to specifically authorised personnel. That combination of level plus controls is what creates the enduring illusion of something "above Top Secret," even though the underlying classification system contains no such tier.
