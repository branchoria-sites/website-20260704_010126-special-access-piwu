## Introduction

Knowing that an unacknowledged Special Access Program (SAP) exists is not the same as being authorised to say that it exists. That distinction is one of the defining features of an [unacknowledged SAP]({{ 'unacknowledged-7fdfef/' | relative_url }}). Within the United States national security system, many officials, military personnel, contractors and congressional members may possess classified knowledge of a programme, but only a very limited set of authorities may publicly acknowledge its existence. In practice, confirmation is a controlled government act rather than an individual judgement.

This distinction explains why public statements about alleged [unacknowledged SAPs]({{ 'unacknowledged/' | relative_url }}) often receive responses such as "cannot confirm or deny". Such replies are not necessarily evidence that a programme exists, nor are they automatic denials. They usually reflect the rule that the existence of an unacknowledged SAP is itself classified information unless an authorised official has decided otherwise under established security procedures. Executive Order 13526 and Department of Defense (DoD) SAP policies both treat the protection of especially sensitive programmes as an institutional responsibility rather than an individual choice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

## Why clearance alone is not enough

A common misunderstanding is that holding a [Top Secret clearance]({{ 'clearance-gap/' | relative_url }}) allows someone to speak about any classified programme they know exists. It does not.

Access to an unacknowledged SAP normally requires several separate conditions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an appropriate security clearance;
* formal approval into that specific SAP;
* a demonstrated need to know;
* continuing responsibility to protect the information under security agreements.

</div>

Even after meeting all of those conditions, authorised access does not create authority to disclose information. The recipient is trusted to protect the programme, not to determine whether its existence should become public.

This separation between **access authority** and **disclosure authority** is deliberate. Security policy concentrates the power to acknowledge sensitive programmes in designated senior officials so that disclosure decisions reflect broader national security, diplomatic, [intelligence]({{ 'intelligence/' | relative_url }}) and operational considerations rather than the judgement of individual participants.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

## How authorised acknowledgement works

Official acknowledgement is typically an institutional decision rather than a spontaneous public statement.

For DoD SAPs, the authority to establish, modify or terminate SAPs rests with senior leadership under departmental policy. Decisions affecting the status of a programme—including whether it remains unacknowledged or may later become acknowledged—move through formal governance structures involving the SAP Oversight Committee (SAPOC), senior review mechanisms and approval by the Secretary or Deputy Secretary of Defense where required.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

In practical terms, public confirmation generally occurs only when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* a formal declassification decision is made;
* the government intentionally transitions a programme from unacknowledged to acknowledged status;
* legislation or executive action requires disclosure;
* authorised officials release programme information through approved public channels.

</div>

Individual employees, military officers or contractors cannot ordinarily make that decision on their own simply because they possess the relevant knowledge.

## Who is actually allowed to say it exists?

There is no single public rule listing every official authorised to acknowledge every unacknowledged SAP, because authority depends on which department owns the programme and what delegation has been made. However, public law and DoD policy indicate that authority is concentrated among the highest levels of government.

Depending on the programme, this may include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the Secretary of Defense or Deputy Secretary of Defense for DoD SAPs;
* other senior officials specifically delegated classification or SAP authority;
* agency heads responsible for comparable programmes outside the DoD;
* the President, whose constitutional and executive authorities include ultimate classification and declassification powers.

</div>

Even these officials do not necessarily make acknowledgement decisions independently. Public disclosure may involve coordinated review across multiple departments to assess operational, intelligence and diplomatic consequences before any statement is authorised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

## Congress may know without being able to announce it

Congressional oversight illustrates the difference between **being informed** and **being authorised to confirm**.

Federal law requires congressional notification for most DoD SAPs, including unacknowledged programmes. Members of designated defence committees receive classified reporting even when the public is told nothing about the programme's existence. The fact that [Congress]({{ 'congress/' | relative_url }}) has been briefed does not automatically permit Members of Congress to disclose classified information publicly. They remain subject to rules governing classified national security information, although Congress possesses certain constitutional privileges and internal procedures that differ from executive branch practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_access_program" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Special access program</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Special access program</span></span></span>

For waived SAPs—the most restricted subset of unacknowledged programmes—notification is narrowed even further. [Statute]({{ 'statute/' | relative_url }}) allows only the chair and ranking members of the four congressional defence committees, together with limited designated staff under specified arrangements, to receive the required reports. Even then, the programme does not become publicly acknowledged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[whs.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

This arrangement demonstrates that oversight and public acknowledgement are separate concepts. A programme can be fully known to designated oversight bodies while remaining officially unacknowledged.

## Why "no comment" is not a denial

Government officials often respond to questions about alleged unacknowledged SAPs by declining to confirm or deny their existence.

This practice exists because confirming that a programme does **not** exist can sometimes reveal nearly as much as confirming that it does. If officials freely denied every inaccurate claim while refusing comment on true programmes, the pattern itself would disclose classified information.

Consequently, agencies frequently use standard responses that neither affirm nor deny the existence of sensitive activities. Such responses should not be interpreted as evidence that a particular alleged programme exists. They simply preserve the government's ability to protect classified information consistently across both genuine and mistaken claims. This logic also underlies the use of "Glomar"-style responses in some national security information cases under the Freedom of Information Act, where acknowledging the existence or non-existence of records could itself disclose protected information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodi/520511p.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DoD Instruction 5205.11, &quot;Management, Administration...12 Sept 2024 — The Secretary of Defense may designate select unacknowledged S...</span></span></span>

## Why unofficial statements do not amount to official confirmation

Former officials, contractors, journalists or researchers may publicly claim that an unacknowledged SAP exists. Such statements can become important leads for investigators or historians, but they are not equivalent to official acknowledgement.

An individual may:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* possess genuine first-hand knowledge;
* misunderstand compartmented information;
* describe an earlier version of a programme;
* speculate based on indirect evidence.

</div>

Until an authorised government authority formally acknowledges the programme or declassifies relevant information, the government's official position remains unchanged. This distinction is important in legal proceedings, classification policy and congressional oversight because institutional acknowledgement—not personal testimony—changes the programme's public status.

## The governance purpose of limiting confirmation authority

Restricting who may confirm an unacknowledged SAP is intended to ensure that secrecy decisions are coherent across government rather than dependent on individual discretion.

If every cleared participant could acknowledge programmes they knew about, compartmentation would rapidly lose its effectiveness. Adversaries could reconstruct sensitive capabilities simply by collecting isolated confirmations from many different officials.

By reserving acknowledgement authority to designated senior decision-makers, the government attempts to balance two competing objectives: protecting exceptionally sensitive national security activities while preserving formal oversight through authorised executive officials and selected members of Congress. The resulting system can create public frustration because many people inside government may know of a programme while almost no one is permitted to say publicly that it exists. That tension is a defining characteristic of unacknowledged SAP governance rather than an accidental feature of the classification system.
